## Overview

The Abisko Trail Stretch Shorts are Fjällräven's take on a everyday hiking short: a mid-waist, 8-inch inseam cut built from a recycled polyamide/elastane stretch weave rather than the brand's heavier G-1000 canvas. They're aimed at day hikers and backpackers who want more give and less bulk than Fjällräven's rugged G-1000 shorts, while keeping the brand's usual attention to pocket layout and PFAS-free finishing.

## Performance

The fabric here is a plain stretch-woven softshell, not the cotton-blend G-1000 that Fjällräven built its reputation on, and that distinction matters. Fjällräven makes a closely related "Trail Stretch" pant from the same fabric family, and a recent long-term test of that pant is a useful proxy for how the shorts will behave on trail. That reviewer found the fit <cite index="3-1">moves naturally without feeling overly loose or baggy, striking a nice middle ground between structure and flexibility</cite>, which tracks with what I'd expect from an 11% elastane blend — enough stretch for scrambling and high steps without the shorts sagging or riding up.

Breathability is the bigger question mark. The pants version was tested <cite index="3-2,3-3">climbing out of a canyon with a 40-pound pack, which is an activity where heat can build quickly</cite>, and while airflow was adequate, the reviewer was clear that <cite index="3-5,3-6">these aren't hot-weather specialists — compared to lighter options like the Patagonia Quandary, they run warmer and feel more restrictive in airflow</cite>. In short form that's less of an issue since your legs are already more exposed, but don't expect these to feel as airy as a running-style trail short. The upside is that the fabric is <cite index="3-8,3-9">more breathable than heavier softshells like the Stio Pinedale or Arc'teryx Gamma, striking a solid balance for shoulder-season use, though once temps climb into the 70s and beyond you'll likely want something lighter</cite>. I'd extend that same shoulder-season framing to the shorts — they're comfortable in cool mornings and mixed weather but not what I'd reach for on a humid August thru-hike.

Durability seems to be a strength of this fabric family. The related pants held up well: the tester wore them <cite index="3-11,3-12">over a month in Utah's canyon country — on backpacking trips, day hikes, and around camp — scrambling through slot canyons and hauling a 40-pound pack up steep terrain, and they struck an impressive balance for shoulder-season use: comfortable and mobile, durable against rough sandstone, and surprisingly capable in steady rain</cite>. That last point is worth flagging for the shorts too — a tightly woven synthetic fabric like this tends to shrug off light rain and shed a wipe-down far better than a cotton canvas blend would.

On the pocket front, Fjällräven's hiking bottoms consistently get praise for their layout. Reviews of sibling products in the Abisko line highlight <cite index="6-13,6-14,6-15,6-16">a sensible array of pockets — two hand pockets for easy access, plus zippered leg pockets strategically placed and sized to securely hold essentials like a phone, a map, or a trail snack without them bouncing around, with zippers that are robust and easy to operate even with one hand</cite>. The Trail Stretch Shorts carry a more modest 3-pocket count than some of those hybrid designs, so don't expect quite that much cargo capacity — but the same attention to secure, non-bouncy storage appears to carry over.

At 200 g, these aren't a featherweight short — that's roughly on par with a mid-weight softshell pant leg cut down, not a 3-4 oz running short. If you're chasing base weight, this isn't the category Fjällräven is optimizing for; the brand's whole "buy it for life" ethos leans toward durability and everyday versatility over shaving grams. It's also worth noting the fit runs true to Fjällräven's regular sizing, described elsewhere in the lineup as <cite index="6-2">a regular fit that is neither baggy nor tight</cite> — useful context if you're deciding between sizes.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Genuine 4-way stretch (11% elastane) makes for unrestricted movement on scrambly terrain
- PFAS-free construction — no "forever chemicals" DWR
- Recycled polyamide content (53%) without sacrificing feel
- Tightly woven fabric resists light rain and abrasion better than cotton-blend alternatives
- Fjällräven's typical thoughtful pocket placement, even with a modest 3-pocket count

**Cons**

- At 200 g, noticeably heavier than ultralight-focused running-style hiking shorts
- Runs warmer than lighter woven shorts once temps climb into the 70s+
- $125 MSRP is a premium price for a shorts category with cheaper competent alternatives
- 8-inch inseam and mid-waist fit skew more "traditional hiker" than minimalist thru-hiker aesthetic

## Who Should Buy This

These make the most sense for hikers who prioritize durability and all-day comfort over gram-counting — think day hikers, weekend backpackers, and shoulder-season trekkers who want one short that can handle scrambling, light rain, and abrasive brush without babying it. If you're chasing an ultralight base weight or hiking primarily in hot, humid conditions, a thinner running-style short will serve you better.

## Verdict

The Abisko Trail Stretch Shorts deliver on Fjällräven's usual promise of durable, well-thought-out design with genuine stretch and a PFAS-free build, but they're not trying to compete on weight or breathability with the ultralight crowd. For hikers who value longevity and all-around versatility over shaving ounces, they're a solid 7/10 — comfortable, tough, and reasonably priced for what you get, even if they won't turn heads on a spreadsheet of pack weights.

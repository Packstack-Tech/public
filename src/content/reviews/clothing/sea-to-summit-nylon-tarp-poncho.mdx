## Overview

The Nylon Tarp Poncho is Sea to Summit's sturdy, mid-weight take on the poncho-tarp: a 70D coated nylon sheet with a taped three-panel hood that covers a hiker and a full pack in the rain, then pitches as a shelter when the day ends. It's built for three-season hikers in temperate, wet country who want one piece of gear to cover rain jacket, pack cover and tarp duty. The trade-off is weight and bulk. It's far tougher than the brand's Ultra-Sil versions, but it's also well over 100 g heavier than the Nano.

## Performance

**Rain protection.** The fabric is the point here. A 2,000 mm PU coating is a respectable rating for a poncho, which doesn't have to survive pack straps grinding into wet shoulders the way a jacket does. Sea to Summit's pitch is that it will handle a "good torrential downpour", and a poncho's cut helps: with no zippers or pit vents to leak, the main entry points for water are the hood and the open hem. The wide brim keeps rain off the face, and the taped hood seams remove the usual weak spot. The cut is generous enough to accommodate a large backpack underneath, so the pack stays dry without a separate cover, and the back panel hangs longer than the front, which keeps it from riding up over a loaded pack.

**Ventilation.** This is where ponchos beat hardshells, and owners of Sea to Summit's ponchos say so consistently. The fabric isn't breathable, but the open sides let air circulate, and unsnapping a few of the side snaps while walking dumps heat before it turns into condensation. On a warm, drizzly day in the Appalachians or the Pacific Northwest, that means arriving at camp damp from rain rather than soaked from the inside. The manufacturer positions it for temperate climates where a waterproof jacket would be too sweaty, and that's the right reading.

**Wind and fit.** The usual poncho complaints apply. Walkers on Camino forums report a fight getting it on in a gusty wind, and one buyer returned a Sea to Summit poncho because it lacked a front closure for venting; the snap-sided design here handles ventilation from the sides instead. In a crosswind, expect flapping. Off-trail scrambling and brush are also poor matches for a loose 104-inch sheet, though the 70D fabric tolerates snags better than the 15D Ultra-Sil lines.

**As a tarp.** Guy attachment points let the poncho pitch with trekking poles or cord, and owners of the brand's poncho-tarps report solid stitching that survives hard tensioning. Sea to Summit rates it as a two-person shelter, but at 54 x 104 inches the coverage is closer to a one-person tarp with room for a pack. Pitched flat, the hood opening is also the low point where water collects. Users who have set up Sea to Summit's poncho-tarps recommend a sloped pitch to shed rain. It can be paired with the brand's bug tents for an ultralight shelter, but as a primary shelter in sustained rain it's a one-person emergency or fair-weather solution.

**Groundsheet and durability.** The heavier fabric is the reason to choose this version over the Ultra-Sil. A 70D nylon sheet will take a season of being sat on, dragged across gravel and used as a groundsheet, which the featherweight versions won't tolerate. The non-PFC DWR will wear and need reproofing over time, as all DWR does. Sea to Summit backs its products with a lifetime warranty against defects in materials or workmanship.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Tough 70D fabric that tolerates real use as a groundsheet and tarp
- Taped seams and taped three-panel hood with wide brim
- Fits over a large pack, replacing a pack cover
- Side snaps give adjustable ventilation
- Non-PFC DWR and recycled, bluesign-approved fabric
- Lifetime warranty against defects

**Cons**

- At 400–435 g, it's heavy by thru-hiker standards
- Packs to the size of a large water bottle
- Poor in wind, with no front closure and a flappy hem
- Two-person shelter claim is optimistic
- Not breathable; a wet, cold day still needs insulation underneath
- $77.95 is steep for a single-purpose sheet

## Who Should Buy This

Three-season hikers, Camino walkers and weekend backpackers in wet, mild climates who want a rugged rain layer that doubles as a pack cover and a basic emergency shelter. It suits anyone who would rather carry one durable sheet than three fragile ones. Gram-counting thru-hikers should look at Sea to Summit's own Ultra-Sil Nano Tarp Poncho, which weighs 230 g, or a dedicated shaped tarp. Hikers in cold, windy or off-trail terrain will be better served by a proper hardshell.

## Verdict

The Nylon Tarp Poncho is a well-built, honest piece of multi-use rain gear that favors durability over grams. It's a smart buy for temperate-climate hikers who want a rugged poncho-tarp, and a poor one for ultralight purists or anyone expecting a roomy two-person shelter.

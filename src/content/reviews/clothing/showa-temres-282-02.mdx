## Overview

The Showa TEMRES 282-02 began as a commercial-fishing and cold-storage work glove. It has since become a fixture in ice-climbing, ski-touring and cold-rain hiking circles. It is a polyurethane-coated, acrylic-lined shell that costs about as much as a restaurant entrée and keeps water out better than gloves several times its price. The trade-off is warmth and durability: this is a wet-weather glove for people who are moving, not a deep-freeze mitten alternative.

## Performance

**Waterproofing.** This is the glove's reason to exist. The TEMRES coating is a continuous polyurethane layer, not a laminate or a membrane that depends on a DWR finish. Better Trail's reviewers found that almost no other glove competes with it on waterproofing, and one tester called it the most waterproof glove in the quiver when new. Adventure Alan's rain-glove testing for hikers reached a similar conclusion, describing the family as totally waterproof with excellent grip. On a sleeting ridge or in wet snow, that means hands that stay dry when fabric gloves have long since soaked through.

**Warmth.** Insulation is a seamless acrylic liner with thin fleece, which is enough to take the edge off but not to carry a winter. Better Trail rates the warmth as moderate. They found it best for high-output days, or for conditions where wind and moisture matter more than raw cold, and merely adequate near the bottom of its range even while skinning or climbing. The -60°F flexibility figure describes how the coating behaves in the cold, not how warm the hand inside will be. Hikers heading into prolonged sub-freezing temperatures at camp or on a summit will want a warmer mitten in the pack.

**Cuff and fit.** The 282-02 is the "Winter" update to the original blue Showa 282, which had a cult following among ice climbers. The new version adds an extended nylon gauntlet with a drawcord and a side-release buckle, and it comes in black. Adventure Alan's testing credits the cuff with sealing out drafts and holding in warmth, and with a better seal against rain without tucking the glove under a jacket sleeve. The cuff adds roughly half an ounce over the standard 282. The wide opening makes the gloves easy to get on and off with cold hands. The buckle also keeps the pair together on a pack strap or carabiner, though there are no leashes.

**Dexterity and grip.** The rough PU surface grabs wet rope, trekking-pole grips and ice tools well. Dexterity gets mixed marks: GearJunkie's reviewer found it good enough for winter recreation, and Better Trail calls it good for a glove with this much waterproofing. Fine tasks like zippers and stove valves are manageable. Fiddly work such as tying small knots is slower than in a thin softshell glove.

**Breathability and comfort.** Breathability is real but limited. GearJunkie's review says plainly that this is not the most breathable glove on the market, and a polyurethane shell will always feel clammier than fabric once effort picks up. Better Trail describes the comfort as utilitarian at best, and the glove is not cozy. It is a tool.

**Durability.** This is the weak point. Better Trail's reviewers call longevity the glove's biggest weakness. Coated gloves lose their waterproofing as the coating abrades and cracks. At this price, owners tend to treat them as consumables and keep a spare pair.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Waterproofing that rivals or beats gloves costing three to five times as much
- Extended drawcord gauntlet seals out snow and rain without tucking into a jacket
- Rough PU grip holds well on wet gear, rope and tool shafts
- Dexterity is good for something this waterproof
- About $28 at most retailers, with some sellers under $25
- 116 g, light enough to carry as a backup rain glove

**Cons**

- Only moderate warmth, best for active use
- Polyurethane shell gets clammy during high-effort stretches
- Shortest lifespan of any glove in its price tier, since the coating wears
- Utilitarian comfort and no leashes or nose wipe
- Work-glove looks and sizing, with no women's-specific cut

## Who Should Buy This

Wet-weather hikers, ski tourers, ice climbers and winter mountaineers who want dry hands above all else will find the best value in outdoor gloves here. It suits shoulder-season thru-hiking, Pacific Northwest or Scottish-style cold rain, and wet spring snow. It also makes a cheap shell or backup for a layered glove system. Anyone facing long, still, truly cold days should look at a warmer insulated glove or mitten. Hikers who want a glove that lasts for years should look elsewhere too.

## Verdict

The Showa TEMRES 282-02 does one job better than almost anything: it keeps water off your hands, for about the price of a sandwich and a coffee. Accept the moderate warmth and the short lifespan, and it is an easy buy for anyone who hikes or climbs in the wet. Pack a second pair, and a warmer layer for the cold.

## Overview
The Cocoon Ultralight Air-Core Pillow is a hybrid: a removable inflatable bladder wrapped in a layer of soft synthetic fill and a two-sided fabric cover. At 116 g, it is not the lightest pillow a thru-hiker can carry, but it is one of the few under 4.5 oz that feels like a pillow rather than a bag of air. The trade-off is durability, since the bladder is the part that tends to fail.

## Performance

**Comfort.** The synthetic layer is what separates this pillow from a bare inflatable. It takes the edge off the plastic feel and adds a little insulation under the cheek. Backpacker's tester called it "amazingly compact for the cush" and described the microfleece side as cheek-friendly soft. Trailspace reviewers praise the pairing of microfiber and nylon, and one said the pillow keeps its shape well enough to be "perfectly sized for any sleeping pad."

Firmness is adjustable, and owners agree that less air is better. A Trailspace reviewer found about two-thirds full was right and warned that overinflating invites neck pain. A gear blogger inflates fully, lays his head down, then bleeds air through the twist valve until the pillow settles. His method pushes air to the sides and creates a cradle under the head. Backpacker offers the same advice: underinflate slightly.

**Size and fit.** Inflated, the pillow is about 12 by 16 inches. That is generous for the weight, and it lets side sleepers stuff a jacket underneath for extra loft. It is also bigger than most ultralight pillows. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers, who tried the largest model in the Air-Core line, found it very firm and so big that it didn't fit into a mummy hood. They judged it better suited to car camping and travel. The tapered Hood/Camp variant exists for hood-fit; this standard version suits sleepers who use a pad and a loose bag or quilt.

**Inflation and packing.** Owners describe the pillow as quick to inflate: a few breaths fill it, and the twist-and-pull valve lets it empty just as fast. A review at Backpackers.com noted that two full breaths gave a dome-shaped cushion. Deflated, it rolls into a baseball-sized bundle, and some owners flatten it and slide it inside the sleeping bag. The valve cap is sewn and reinforced rather than glued, which one reviewer, who has used his dozens of times, singled out as a point in its favor.

**Warmth and surface.** The microfiber side is soft and warm, sometimes too warm. A Trailspace reviewer sleeping in a mummy bag in the cold Sierra noted the cheek got a bit toasty, though flipping the pillow solved it. Outdoor Gear Lab found the nylon side slippery on a pad. One owner puts the microfiber side down against the sleeping bag so the pillow stays put.

**Durability.** This is the pillow's real vulnerability. Reviews of Cocoon's Air-Core line include reports of slow or complete leaks: a hammock forum poster found his pillow fully deflated after the first night, and a REI reviewer said theirs leaked after two trips. Other owners report years of trouble-free use. Overinflating stresses the seams, so the comfort advice also doubles as a durability tip. Because the bladder is removable, a failure doesn't ruin the cover, but a torn bladder is hard to repair properly.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**
- 116 g for a full-size, 12 x 16 in pillow
- Synthetic fill and fabric cover feel far softer than bare inflatables
- Packs to about the size of a baseball
- Fast to inflate and deflate, with easy firmness adjustment
- Hidden zipper means the cover can be removed and washed
- Two-sided cover lets sleepers pick warm or cool
- Costs about $25, a fraction of premium ultralight pillows

**Cons**
- Heavier than minimalist inflatable pillows
- Mixed durability reports, including leaks within a few nights for some owners
- Large enough that it won't fit a mummy hood
- Nylon side can slide on a sleeping pad
- Microfiber side can run warm

## Who Should Buy This
Backpackers who sleep poorly on a stuff-sack pillow and want a real pillow for about 4 oz will find a lot to like. It suits side sleepers, quilt users and anyone on a long trip willing to trade a couple of ounces for better rest. It is also a sensible budget choice for travel and shoulder-season camping.

Gram-counters who already sleep well on a jacket-stuffed sack should skip it, as should hammock or hood-bag users who want a shaped pillow (the Hood/Camp version suits them better). Anyone who needs inflatable gear to survive a six-month thru-hike without a backup plan should look at heavier, more robust options or carry a patch kit.

## Verdict
The Air-Core Ultralight delivers more comfort per ounce and per dollar than almost any pillow in its class, provided it is treated gently and inflated only partway. Its main weakness is a bladder that some owners found leaked. It is a good buy for comfort-minded lightweight hikers and a poor one for anyone who wants indestructible gear.

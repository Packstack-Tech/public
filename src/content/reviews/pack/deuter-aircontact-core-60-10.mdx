## Overview

The Deuter Aircontact Core 60+10 is a traditional trekking pack in the best and worst senses of the term: a rigid aluminum Y-frame, a trampoline-style ventilated back panel, torso adjustment that takes seconds, and a pocket for nearly everything. At 2,310 g empty, it sits well outside ultralight territory. What it offers in return is stable carrying of heavy loads, which makes it a pack for winter, shoulder-season and expedition-style trips rather than a base-weight-obsessed thru-hike.

## Performance

**Carrying comfort.** Load handling is the pack's reason to exist. The Y-shaped frame is stiff, and Outdoor Gear Lab's testers found it spreads weight effectively, with load-lifter straps keeping much of the burden off the shoulders and the perforated hip belt padding taking the rest. Outdoor Life's testers reported the pack carried heavy shoulder-season loads well and rated it comfortable up to about 45 pounds. The cost of that stiffness is bulk: Outdoor Gear Lab noted the back panel cushioning is plush but adds weight.

**Ventilation.** The Aircontact back system holds a padded, mesh-covered panel off the wearer's back, leaving a wide channel for airflow. Reviewers who have carried it on summer outings, including a Switchback Travel tester on a 45-mile loop in Washington's Glacier Peak Wilderness, singled out breathability as a highlight. The trade-off is familiar to anyone who has worn a trampoline-style pack: the load rides a little farther from the spine than on a close-fitting ultralight frame, and the pack is taller and narrower to compensate.

**Fit and adjustability.** The VariSlide system lets the shoulder harness slide along the back panel across a 17–24 inch back-length range, and testers describe the adjustment as quick and intuitive. The hip fins are articulated to move with the hips, and the hip belt tightens by pulling forward. One caution from testers: the tall, slender profile can overwhelm smaller frames. Outdoor Life's shorter testers reported that the back panel curve affected their posture, and thinner hikers struggled with balance. Deuter makes a narrower SL version for those builds.

**Access and organization.** The J-shaped front zip is the practical upgrade over old top-loaders. Digging for a stove at the bottom of the bag no longer means unpacking from the top down. Owners also like the quantity of storage: pockets on the main body, a roomy lid, and removable hip-belt pockets that hold snacks, a phone, or a 1.5 L bottle. Outdoor Gear Lab called the lid pocket capacity exceptional and said the pack stays balanced under a heavy load. The lid can be removed to save weight or used as a daypack on versions that include that feature, and a rain cover is included.

**Weight and bulk.** Every review of this pack mentions weight, and the pattern is consistent: the padding, frame and feature count add up. At 2,310 g, the Aircontact Core weighs roughly double what many frameless or lightly framed thru-hiking packs do, and it isn't pitched against them. Its closest competitors are heavy-duty carriers like the Osprey Atmos AG and Gregory Baltoro, and it generally undercuts those on price.

**Bear canisters.** Outdoor Life's testers fit a Bear Vault BV500 and a Counter Assault Bear Keg in the pack, vertically only.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Stiff Y-frame and wide hip belt carry heavy loads without shoulder strain
- Excellent back ventilation from the Aircontact Air Spacer Mesh
- Quick, tool-free torso adjustment across 17–24 in
- J-shaped front zip gives access to the whole main compartment
- Generous pocketry, including removable hip-belt pockets, plus an included rain cover
- 10 L collar extension covers occasional bulky loads such as winter gear

**Cons**

- 2,310 g is heavy for a 60 L pack
- Tall, narrow profile can be awkward for shorter or slighter hikers
- Rigid frame and thick padding add bulk with no weight-saving mode
- Bear canisters fit vertically only
- Too much structure and too many pockets for hikers carrying 15 lb or less

## Who Should Buy This

This pack suits backpackers who carry 30–45 pounds: winter campers, families hauling shared gear, photographers, and anyone whose trips start with a heavy food carry. It also suits hikers who want a pack that organizes everything, adjusts without fuss, and stays cool on long climbs. Ultralight and lightweight thru-hikers with a base weight under about 12 lb should look elsewhere. The frame and padding will go largely unused, and a 1,000 g pack would carry their kit just as comfortably. Smaller-framed hikers should try the SL variant rather than this unisex cut.

## Verdict

The Aircontact Core 60+10 earns its weight with stiff, ventilated, easy-to-adjust load carrying and the convenience of front access. It is a comfortable workhorse for heavy trips, and a poor choice for anyone counting grams. Buy it if your loads justify the frame; skip it if they don't.

## Overview

The Petzl Irvis is a 10-point steel crampon built for the part of the mountains where the trail gives way to snow and the terrain stays reasonable: glacier walks, ski-touring approaches, and non-technical alpine routes. It is among the lighter all-steel crampons on the market, and its modular parts can be swapped one at a time. The trade-off is simple. At up to 790 g it carries about 220 g more than Petzl's own aluminum-heeled Irvis Hybrid, and a short 10-point layout means it is not the crampon for sustained steep ice.

## Performance

**Traction and stability.** The Irvis uses two wide, horizontal front points that bite into snow, backed by a second row of teeth angled forward for support when front-pointing. Lateral points handle traverses, and two teeth at the rear help on face-down descents. The points are sized for walking on hard snow without high-stepping. On a glacier approach or a Scottish winter day, that is the right tool. UK tester Kirsty Pallas, who awarded the Irvis a Best Buy and 4.5/5 in The Great Outdoors' 2023 crampon test, found the front points handled the odd steeper section without trouble. She also noted they may feel less sturdy over long mountaineering routes, which is the honest ceiling of a 10-point design.

**Snow balling.** The ANTISNOW plates are a quiet strength. An earlier Great Outdoors reviewer reported no snow accumulation underfoot in any texture, and the 2023 test called the replaceable plates neat and effective. Anyone who has walked a slushy glacier on bare crampons will understand why that matters.

**Durability.** The all-steel construction is the Irvis's main argument over aluminum. WildSnow's tester, who used the crampons for splitboard mountaineering on a glacier icefield, called them a good one-crampon quiver for ski mountaineering and glacier travel. He pointed to the combination of durability and low weight, and noted that steel's one drawback is rust, so a rinse and dry after wet or salty days is worth the effort. Steel also tolerates rock contact, which matters on the walk back to the car with a few hundred meters of dry scree.

**Fit and binding.** Petzl sells the Irvis with two binding systems. Leverlock Universel suits boots with heel welts, such as ski-touring boots and stiffer mountaineering boots. Flexlock is the strap-style setup for boots without welts. The Flexlock front and rear cradles are malleable, so they can be shaped close to most boot profiles, and the narrow tape tensions easily through a double buckle. A Leverlock owner at The Outdoor Ascent said the crampons go on in under a minute, feel secure on rocky ground, and have a buckle that stays put. The same owner reported one failure: a few stones lodged under the metal adjuster and eventually pushed out the pin, so the crampon came apart. It is worth checking the adjustment area after scrambling over rubble.

The front sections are tuned for small to medium footwear, and fine length adjustment is made through offset holes along the central bar. Large boots near the top of the EU 45 range fit but sit at the edge of the design.

**Modularity.** Under Petzl's ALPEN ADAPT system, front sections, linking bars, anti-ball plates and bindings can all be replaced separately. The front sections can also be swapped for Vasak or Sarken units, which turns the Irvis into a 12-point technical crampon. That upgrade path is unusual at this price, with listings around $139 in the US.

**Weight and packing.** The 790 g figure is Petzl's maximum with ANTISNOW plates on the Flexlock version. The Leverlock Universel version is lighter, at 740 g with the FIL toe bail and 770 g with FIL FLEX. The Great Outdoors weighed a pair at 746 g. For a steel crampon with 10 points, that is light. For an ultralight pack, however, it is still a lot of weight for a tool used only on certain days. Skiers and fastpackers who prioritize grams will find the Irvis Hybrid at 570 g in a different weight class. It has a steel toe and aluminum heel joined by a Dyneema cord, and it folds smaller, though aluminum heels wear faster on rock.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- All-steel construction tolerates rock contact better than aluminum-heeled designs
- ANTISNOW plates resist balling and can be replaced
- Modular system: parts are replaceable, and Vasak or Sarken front sections can upgrade it to 12 points
- Two binding options cover welted and unwelted boots
- Tool-free length adjustment with fine increments
- Low price for a steel crampon, with a 3-year guarantee

**Cons**

- Up to 790 g, roughly 220 g heavier than the Irvis Hybrid
- 10 points and compact front sections limit its use on sustained steep ice
- Bulkier in the pack than cord-linked designs
- Steel needs drying to prevent rust
- Fit is tuned for small to medium boots (EU 36–45)

## Who Should Buy This

The Irvis suits glacier travelers, ski mountaineers who expect mixed snow and rock, and hikers moving into mountaineering who want a durable steel crampon that won't be outgrown quickly. It is a good match for hikers on long routes with snow crossings who want one reliable pair. Buyers who count every gram on fast ski missions should look at the Irvis Hybrid. Those planning steep or sustained technical ice should start with a 12-point crampon such as the Vasak.

## Verdict

The Irvis is a durable, easy-to-fit steel crampon that handles snow-covered approaches, glacier walks, and moderate alpine ground without fuss. It carries a weight penalty against hybrid designs and stops short of technical ice, but for general mountaineering it is the more rugged pick, and the modular parts help it last. Buy it for glaciers and moderate snow routes, and choose a lighter or more technical crampon if your objectives lean that way.

## Overview

The Torre is a New Zealand tramping classic: a big, plain, single-compartment sack in cotton-polyester canvas, hung on a rigid aluminium-framed harness. It is built for people who carry heavy loads into rough country and expect the pack to outlast the trip, and probably the owner's next three packs. The trade-off is weight. At 2.94 kg empty, the Torre sits at the opposite end of the spectrum from anything a thru-hiker would call light.

## Performance

**Durability.** This is the Torre's reason for existing. Macpac describes AzTec as its toughest canvas, a tightly woven polyester-cotton blend with a weather-resistant finish, and owners back that up with decades of use. One Trailspace reviewer carried an earlier Torre through New Zealand, Canada and the US, a six-month overland trip from London to Singapore, and ski-mountaineering seasons. The same reviewer described the fabric as close to indestructible and accepted that it weighs more than nylon rivals because it outlasts them many times over. Another owner reported 18 years of heavy use with the pack nowhere near retirement. The base is 840D nylon, so the part that scrapes on rock and gravel isn't canvas.

**Carry comfort.** The current Liberator FreeLoad harness uses full-length perimeter rods and a mix of compression-resistant and softer foams. Macpac says the rods are now fixed, so they transfer load to the hip belt more directly than earlier versions did. Owners of the Torre and its Cascade sibling, which uses the same harness family, describe it as comfortable under heavy loads. One Cascade owner carried 20 kg or more for a two-person trip without trouble. Fit takes work. The shoulder-strap length is set from inside the pack, and the lid stabiliser straps come in two height options. Slick, wicking base layers can let the hip belt slide down onto the shoulders, an issue one Liberator owner worked around by changing shirts. An older Torre owner also noted that the shoulder straps slipped over the course of a day.

**Weather resistance.** Reviewers call the canvas close to waterproof, a claim that mostly reflects the tight weave and finish. This is still a cotton blend, and the pack has a zipped front pocket and a top opening rather than a fully taped, coated shell. Hikers in sustained rain should still line the main bag.

**Organization.** The layout is deliberately spare. There is one big bag with no sleeping-bag zip or divider, plus an under-lid pocket, a zipped front pocket and stretch mesh side pockets for bottles. Owners praise the generous lid pocket and the way the main body swallows gear. The simplicity suits packers who stuff and cinch. It frustrates anyone who wants to reach the bottom of the bag without unloading. The side compression straps can cinch the pack down for a summit day or carry a sleeping mat, tent or skis, and the optional bungee overload system on the front handles wet layers.

**Mountain use.** Dual ice axe or pole attachments and a clean, snag-free profile have long made the Torre popular with climbers. A ProductReview.com.au owner noted that the plain design suits alpinists, though it can bump the back of a helmet when looking up.

**Weight.** Every long-term owner mentions it. At 2.94 kg, the pack alone outweighs the entire sleep system and shelter of many thru-hikers. That figure is the cost of the canvas and the rigid frame. The pack also appears oversized to some buyers: one seller of a barely used Torre said an 80 L pack turned out to be more than a one-night trip needed.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Canvas and 840D nylon construction that owners report lasting for decades
- Rigid aluminium frame and Liberator FreeLoad harness carry heavy loads comfortably
- Adjustable back length and two lid-strap height options
- Simple single-compartment design with a roomy lid pocket and few parts to fail
- Dual ice axe/pole attachments, side compression straps and an optional bungee overload system
- Hydration compatible

**Cons**

- 2,940 g empty, which is several times the weight of a typical ultralight pack
- Single compartment with no bottom access, so reaching the bottom of the bag means unpacking
- Cotton blend canvas is weather resistant but not a waterproof shell
- Harness fit takes time, and slick shirts can cause hip-belt slippage
- 80 L is more volume than most three-season trips need

## Who Should Buy This

The Torre suits trampers, bushwalkers, mountaineers and expedition-style trekkers who carry 20 kg or more through scrub, scree and rain, and who value a pack that will be in service decades from now. It also works for anyone hauling bulky winter or glacier gear on trips of a week or more. Thru-hikers, ultralight backpackers and anyone with a base weight under about 5 kg should look elsewhere, because the pack's capacity, frame and fabric are all more than they need.

## Verdict

The Torre does what Macpac says it does. It carries heavy loads comfortably and holds up to abuse that wears out lighter packs. It is the wrong tool for light and fast travel, and 2.94 kg is a large penalty for anyone counting grams. Buy it for rough country and heavy loads, and skip it for long-distance trails.

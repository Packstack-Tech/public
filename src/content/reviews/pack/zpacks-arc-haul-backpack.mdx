## Overview

The Zpacks Arc Haul is a discontinued ultralight roll-top pack built for thru-hikers who want real volume, real hip transfer and a gap of air between their spine and their gear. It comes in 62 L and 70 L versions, and both ride on the same idea: a curved carbon fiber frame that holds a mesh back panel off the body. The trade-off is durability at the margins. The frame can be fussy to tune and the stays have a reputation for being the weak link. Zpacks has replaced the pack with the Arc Haul Ultra, so most buyers will be shopping the used market.

## Performance

**The Arc Frame.** The pack's signature is Zpacks' patented Flexed Arc Frame: a trampoline-style mesh back panel stretched across an external frame of three horizontal carbon stays and two vertical ones. The hip belt attaches to the bottom stay and the shoulder straps to the top, which keeps the pack stiff and sends most of the load to the hips. Zpacks recommends tensioning the mesh to roughly 2.5" of arch. The gap that creates is the reason owners pick this pack for humid trails, where a flat back panel turns into a sponge by mid-morning.

Setup is not plug-and-play. Getting an even arc on both sides without twisting the back panel takes practice, and owners suggest watching Zpacks' fit video before the first loaded walk.

**Carrying comfort.** Hikers with a good fit describe the Arc Haul as exceptionally comfortable, crediting the adjustable shoulder straps, load lifters and the interchangeable padded belt. The belt's V-style webbing lets the top and bottom edges be cinched independently, which grips the hips more securely than a single-pull belt.

The comfort window is narrower than the pack's volume suggests. Around 30 lb is the sweet spot. Past that, particularly on long food carries, the top bar can press into the back. At loads above roughly 12 kg the bottom of the frame becomes noticeable, and above about 15 kg the belt can twist and push the bottom stay into the lower back. An optional lumbar pad addresses the lower-back contact. Hikers with narrower shoulders report the straps sit wide. Pulling them in with the sternum strap led to shoulder soreness on longer days. Zpacks sells alternative straps, so narrow-framed buyers should sort that out before committing.

**Weather resistance.** The Gridstop fabric is rated to 20,000 mmH₂O, seams and attachment points are taped, and the roll-top closes like a dry bag. Zpacks still recommends dry bags for anything that can't get wet, a sensible habit for sleeping bags.

**Organization.** There's one big sack, two low side pockets angled forward so 1 L or 1.5 L bottles can be reached without removing the pack, and a large stretch-mesh front pocket. There is no internal divider and no hip belt pockets. Belt pouches and shoulder pouches are all paid add-ons, and the weight and cost of those accessories add up fast.

**Bear canisters.** In the 62 L, standard canisters, including the Bearikade Expedition and BearVault 500, stand on end in the upper half of the pack with room left to wrap a foam pad around them or stand a medium stuff sack alongside. The 70 L's larger main body is sized for a BearVault 500 to lie horizontally, which suits the Sierra and other mandatory-canister stretches.

**Durability.** The Gridstop body holds up. Its crisscrossed nylon and HDPE threads resist tearing, and one early pack bought in 2017 was reported to look and work like new after years of regular use. The carbon stays are less forgiving. Owners call the pack light and well laid out but slightly fragile, and at least one hiker had a rod snap mid-trip. Quality control on stays has been uneven, with some arriving with rough cut ends or machined notches. Zpacks has generally replaced defective parts at no charge, but a mid-trail break is a problem a mailed replacement can't fix in time. Thru-hikers carry spares.

## Choosing a Version

The two versions share fabric, frame concept, pocket layout and load rating. What changes is the size of the main body.

- **62 L (682 g):** A 49 L main body plus 10.5 L of external pockets. This suits hikers carrying a sub-12 lb base weight on 5–6 day food carries, and it stands a bear canister upright with room to spare. It is 64 g heavier than the 70 L on paper.
- **70 L (618 g):** The main body grows to 57 L. It is the one for bulky quilts and puffy jackets, for 7–10 day carries and for horizontal canister loading. It is also the lighter of the two by 64 g. Buyers should keep in mind that more volume invites more weight, and the frame's comfort ceiling stays near 30 lb either way.

Hikers who regularly fill the 62 L and want canister flexibility should take the 70 L. Everyone else is better served by the 62 L, which compresses cleanly with the roll-top.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Arc Frame ventilation gives a real edge in heat and humidity
- Solid hip transfer for a pack in this weight class, comfortable to about 30 lb
- Adjustable torso height and an interchangeable belt cover a wide range of builds
- Gridstop fabric is tear resistant and sealed against rain
- Two volumes, with the 70 L lighter than the 62 L
- Handles full-size bear canisters in both versions

**Cons**

- Discontinued: used market only, or step up to the Arc Haul Ultra
- Carbon stays can crack, and quality control on them has been inconsistent
- Straps run wide for narrow shoulders
- No internal organization, and hip belt pockets cost extra
- Frame tensioning takes time to learn
- Heavy loads push the top bar and bottom stay into the back

## Who Should Buy This

The Arc Haul suits ultralight-leaning thru-hikers with a base weight near or below 12 lb who need volume for long carries in warm, sweaty climates. It rewards people who will spend an evening tuning the arch and who carry a spare set of stays. It is the wrong pack for anyone who regularly hauls 35 lb or more, for narrow-shouldered hikers unwilling to swap straps, and for buyers who want a warranty and new stock. Those buyers should look at the Arc Haul Ultra, which uses a tougher fabric and a Lycra back panel.

## Verdict

The Arc Haul earned its thru-hiker following: light, better ventilated than most framed packs, and adjustable in ways ultralight packs rarely are. The fragile stays and the wide straps are real, but hikers who stay under 30 lb and pack a spare stay can live with both. At the right used price it remains a strong choice, though the Ultra fixes several of its rough edges. 8/10.

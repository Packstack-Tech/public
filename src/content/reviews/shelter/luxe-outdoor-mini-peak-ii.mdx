## Overview

The Mini Peak II is a square-ish pyramid from Luxe Outdoor that stands on one trekking pole and gives a solo hiker far more room than most one-person shelters. It has two doors, two apex vents and an optional drop-in inner. Pitching is easy and the price is low. The costs are polyester that is a notch below premium cuben or Dyneema fabrics, and an inner that is heavy and awkward.

## Performance

**Space.** Room is the reason to buy this tent. The 250 x 210 cm footprint is nearly square, with standing-height-ish volume under the apex. Chris Townsend, testing single-pole shelters for The Great Outdoors, praised the headroom in the centre and found space for two people plus gear and cooking. Owners on bikepacking and adventure-motorcycle forums say the same: a 6-footer can sit upright under the peak, and the pack size is small for the volume. One owner calls it "a palace" for a single occupant.

**Pitching.** Setup is a single pole, a handful of stakes and some tensioning. Townsend found it easy to pitch with a trekking pole long enough to reach the apex (he cites 145 cm). Hikers with shorter poles can use two poles with an extender or buy Luxe's optional pole, which he described as really solid. Retailer listings suggest about 137–140 cm, so a standard adjustable pole on its longer setting should work. A Rokslide owner who struggled to get a "bell" shape from another brand's pyramid found the Luxe's pyramid geometry pitched cleanly, with no loss of interior volume. The included stakes are short, and Townsend would swap in longer ones for the corners.

**Weather.** The 1,500 mm PU-coated polyester flysheet is a mid-range spec. Owners nonetheless report it holding up. One user rode out a heavy wind and rain without leaks, and another used it on a three-week trip and called it dependable. Polyester also dries quickly and does not sag the way silnylon does when it gets wet, which means fewer midnight guyline adjustments. A pyramid shape sheds wind well, though this is a three-season shelter. Owners advise against relying on it for serious winter use.

**Ventilation.** Two doors and two adjustable apex vents give good airflow. One owner likes opening both sides so the prevailing breeze passes through. The two doors also let you open the shelter fully for views, then close up with the vents as a condensation bleed.

**The inner.** This is the weak point. The drop-in mesh inner covers about half the floor and leaves the rest for gear and cooking. It clips to the corners and apex, and it can be lowered for more room. Townsend weighed it at 438 g, which he called heavy. It has a door on only part of one side, so one of the outer doors is blocked once the inner is up, and the inner cannot be opened fully to match the fly. Early owners found the attachment points hard to work out. The mesh is described as mosquito-proof but not midge-proof. Townsend's suggestion is to use a lighter groundsheet most of the time and bring the inner only in bug season. Those hoping to cut weight further can also leave the inner at home entirely. Luxe sells a separate two-person inner (Rect Inner 230) that converts the tent for two.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Very large footprint for a solo shelter, with strong headroom at the centre
- Single-pole pitch uses trekking poles you already carry
- Two doors and two apex vents give flexible ventilation
- Small packed size, roughly 12 x 12 x 42 cm
- Low price compared with similar pyramid tents, according to owners and testers
- Seam-taped flysheet; owners report dependable rain performance

**Cons**

- 1,067 g for the flysheet alone is not ultralight by cuben or Dyneema standards
- The inner adds weight (438 g in Townsend's test) and has an awkward, partial door
- Short stock stakes
- 1,500 mm polyester is less refined than premium shelter fabrics
- Entry and exit can be awkward with the inner pitched
- Some owners over about 5'9" (180 cm) recommend moving up to the XL

## Who Should Buy This

This is for the budget-conscious solo hiker, bikepacker or canoe camper who values room and ease of setup over the last few hundred grams. It suits anyone who already carries trekking poles, wants space to cook and store gear out of the rain, and does not mind that the fabrics are not top tier. Taller hikers, and anyone who wants to share the shelter, should look at the larger Mini Peak XL or add the two-person inner. Gram-counters who want a shelter well under a kilogram, or anyone who expects regular winter use, should look at a lighter or stronger option.

## Verdict

The Mini Peak II delivers a large, stable, easily pitched pyramid at a price that few competitors match, and owners report it holds up in wind and rain. The inner and the fabric quality are the compromises, along with a weight that falls short of true ultralight. For solo three-season use on a budget, it is a good buy.

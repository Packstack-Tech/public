## Overview

The K4 3600 is Exo Mountain Gear's mid-size backcountry hunting pack, built around a carbon fiber external frame that's designed to carry heavy, awkward loads — think meat, antlers, and multi-day gear — without turning your spine into a question mark. It's aimed at backpack hunters and heavy-load backcountry travelers who need a system, not just a bag: frame, main pack, and lid are sold together and can be mixed, matched, and swapped depending on the trip. This isn't a pack for counting grams on a thru-hike; it's a pack for people who need to move serious weight through rough terrain and go home comfortable.

## Performance

The headline feature of the K4 is the frame, and it's the part reviewers keep coming back to. One long-term Rokslide reviewer who'd already run the previous K2 and K3 frames <cite index="1-16,1-17">said the K4 was even more comfortable, noting it feels like it locks onto his hips and doesn't move, even with heavy loads.</cite> That's not a small claim from someone with that much saddle time in the brand's earlier frames. Carryology's reviewer echoed the same theme after three months of testing across mixed terrain and weather, describing the K4 as best suited to <cite index="8-10,8-11,8-12">a solo hunting trip to Alaska or extended bushcrafting with a heavy pack full of gear — it's a heavy-load carry tool</cite> at its core.

That comfort under load isn't just marketing copy, either. Forum reports back it up anecdotally — one Rokslide poster hauling a set of sheds said the weight <cite index="12-20,12-21">was at least 310 plus pounds and he loved how light and stable the pack felt on his back</cite> (obviously frame-plus-load, not the pack's own weight, but still telling). Another hunter who switched over from a Kifaru summed it up bluntly: <cite index="11-10,11-11">there was nothing the Kifaru did better for him than the K4, and there were several things the K4 did better, calling it a category by itself after hauling several animals and hunting with it a lot</cite>.

The bag-to-frame relationship is the other big functional piece. The main bag detaches from the frame so you can load meat directly onto the frame's load shelf while your camp gear rides separately in the bag — <cite index="6-9,6-10,6-11,6-12">the bag detaches from the frame so the hunter can load meat directly onto the frame itself while having camp gear in the actual bag, a tailor-made system for backpack hunters</cite>. On the 3600 specifically, access is via <cite index="1-23">a single zipper along the side of the back panel</cite> plus a roll-top, which is a simpler layout than the horseshoe-zip 5000 bag but works fine for a pack this size.

Where the 3600 gets a little more nuanced is bag shape and capacity ceiling. One Rokslide user found the bottom of the main compartment narrower than expected, noting it was hard to fit a bulky sleeping bag (a Western Mountaineering Kodiak in its stuff sack) <cite index="11-7,11-8">without it taking up damn near the whole main bag, unlike a competitor's pack where it slid neatly into the bottom with room left over</cite>. If you're running bulky, low-fill-power bags or non-compressible gear, that's worth factoring in — you may want to size up to the 7200 or plan your packing order carefully.

On weather resistance, the K4 line (reviewers tested the 3600 specifically) held up well in general use but isn't bombproof in a true deluge. Carryology's tester was direct about it: <cite index="2-6,2-7">it's a seriously weatherproof pack and the main fabric is waterproof, so it'll be fine in light rain or snow</cite>, but <cite index="2-8,2-9">some water can leak through the stitching lines in a long-lasting downpour, so if that's a concern you'll want an aftermarket rain cover or a dedicated waterproof drybag insert</cite> — which Exo sells for most bag sizes.

Sizing is the other thing to plan for before you buy. This isn't a one-size-fits-most pack — you're picking a frame height (22" to 26.5") and one of five hip belt sizes based on actual body measurements, not just "S/M/L" guesswork, as one reviewer noted when comparing it to Exo's earlier, simpler sizing system. That's a strength for dial-in fit, but it does mean more homework (and a tape measure) before you order.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Exceptional heavy-load comfort — multiple long-term users switching from brands like Kifaru report the K4 frame carries big weight better
- Carbon fiber frame keeps total system weight to 5 lb 8 oz despite a 150+ lb load rating
- Modular system: bag detaches for meat-on-the-frame hauling, swappable across bag sizes
- Made in the USA with a reputation for excellent build quality and customer service
- Precise fit customization via frame height and hip belt sizing

**Cons**

- Not cheap — the base 3600 system runs in the $650-800 range depending on options
- Sizing requires real measurements, not just picking S/M/L off a chart
- Main bag's narrower bottom shape can make bulky items (large sleeping bags, bear cans) awkward to pack
- Seam leakage possible in sustained heavy rain without an added rain cover or liner
- Overkill and not weight-optimized for anyone not routinely carrying heavy loads

## Who Should Buy This

The K4 3600 is built for backpack hunters and heavy-load backcountry travelers — people regularly carrying 40-100+ lbs of gear, meat, or antlers over multiple days in rough terrain. If your trips top out at a comfortable 25 lbs and you're optimizing for base weight, this pack is the wrong tool; look at a standard internal-frame ultralight pack instead. But if you've ever cursed a pack that folded under a heavy quarter of elk or a loaded spotting scope kit, this is exactly the category the K4 was built to dominate.

## Verdict

The K4 3600 delivers on its core promise: it carries heavy, awkward loads more comfortably than most packs in its class, backed by a genuinely impressive carbon frame and a made-in-USA build that reviewers and hunters keep coming back to. It's expensive and demands some sizing homework, and the main bag's shape isn't ideal for bulky, low-compression gear — but for its intended job of hauling serious weight through the backcountry, it's hard to beat. I'd rate it an 8.5/10 for the heavy-load hunting crowd it's designed for, and a poor fit for anyone chasing ultralight base weights.

## Overview

The Torrid Mitts are Enlightened Equipment's minimalist insulated mittens: a thin shell, a layer of Climashield Apex, a gauntlet cuff and not much else. They're aimed at thru-hikers and shoulder-season backpackers who want a warm hand layer for cold mornings and camp chores, and who'd rather not carry a pound of winter gloves. The trade-off is the usual ultralight one: very little warmth per gram, and very little armor in return.

One correction to the catalog blurb: despite the "down" billing, the Torrid Mitts are filled with synthetic Climashield Apex, which is the reason many hikers choose them.

## Performance

**Warmth.** Owners describe the Torrid Mitts as warm for their weight. One reviewer who used them for shoulder-season bikepacking and backpacking reported toasty hands down to the mid-20s °F, and called them an ideal fit for the first hours of a cold start and the first hours in camp. That's their sweet spot: a mitt that goes on when the hands are idle or lightly working, then stuffs into a pocket when the sun comes up. They are not an expedition mitt, and they don't pretend to be.

**Wet-weather behavior.** Apex is a polyester continuous-filament batting that keeps insulating when damp and dries quickly, which is the case for synthetic over down in hand insulation, where wet is the norm. Reviewers who compare winter handwear note that thru-hikers rarely get the chance to dry gear out, so insulation that tolerates moisture matters more than a few extra grams. The shell is water repellent rather than waterproof. In sustained rain or wet snow, the mitts do best under a shell mitt or over a thin liner, a pairing the gear-testing site Adventure Alan recommends for harsher conditions.

**Dexterity and grip.** Mittens are never nimble, but the Torrid's thin build is workable. A Spanish ultralight retailer notes that despite the Apex thickness, the mitts remain usable for holding trekking poles and driving stakes. The Flip Top version adds a fingertip cover that pulls back and secures with shock cord, freeing the fingers for zippers, stoves and phone screens without removing the mitt. Anyone who expects to do much fiddly work should pick that version.

**Fit and weather sealing.** The gauntlet cuff is long enough to cover the wrist, and the shock cord and cordlock cinch it down to keep wind and snow out. The cord is thin, so it's a light closure rather than a robust one. Sizing runs true by hand length; a reviewer who wears a medium in most gloves found the medium right.

**Durability.** The Torrid line trades fabric weight for low mass, and these mitts carry no reinforced palm overlays, D-rings or gripper patches. Even so, the reviewer who used them with handlebar grips and trekking poles over many days saw no signs of wear. Owners should still expect the care that goes with thin nylon: keep them off rough rock and abrasive pack straps. Apex also tends to lose loft if stored compressed for months, so a loose stuff in the lid pocket beats a tight stuff sack over the winter. Washing synthetic mitts at home is simple.

## Choosing a Version

The two versions differ only in size. The Standard version, at 75 g, covers the small-through-large range, while the XLarge at 78 g is cut for hand lengths of 8.25 to 9 in (21–23 cm). The 3 g penalty is negligible, so the decision is just fit. A mitt that is too tight compresses the Apex and loses loft; one that is too loose lets heat slip out the cuff. Measure from wrist crease to fingertip, and size by the maker's chart: small is 6–6.75 in, medium 6.75–7.5 in, large 7.5–8.25 in. Hikers who wear a larger insulating liner underneath should consider going up a size.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Weighs 75–78 g, light enough to carry as a just-in-case item
- Synthetic Apex keeps insulating when damp and dries fast
- No sewn-through seams on the insulated body, so fewer cold spots
- Gauntlet cuff with cordlock seals out wind and snow
- Packs small; easy to wash
- Optional Flip Top for finger access
- Priced at $70

**Cons**

- Water repellent, not waterproof; needs a shell in sustained wet
- No reinforced palm or grip features
- Mitten dexterity limits camp chores without the Flip Top
- Not warm enough for deep winter on its own
- Thin cuff cord is not built for abuse

## Who Should Buy This

Three-season thru-hikers, shoulder-season backpackers and bikepackers who want a warm layer for cold mornings and evenings, and who care about every gram, are the target. It also suits anyone building a winter system: the Torrid slots in under a shell mitt as the insulation layer. Hikers headed into sustained sub-zero temperatures, or those who need a mitt that handles rope, ice tools or heavy wet work on its own, should look at a heavier, fully waterproof system instead.

## Verdict

The Torrid Mitts do one job well: they deliver a lot of damp-tolerant hand warmth at a weight that barely registers on a gear list. They aren't rugged or fully weatherproof, and they don't need to be. For ultralight hikers in cold-morning, shoulder-season conditions, they're an easy yes, preferably in the Flip Top version if there's any camp work to do.

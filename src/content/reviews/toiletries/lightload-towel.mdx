## Overview

The Lightload Towel is a Lyocell cloth vacuum-compressed into a disc roughly the size of a pair of dice, which swells into a usable towel once it meets water. It weighs 6 to 14 grams depending on size, so it weighs less than most bandanas. The trade-off is that it's a semi-disposable wipe-and-dry cloth, not a towel you'll dry your whole body with after a lake swim.

## Performance

**Packing and opening.** The compression is the point. Out of the wrapper, the towel looks like a tight puck of tissue. Lightload says the 12x12 vacuum-seals to about the size of dice, and the larger 12x24 to roughly a silver dollar. A splash of water or a swipe across a sweaty forehead softens it and takes out the wrinkles. In cold conditions it can be opened dry: squeeze the sides of the puck out of the package and unravel it slowly. Reviewers who tried it dry describe a papery feel at first. One early tester also noted that the fabric is tougher than it looks and hard to tear by hand.

**Absorbency and drying.** Lyocell absorbs more moisture than cotton, and the manufacturer cites 9–10 times the towel's weight in water. Testers at Active Gear Review found the hand-towel size dried dishes far longer than their regular dish towel before it needed wringing. The method for a bigger job is to dry off, wring it out and keep going. A backpacker writing on Go Backpacking said the towel took in a lot of water, wrung out well and reabsorbed more without trouble. That is the workflow for a 12x24: a quick rinse-and-dry of face, neck and arms, not a full-body towel-off. Lyocell also dries quickly, and Litesmith, a retailer that sells it, says it wicks moisture and dries faster than a bandana. For comparison, a standard microfiber travel towel from Cocoon claims about 4x its weight in absorbency but weighs several ounces in the medium size.

**Durability and care.** Owners generally call it surprisingly durable for something this thin. Users on Litesmith's product page report using it to wipe hands and dog paws, mop condensation off a tent wall and wash dishes. Washing is the weak spot. Litesmith recommends hand washing to limit pilling and fraying, and a Hiking Lady reviewer found her washing machine too rough for it, though Active Gear Review's testers say theirs survived several trips through the washer. A 2009 blog test found a machine wash and dry left the cloth feathery soft with only minimal fraying, and slightly larger than before. The realistic expectation is a towel that lasts a season or a few trips with care and is replaced cheaply, not one that lasts for years. The retail price of about $6 for a two-pack of 12x12s makes that acceptable.

**Other uses.** The brand pushes the towel as a survival tool, and a few of those claims hold up. It works as a handkerchief, a pre-filter for silty water and a neck wrap. The cellulose also burns readily. A Go Backpacking tester lit a dry one with a single match and it burned to almost nothing, which is useful as tinder, but also a reason to keep it clear of the stove.

## Choosing a Version

- **12" x 12" (6 g):** The lightest option and the one most hikers carry. It handles face, hands and dishes, and fits in a pocket of the first-aid kit. Retailers sell it in multipacks, so a thru-hiker can carry one and mail the rest ahead.
- **30 x 30 cm (9 g):** About the same footprint as the 12x12, listed at 9 g. European retailers carry this metric version in three-packs. Choose it if that's the version your local shop stocks. It's a neutral change, not a different tool.
- **12" x 24" (14 g):** Double the area. This is the size reviewers call out for backpacking, because it's long enough to wrap around a neck or cover a head and is large enough to dry arms and legs. It's the pick for anyone who wants a rinse-off after a sweaty day, at a cost of about 8 g over the smallest square.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 6–14 g, lighter than a bandana and most pack towels.
- Compresses to a dice-sized puck and opens with a few drops of water.
- Absorbs up to 9–10x its weight and wrings out for repeated use.
- Cheap enough to carry spares or replace mid-trip.
- Burnable and biodegradable at end of life.
- Multiple uses: washcloth, tent-wall wipe, dish towel, neck wrap, tinder.

**Cons**

- Too small to dry off your entire body.
- Gentle washing needed; fraying and pilling show up with machine washing.
- Fabric feels papery until wet or washed.
- Not built to last for years.
- Burns easily near a flame.

## Who Should Buy This

Section hikers, thru-hikers and gram-counters who want a wash cloth for face, hands, dishes and tent condensation, and who already keep a bandana for other jobs. Cheap multipacks also make it a smart addition to first-aid or bear-canister kits. Anyone who wants a towel for post-swim drying, or a cloth that can be machine-washed hard for years, should look at a conventional microfiber pack towel instead.

## Verdict

The Lightload Towel is an inexpensive and very light way to carry something to wipe with. It's best treated as a consumable. For hikers who want a 14 g cloth for a rinse-down or a 6 g cloth for hands and dishes, it's easy to recommend. Anyone wanting a full-size towel should look elsewhere.

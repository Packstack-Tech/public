## Overview

The Soto Micro Lifter is a pot gripper pared down to almost nothing: 13 grams of aluminum and stainless steel, about 3.5 inches long, small enough to ride inside a mug or a modest titanium pot. It's built for solo backpackers who boil water in a handle-less or short-handled pot and want a way to lift it off the flame without singed fingertips. The trade-off is plain from the dimensions. Something this small is easy to pack and not especially generous to hold.

## Performance

**Weight and packed size.** This is the Micro Lifter's whole argument. At 13 g, it weighs about as much as a few sheets of paper, and its 88 mm length means it tucks into the cavity of many small pots. Retailers that stock it single out the pack size over the grip. One UK ultralight shop calls it one of the lightest pot lifters it carries, and says it is surprisingly strong for something so small. Owners in Japan, where Soto is a household name, make the same point: it is light, compact and stacks neatly with other cookware.

**Heat management.** A clip-on lifter has one structural advantage over a pot's built-in handle. The metal grips the pot, but the part a hand holds stays away from the heat. Litesmith notes that, unlike pot handles, the lifter stays cool. That matters most with a bare-wall mug or a pot whose own handles heat up in a flame, and it is the reason a lifter can replace a handle entirely on a stripped-down kit.

**Grip and handling.** The trade-off shows up in the hand. One Japanese buyer's review, one of roughly 200 ratings on Amazon (averaging 4.0 of 5), praises the size and stackability but says the compactness comes at the expense of an easy hold. That fits the geometry: a 22 mm-square body gives the fingers little to wrap around. Expect it to suit a 750 ml pot of boiling water. A full 1.1-liter pot of noodles will feel less secure than it would with a larger gripper or a pot-mounted handle. Testers who have lifted heavier pans with it describe it as stronger than it looks, but the comfort ceiling is lower than that of bigger tongs.

**Compatibility.** Soto designed it around the Thermostack and Thermolite lineup, and its listings extend to the company's titanium pots. Soto also says it works with aluminum camping cookware and even grill grates, while retailers describe it as workable with a wide range of pans. In practice, the lifter needs a pot with a decent rim or lip to bite on. Straight-sided pots and mugs are the natural match.

**Durability.** The aluminum-and-steel construction has drawn little criticism. Reviewers on European retail pages, a small sample of customers who rate it 5 of 5, mention its lightness, compactness and value, and none flag breakage or loosening.

**Price.** At $16 to $20 depending on the retailer, it costs more per gram than almost anything else in a cook kit. It is a niche purchase: the buyer is paying for the weight and bulk it saves.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 13 g, among the lightest pot grippers on the market
- Fits inside many small pots and mugs
- Handle stays cool while the pot is on the flame
- Aluminum and stainless construction with no reported durability complaints
- Fits Soto's Thermostack and Thermolite pots and works with a range of other cookware

**Cons**

- Small body offers a limited hold, especially with gloves or a full pot
- Best with small pots; less reassuring with heavy or large-diameter cookware
- Pricey for what it is, at about $16 or more
- Needs a rim or lip to grip

## Who Should Buy This

The ideal buyer is a solo backpacker or thru-hiker cooking in a 750 ml-class pot or a mug who wants a handle that weighs next to nothing and disappears into the cook kit. It also suits owners of Soto's Thermostack or Thermolite pots, which the lifter was designed to fit. Anyone who cooks for two, lifts heavy pots regularly, or wears thick gloves in cold-weather camps should skip it for a bigger gripper or a pot with locking handles. Hikers whose pot already has serviceable folding handles gain very little.

## Verdict

The Micro Lifter does one thing: it moves a hot small pot off a stove while adding almost no weight or bulk to the kit. The cost is a grip that favors packing over comfort. For solo stove cooks building a lean kit, it earns a place; for everyone else, a larger gripper is the better tool.

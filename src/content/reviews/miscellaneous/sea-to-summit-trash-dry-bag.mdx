## Overview

The Sea to Summit Trash Dry Bag is exactly what it sounds like: a 10L roll-top dry bag purpose-built for hauling out your garbage instead of stuffing it into whatever grocery sack you happened to bring. It's aimed at backpackers, bikepackers, and paddlers who want a Leave No Trace setup that doesn't leak food juice or smells into the rest of their pack. If you've ever had a trash bag tear open inside your pack liner three days into a trip, this is the product category that exists to fix that specific misery.

## Performance

At its core, this is a standard Sea to Summit dry bag body — 70D recycled nylon, taped seams, double stitching — so the waterproofing fundamentals are solid. One retailer notes the bag carries a <cite index="6-7">10,000mm hydrostatic head, and while a trash dry sack is not intended for submersion, contents will stay dry in rain or wet weather</cite>. That's the right spec for this job: you're not swimming with it, you're hanging it from a tree or bungee-ing it to a bike rack overnight.

The feature that actually separates this from a generic dry bag is the roll-top "garbage bag keeper" system. It's a bit fiddly the first time — <cite index="7-8,7-9,7-10,7-11">the cleverness of this sack is in the roll top, where the throat has plastic ribbing, the liner plastic bag tucks over the plastic, and the plastic then folds down containing the bag, and finally the top of the dry bag pulls up and over the top, locking everything in place</cite>. Once you've done it a couple times it becomes second nature, and several reviewers confirm this: <cite index="2-5,2-6">the roll-top has a series of tabs and flaps that will secure a disposable garbage bag liner in place, and it's a bit complex to figure out at first, but once you read the directions printed on the side of the bag, it works without a hitch</cite>.

That said, this isn't universal praise. A recurring theme across owner reviews is that getting the liner seated correctly takes practice and trial-and-error with liner bag sizing. One REI reviewer put it plainly: <cite index="3-11,3-12,3-13">fitting a trash bag liner is a chore that takes some practice, and it took a while to find an adequate liner bag before settling on one that fit and worked best</cite>. Another reviewer found their own solution: <cite index="7-25,7-26">the best liner bags they've found are Glad Tuff Stuff medium 51x55cm, and they haven't had a leak yet</cite>. Translation: expect to experiment with liner brands/sizes before you find your groove, and once you do, it's a "set it and forget it" system.

Durability-wise, feedback is largely positive over the long haul. One multi-year REI owner reported: <cite index="3-5">of all the dry bags/stuff sacks I've purchased, these are the ones I use the most often - with the constant packing and unpacking, they've held up great</cite>. That's a meaningful data point given how much abuse a trash bag takes — sharp bone scraps, hot food residue, repeated cinching.

On attachment, the daisy chain webbing and dual hypalon clip loops do what they're supposed to: let you hang the bag from a tree to keep it away from animals, or clip it externally to a pack or kayak deck so you're not sharing internal pack space with garbage. Practically, I'd treat this as a bear-hang-adjacent accessory in camp, not just a stuff sack — that's really the point of the clip loops.

One usability quirk worth flagging: without the liner, some users found the roll-top alone doesn't fully replace a liner bag for messy contents — it's the combination of dry bag + liner that gets you the "no smell, no leak" result, not the dry bag on its own for anything truly wet. A few Backcountry reviewers noted <cite index="1-9">the lightweight and compact design makes it convenient to bring along, though some users had difficulty with the liner bag staying in place and a few experienced manufacturing issues</cite>, so quality control hiccups, while not common, aren't unheard of.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Genuinely light at 99g for what it does — barely registers in your base weight
- Taped seams and 70D nylon mean it survives sharp gravel, thorny brush, and general trail abuse
- Roll-top + liner-keeper system, once learned, reliably contains odor and leaks
- Daisy chain and dual clip loops make external hanging/attachment easy
- Doubles as a general-purpose dry bag if you're not currently hauling trash

**Cons**

- Liner-fitting learning curve — expect some trial and error on liner bag size/brand
- Not rated for submersion, so don't treat it like a whitewater dry bag
- Occasional manufacturing inconsistencies reported by a small number of buyers
- At ~$25-30, it's a premium price for what is functionally a trash bag holder

## Who Should Buy This

This makes the most sense for multi-day backpackers, bikepackers, and paddlers who consistently pack out food waste or trash over several days rather than daily — car campers with curbside bins don't need this. If you're the type who already carries a dry bag for gear and hates the idea of a torn Safeway bag leaking into your pack, this solves a real, specific problem for under 100 grams. Thru-hikers on Leave No Trace-heavy routes (national parks, wilderness permits requiring pack-out) are the clearest use case.

## Verdict

The Sea to Summit Trash Dry Bag does one narrow job well: it keeps multi-day trash contained, odor-sealed, and off your skin without adding meaningful weight. The liner system has a learning curve and isn't flawless, but once dialed in, it's a smart, low-weight upgrade over a grocery bag and a prayer. I'd rate it a 7.5/10 — solid and purpose-built, just don't expect it to be foolproof out of the box.

## Overview

The Black Diamond ATC is the device most climbers picture when someone says "belay plate": a slotted aluminum tube with two rope slots, a wire keeper for the carabiner, and almost nothing else. At 91 g it belays leaders, lowers, and rappels, and its design has barely changed in two decades. The trade-off is that it does only those things. It has no guide mode for belaying a second off the anchor and no assisted braking, so multi-pitch climbers will want its sibling, the ATC-Guide.

## Performance

The ATC's reputation rests on rope handling. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers called it simple, light, compact, and smooth, and found it worked especially well on ropes 10 mm and thicker. In that test it was the second-smallest and second-lightest device in the field, behind only the pricier Petzl Verso. Its large surface area also helps shed heat on long rappels, one reason tube devices remain the standard descending tool.

Feeding slack is where the ATC feels best. Rope runs through the slots with little fuss, and the friction is predictable enough that new belayers can learn the standard brake-hand technique on it without fighting the device. That predictability is the main reason gyms and guiding programs keep recommending tubes.

The weak spot is lowering. Outdoor Gear Lab scored the ATC 6.0 for lowering against 8.0 for sport and gym belaying. A smooth tube gives less friction than a grooved or toothed one, so on skinny ropes, or when belaying a heavy climber, the brake hand has to work harder. Anyone running a 9 mm single or a pair of half ropes will feel this more than someone on a 10.5 mm gym rope. Owners on the ATC-XP, which adds a grippier textured side, often say they flip the device over to use the smooth side for lighter partners. The plain ATC has no such option.

Durability is a non-issue in the way owners tend to describe it: climbers who've carried one for decades still use it. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers noted that several had no plans to change devices. The one caveat they raised is basic hygiene. A device that has been dropped, or whose keeper cable is frayed, should be retired.

Anyone who counts grams will notice the ATC is not the lightest tube on the market. Black Diamond lists the ATC-XP at 64 g, and the XP's chunkier friction grooves come in under the original's weight. On a rack where every gram matters, that gap is the main argument for stepping up.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 91 g and compact enough to clip to a harness and forget
- Smooth, predictable rope feed, especially on ropes 10 mm and up
- Wide 7.7–11 mm rope range covers single, half, and twin ropes
- Inexpensive; Outdoor Gear Lab priced it around $19, among the better values in its test
- Simple enough to teach a first-time belayer
- Large surface area dissipates heat on rappels

**Cons**

- No guide mode, so it can't belay a follower directly off the anchor
- No assisted braking; the brake hand does all the work
- Less friction on thin ropes, and lowering scored lower than belaying in testing
- Heavier than Black Diamond's own ATC-XP

## Who Should Buy This

The ATC suits gym climbers, sport climbers, and anyone learning to belay who wants a cheap, dependable tool for leading, top-roping, and rappelling on ropes of roughly 9.5 mm and up. It also earns a place as a backup device or a rappel-only tool on a rack. Multi-pitch and trad climbers should skip it and buy the ATC-Guide for the extra anchor-belay function. Climbers who want the lightest possible setup, or who regularly belay on skinny single ropes, are better served by the ATC-XP or the Petzl Verso. Anyone who wants a safety net against a lapse in brake-hand discipline needs an assisted-braking device instead.

## Verdict

The ATC is a proven, no-nonsense tube that does the basics smoothly and costs about as much as a pizza. It loses points because it can't do the multi-pitch work that the ATC-Guide handles for little more money, and its friction falls short on thin ropes. For gym and single-pitch use, it's an easy recommendation.

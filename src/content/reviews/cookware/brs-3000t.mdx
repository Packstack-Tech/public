## Overview

The BRS-3000T is a titanium-alloy canister stove that weighs 26 g, just under an ounce, and sells for roughly $15–20. It threads onto any EN417 isobutane/propane canister and consists of a burner, a valve and three folding pot supports. It suits ultralight backpackers and thru-hikers who mostly boil water for freeze-dried meals and coffee, and the trade-off is plain: very little weight and money in exchange for poor wind tolerance and uneven build quality.

## Performance

**Boil times.** In still air the BRS-3000T does what a 2,700 W burner should. CleverHiker timed one liter of water at 4 minutes 45 seconds without wind. In a separate informal test by HikingHammonds, two cups came to a boil in about 2.5 minutes, between 40 seconds and a minute quicker than an MSR Pocket Rocket under the same conditions. Elevation, canister fill and pot size shift those numbers, but the stove is not slow.

**Wind.** The exposed burner is the stove's weak point. When CleverHiker aimed a small fan at it, the stove failed to bring a liter to a boil in 15 minutes. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers saw the flame blow out several times in the field. CleverHiker also noted that real mountain wind, which arrives in gusts and not as a steady blast, caused them little trouble. The practical answer is a lightweight windscreen or a sheltered cook spot. Experienced hikers tend to do both anyway, but the stove gives no margin for skipping either.

**Simmer control.** Without a regulator, the flame has effectively a high setting and a low one, with little in between. The small flame is also unstable at its lowest settings, and its narrow footprint can scorch food. The BRS-3000T is a boil-only stove. Backing the valve off by about a third pays off, though: one reviewer measured roughly 9 g of fuel per 1.5-cup boil at full flame and about 6 g at two-thirds flame. Because there is no regulator, output also fades as the canister empties, so the valve needs periodic readjustment on a long trip.

**Stability and pot compatibility.** The pot-support footprint is small, so stability depends heavily on the canister and flat ground. Small and medium pots work. Larger ones risk spills. Some owners report the supports bending or collapsing under more than a liter or so of water at full flame. Narrow pots of one liter or less, with the flame kept moderate, avoid most of this. The supports lock in place with a small metal notch when unfolded, which removes wobble once they are set.

**Packability.** This is the stove's best argument. Folded, it is barely bigger than a stick of lip balm and fits in the palm of a hand. It slips inside an MSR Titan pot alongside a 4 oz canister, so it adds almost nothing to a cook kit.

**Durability.** Reports run in both directions. Some thru-hikers have used a single unit for thousands of miles, including one CDT hiker who carried it the full trail despite its flimsy look. One long-term reviewer used theirs on nearly every backpacking and bike-touring trip for five years, with the flame flickering only in the last couple of months. Others report units that failed after a handful of boils. Quality control is inconsistent, and that variance is the main reason to carry a backup ignition source and a plan B on longer trips.

**Build feel.** The stove feels as cheap as it is. The folded legs rattle slightly. Some owners report that the threads are aluminum rather than titanium and can cross-thread, so the stove should go onto a canister slowly, especially with cold hands. Because the flame burns outward in all directions at high output, it also needs careful handling. At full flame it is loud.

**Fuel cost over distance.** At about 140 g/hr, the BRS-3000T is thirstier than more efficient, regulated designs such as the Soto Amicus or the MSR Pocket Rocket. On a long thru-hike, the extra canisters can erase part of the savings from its low purchase price.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 26 g, and it packs to about the size of a lip balm stick
- Fits inside an MSR Titan pot with a 4 oz canister
- $15–20 makes it cheap enough to carry as a backup stove
- Fast boil times in calm air
- Simple operation: thread on, open the valve, light
- Includes a stuff sack and a spare O-ring

**Cons**

- No igniter, so a separate lighter is mandatory
- No regulator; output fades as the canister empties
- Poor wind resistance, making a windscreen close to essential
- Small pot-support footprint; larger pots can be unstable
- Inconsistent quality control; some units fail early
- Loud at full flame, with poor low-flame simmer
- Higher fuel consumption than more efficient stoves

## Who Should Buy This

The BRS-3000T fits solo three-season hikers on a boil-only routine of freeze-dried dinners and morning coffee, who can find or build wind cover and want every gram accounted for. It also works as an emergency or redundancy stove, since it adds almost no weight or cost to a kit. Car campers, group cooks, anyone expecting sustained wind, and anyone who wants to do actual cooking should look at a regulated stove such as the Soto Amicus or MSR Pocket Rocket instead.

## Verdict

The BRS-3000T boils water faster than its price suggests and disappears into a cook pot. Its wind sensitivity and spotty quality control are real limits, not quibbles. Buy it for fair-weather ultralight trips or as a featherweight backup, and don't make it the only stove on an alpine trip.

## Overview

The Modulaire Slaapzak is the Dutch Army's answer to a simple question: what if one sleeping bag could be three? A 1,000 g summer bag, a 1,900 g three-season bag and a zip-up liner sheet can be used alone, or nested for winter. Soldiers get a system that scales from mild nights to deep freeze. Ultralight backpackers get a 3.5 kg kit that costs roughly what a good down quilt's stuff sack does.

## Performance

**Warmth.** The system's appeal is range. The summer bag is rated to 5°C comfort on its own. The three-season bag alone is where the claims get ambitious: the -19°C comfort figure appears in Dutch surplus listings, and owners on bushcraft forums have questioned it, noting that the number reads more like an extreme-survival rating than a comfort one. One surplus reviewer rates the outer bag at about -10°C when used by itself, which is a more cautious reading and a better planning number for sleeping in it. Nest the two bags and the rated comfort drops to -40°C, a figure that comes from the manufacturer and is not an independent measurement. Retailer Bushpappa reports testing the bags down to -20°C without feeling cold, though the retailer also carries them with a bivy bag. Buyers describe the full combination as warm to the point of overkill in moderate cold; one customer slept in the winter configuration with the liner and called it borderline too warm.

**Weight and bulk.** This is where the system loses an ultralight audience. The two bags alone come to 2,900 g, and the liner and compression sack bring the full kit to about 3,500 g in Medium. For comparison, that is several times the weight of a typical three-season down quilt. Synthetic hollow fiber is also bulky. The three-season bag compresses to 32 x 27 cm, and the full winter stack to 45 x 27 cm, which fills a good share of a 40-liter pack. Bushpappa's own guides are frank about the trade-off, noting that the Dutch sleeping bags are fairly heavy and that they accept the weight for comfort. Nobody here is pretending otherwise.

**Fit and features.** The cut is generous. A 6'4", 290 lb reviewer on Bushcraft USA found the Dutch system snug but workable, and a better fit than the US military equivalent. The bags use a two-way zipper and an adjustable face opening. The summer bag adds a zip-up mesh insect screen over the hood. The underside has loops for a sleeping pad, which stops the bag from migrating off the pad overnight. That is a small touch that anyone who has slid sideways off a slick pad at 3 a.m. will appreciate. The liner attaches with eight ribbons threaded through loops, adds a little warmth, keeps the bag cleaner, and works as a standalone sheet in hot weather.

**Durability and moisture.** The shells are ripstop nylon, and the bags are built for hard use. Hollow-fiber synthetic fill tolerates damp better than down and doesn't need babying, which suits wet-climate and canoe-trip use. The usual synthetic caveat applies: sweat and condensation build up in a full winter stack. Users advise airing the bags regularly (each has hang loops at the foot) and storing them uncompressed.

**Condition and sourcing.** The bags are military surplus. Bushpappa lists the system at EUR 124.95 and other Dutch dealers sell used sets at similar prices, so condition varies. Buyers on retailer pages and eBay mostly describe near-new or lightly used sets, though pulled threads and wear turn up in some used examples.

## Choosing a Version

Three lengths are offered, and the choice is about height, not warmth. Medium suits users up to 1.8 m and is the 3,500 g baseline. Large fits up to 1.9 m and weighs about 3,800 g. Extra Large covers users up to 2.2 m. Surplus listings for the Dutch system give flat lengths of roughly 200, 210 and 220 cm for Medium, Large and XL, with the XL also widening at the shoulders. Because the nested winter configuration sits tight, taller users should size up rather than rely on the bags stretching. Anyone at the top of a size's height range will find the stacked bags noticeably snug.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Three temperature configurations from one purchase
- Winter combination rated to -40°C by the manufacturer
- Synthetic fill that handles damp
- Generous cut and sleeping-pad loops
- Priced well below comparable new modular systems
- Durable ripstop shells that tolerate rough use

**Cons**

- 3,500 g for the complete system (Medium) is far from ultralight
- Bulky: 45 x 27 cm packed in winter mode
- Comfort ratings are optimistic, and owners recommend treating the -19°C figure with caution
- Surplus condition varies
- Nested bags run snug and can feel too warm in moderate cold

## Who Should Buy This

This system suits bushcrafters, canoe-trip campers, winter car campers, scout leaders and anyone who wants one purchase to cover summer through deep winter without the cost of separate bags. It also works as a way to learn winter camping, since the layers can be added as nights get colder. Thru-hikers, gram-counters and anyone carrying a pack for more than a couple of days should look elsewhere. The summer bag alone at 1,000 g is the only piece that comes close to backpacking weight, and even that is heavy for a +5°C bag.

## Verdict

The Modulaire Slaapzak is a good value for what it is: a rugged, adaptable and warm sleep system for people who drive, paddle or pull a sled. It is a poor fit for anyone counting grams. Buy it for base-camp and winter use, and use a down quilt for the trail.

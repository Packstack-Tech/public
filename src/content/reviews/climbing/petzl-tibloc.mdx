## Overview

The Petzl Tibloc is a stainless-steel rope clamp about the size of a walnut. It weighs 35 g and does the work of a full-size ascender in a pinch: climbing a rope, capturing progress in a hauling system, or standing in for a friction hitch. It has been around since 1998, and its appeal for weight-conscious alpinists and glacier travelers hasn't changed. The trade-off is that it's a blunt, toothed tool. It's unforgiving on ropes and awkward to handle, and it demands practice.

## Performance

**Weight and packability.** This is where the Tibloc wins outright. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers weighed it at 34 g, about the same as a small wire-gate carabiner, and scored it a perfect 10 for weight. Its lead tester often carries one or two on the back of the harness, racked with an autoblock and a small knife as a mini emergency kit. It earned the site's Best Buy award among ascenders. Even a hiker counting grams on a glacier traverse has trouble arguing against 35 g.

**Grip and the 2018 redesign.** The current Tibloc is the second generation. Petzl added an orange plastic hood with a small spring that pushes the carabiner against the rope, so the teeth engage immediately in any position. The original relied on the user to seat it correctly. If it sat partly engaged and was loaded or moved, it could tear the sheath. Reviewers who have used both describe the update as a large step forward in ease of use. It also brought a small weight saving, from 37 g to 35 g. The cost is moving and plastic parts, so the new version needs the occasional inspection. A few long-time users still say they prefer the simpler first generation.

**Thin cord.** The redesign made the Tibloc far more useful on 6 mm cord. It is approved for Petzl's RAD LINE crevasse-rescue cord, and glacier travelers who carry it report it is faster to place than a prusik and works on skinny cord that prusiks handle poorly. One early tester found the spring needed a larger carabiner, a Petzl Hera rather than an Attache 3D, to engage fully on 6 mm line. Carabiner choice matters, and a large-radius locker is the safe default.

**Ice, mud and wet ropes.** The angled teeth and slot are designed to shed debris. Users and retailers consistently report that it bites better than a prusik on frozen or slimy rope. The steel doesn't degrade in UV the way cord does.

**Handling.** Here the Tibloc shows its limits. The carabiner serves as the handle, so there's nothing to grip while ascending, and Outdoor Gear Lab scored it 5 for clipping and 4 for comfort. Users report it is hard to work with gloves on. They also say it is difficult to slide back down the rope when switching to rappel mode. This is an ascender for short distances and emergencies. It is not for jugging a long way.

**Rope damage.** Toothed clamps mark ropes, and the Tibloc is the most notorious of them. One retailer review puts the point at which it starts to damage a rope at around 4 kN, even when correctly set up. Guides describe a crevasse-rescue training session in which a heavy climber put a core shot a meter long into an 8 mm dynamic rope by weighting the Tibloc. Climbers who know the device say it is poorly suited to anything involving a shock load. Petzl's documentation does not list it for self-belaying or rope soloing, and retailers explicitly warn against it. It can protect the leader in simul-climbing, but that is advanced use. Thin ropes at the bottom of the 8 mm range are the least forgiving.

**Durability.** The all-steel body survives falls and rough handling. One owner reports it came through a 200-ft drop undamaged. It is small enough to lose, so a keeper cord through the attachment hole is worth the extra few grams.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 35 g, about as light as a mechanical rope grab gets
- Faster to place than a prusik, and it grips icy, muddy or thin cord more reliably
- Approved for 6 mm RAD LINE, which suits compact glacier-rescue kits
- Spring-loaded hood on the current version engages the teeth automatically
- Stainless body is corrosion-resistant and unaffected by UV, unlike cord
- Inexpensive compared with other ascenders and pulley-style capture devices

**Cons**

- Teeth can damage ropes, especially thin ones and under shock loads
- No handle, so the carabiner is the only thing to hold; hard to operate with gloves
- Difficult to release and slide down the rope
- Not approved for self-belaying
- Needs the right carabiner; the plastic-and-spring hood requires periodic inspection
- Small enough to drop or lose

## Who Should Buy This

Glacier travelers, alpinists, and multi-pitch climbers who want a backup ascender or a lightweight crevasse-rescue component should buy it. It suits a team that already practices hauling systems and wants to ditch the prusik cords. It also fits skiers carrying 6 mm cord, for whom the RAD LINE compatibility is the main draw.

Anyone who expects to ascend long sections of rope, work in thick gloves, or rope-solo should look elsewhere. A Micro Traxion, Ropeman or conventional handled ascender suits those jobs better. Climbers who haven't practiced the system should learn it in a controlled setting before they depend on it.

## Verdict

The Tibloc is a cheap, almost weightless piece of insurance that does one job well: a faster, more reliable grip than a prusik when something has gone wrong. It's hard on rope and awkward to use, so it belongs in the emergency kit and not as a primary ascender. For anyone who carries a rope in the mountains, it's an easy buy.

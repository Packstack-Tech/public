## Overview

The Windrider 55 is Hyperlite Mountain Gear's original ultralight backpacking pack: a roll-top bag of waterproof Dyneema Composite Fabric with three mesh pockets bolted to the outside. At 896 g it carries a week of food for a hiker who has already trimmed the rest of the kit. The trade-off is plain. It gives up load lifters, zippers and a forgiving suspension for a pack that sheds water, dries fast and weighs about as much as a pair of trail runners.

## Performance

**Weather protection.** The Windrider's best trait is the one hikers mention first. DCH is waterproof, the seams are taped, and the roll-top keeps water out when it's rolled properly. A reviewer who carried the pack on a 2023 Appalachian Trail thru-hike, starting in March and finishing in summer heat, put it through multiple days of torrential rain and temperatures from -5°F to 95°F. He concluded it suits wet, wooded routes like the AT. The mesh pockets cut the other way: they drain immediately and let a wet tarp or shelter dry on the move.

**Capacity.** The 55 L figure is the main bag filled until the top can still be rolled at least three times. The Big Outside notes that the roll-top works as compression and leaves some slack above that. The 9.8 L of mesh on the outside brings the working total closer to 65 L, and the Y-strap on top holds a foam pad or a bulky item. The Big Outside calls the Windrider unusual for how many days between resupplies it can handle relative to its empty weight. Hikers with a compact kit routinely stretch it to a week of food. Past that, or with a bear canister, it fills quickly.

**Comfort and carry.** The suspension is a foam back panel and shoulder straps with spacer mesh, plus two aluminum stays that can be pulled to save weight. Owners are largely positive. A couple who crossed Iceland, roughly 400 miles with up to eight days of food and plenty of water, reported none of the shoulder pain they'd had in other packs. The 40 lb rating is a real ceiling. The stays give a lightweight load some structure, but a heavy start (a long water carry or a winter kit) tests the frameless-feeling carry. The AT reviewer also flagged the absence of load lifters, which matters most when the pack is stuffed high. The large hip belt pockets draw praise for holding snacks and a phone without a stop.

**Durability.** DCH50 on the body and DCH150 on the bottom is a sensible split: the heavier fabric goes where the pack gets set down on rock and roots. The Windrider's weak point is the mesh, not the Dyneema. SectionHiker's buyer's guide points out that the mesh can catch on overhanging vegetation and tear, which makes it less durable than the Southwest, which has solid pockets. Thru-hikers on brushy, less-traveled trails should expect to patch it at some point.

**Breathability.** DCH doesn't breathe, and the AT reviewer listed that as a drawback in hot weather. The back panel takes the sweat.

**Fit.** The same reviewer found sizing difficult for smaller women. Torso length should be measured before ordering, and the pack should be tried loaded if possible.

**Owner satisfaction.** The product page lists a 4.8 out of 5 average across 490 reviews, with 98 percent of reviewers recommending it. That's a manufacturer-hosted tally, but the complaints in independent reviews are consistent with it: the issues are specific and mostly about features the pack leaves out by design.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 896 g with a 40 lb rating and a removable frame
- Waterproof DCH fabric with taped seams and a roll-top
- 9.8 L of mesh drains and dries wet gear as you hike
- Large hip belt pockets
- Expands and contracts well for anything from a weekend to a resupply-light week
- Owners report all-day comfort at moderate loads

**Cons**

- No load lifters
- Mesh pockets snag and tear on brushy trails
- DCH doesn't vent in heat
- Fit is difficult for some smaller frames
- No lid, no zippered access, no top-loading convenience
- Premium Dyneema price

## Who Should Buy This

Thru-hikers and section hikers with a base weight low enough to keep the pack under 30 lb or so, particularly on wet-climate trails such as the AT, the Pacific Northwest or the Scottish and Icelandic crossings that owners cite. It's also a good fit for anyone who wants the hardest-wearing fabric Hyperlite sells without paying for a heavier pack. Hikers who push through thick brush should look at the Southwest or Junction, which swap mesh for solid pockets. Hikers who regularly carry more than 40 lb, who want load lifters, or who want zippered access to the main bag should look elsewhere.

## Verdict

The Windrider 55 is a proven thru-hiking pack: waterproof, light, comfortable at moderate weights and simple enough that little can go wrong. Its mesh pockets and missing load lifters make it a better choice for disciplined ultralighters than for hikers still carrying a heavy kit. For its intended load and conditions, it's one of the safer buys in the category.

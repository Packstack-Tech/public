## Overview

The Lone Peak 8 is the shoe that a large share of the Appalachian Trail and PCT crowd wears, mostly because of one feature: a toe box that lets swollen feet spread out. Version 8 swaps the previous air mesh for a ripstop upper and keeps the zero-drop, 25 mm platform intact. The trade-off is the same as it has been for years. The foot is happy, but the outsole rubber is the first part to give out.

## Performance

**Fit and comfort.** The toe box is the reason to buy this shoe. Thru-hikers have long recommended Lone Peaks as a blister preventive. The extra forefoot volume gives toes room to splay and absorbs the swelling that arrives in the first week of a long walk or on a hot afternoon. RunRepeat's testers described the fit as secure through the midfoot yet accommodating up front, and a flexible ride that works for both running and extended hiking. The heel collar is slightly stiffer and more reinforced than on the Lone Peak 7, and reviewers report decent heel and ankle lockdown.

**Zero drop.** Altra recommends up to three weeks of gradual break-in to its zero-drop geometry, and hikers coming from a conventional heel-lifted shoe should take that seriously. Calves and Achilles tendons notice the change first. Once adapted, testers describe a natural, low-to-the-ground ride. Outdoor Gear Lab's reviewers found the 25 mm stack stable and gentle on ankles, knees, and hips without feeling harsh.

**Upper.** The ripstop mesh was the headline update over the Lone Peak 7, and it addressed the most common complaint about that shoe, uppers that tore early. The mesh breathes well and dries quickly. It is not waterproof, so bogs and sustained stream crossings leave the shoe saturated, though it sheds water fast once the trail dries out. A wide toe bumper guards against rocks and roots. Owners who followed the line through several generations did note that the stitching on the toe was dropped.

**Traction.** Opinion splits here. Outdoor Gear Lab called the rubber among the softest it has tested, with good grip in the wet, and noted that hikers coming from large lugs may feel less confident on loose ground. The Trek's long-distance reviewer found the grip fairly average and said wet rock and ice call for extra caution. RunRepeat flagged mediocre wet grip as a carryover from the Lone Peak 7. The practical read: the MaxTrac outsole is fine for dry, moderate trail and dirt, acceptable on wet leaves, and not the tool for steep mud, snow, or slick granite.

**Protection.** The Stoneguard layer and a tough outsole give some armor against sharp rocks, and one running-store tester reported no worry over rocks and roots. Outdoor Gear Lab saw it differently, noting that the soft forefoot cushioning gives good ground feel at the cost of reduced protection from debris. Heavy packs on rocky terrain will expose that.

**Durability.** This is the shoe's persistent weakness. The upper now outlasts the outsole, and testers who logged several hundred kilometers saw lug wear from abrasive rock. Hikers who have gone through multiple pairs of Lone Peaks on a single thru-hike say the shoes have improved since the early versions but remain short-lived compared with boots and some rival trail runners. Owners have also asked repeatedly for Vibram rubber in place of MaxTrac. Altra's own later Lone Peak 9 offers a Vibram option, which is telling.

**Gaiter compatibility.** The heel velcro tab grabs the matching velcro on running gaiters, saving hikers from the superglue-and-hope method that tends to fail mid-trail.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Wide FootShape toe box that suits swollen, long-distance feet
- Ripstop upper is tougher than the Lone Peak 7's mesh
- Light at 303 g per shoe, breathable, and quick to dry
- Stable 25 mm zero-drop platform
- Built-in gaiter trap
- Lower MSRP than its predecessor, reported at $140

**Cons**

- MaxTrac outsole wears faster than premium rubber such as Vibram
- Grip on wet rock, mud, and ice is inconsistent
- Not waterproof, a poor match for sustained snow or boggy routes
- Zero drop requires a gradual adjustment
- Some longtime owners find recent generations narrower and less durable than older Lone Peaks

## Who Should Buy This

Hikers with wide or swelling feet, anyone already comfortable in zero-drop shoes, and thru-hikers who expect to replace shoes every 500 miles or so will get the most from the Lone Peak 8. It suits dry to moderately wet, mixed trail, and those who use gaiters will like the attachment. Buyers who need sticky rubber for wet granite, aggressive lugs for mud, or a shoe that lasts a thousand miles should look at Vibram-soled alternatives. So should anyone who needs a heel-to-toe drop or a narrow performance fit.

## Verdict

The Lone Peak 8 earns its reputation as a comfortable, forgiving long-distance shoe, and its tougher upper fixes the Lone Peak 7's most serious flaw. The outsole keeps it from being a complete answer, and hikers who care most about grip and mileage should check the Vibram-equipped Lone Peak 9 first.

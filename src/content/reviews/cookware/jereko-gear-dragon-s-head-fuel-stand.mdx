## Overview

The Dragon's Head Fuel Stand is a 3D-printed, plant-based plastic canister stabilizer from Jereko Gear, a small cottage brand out of Varysburg, NY. It's built for canister-stove users who want a wider, more stable base on uneven ground without adding real weight to their cook kit — and it's the kind of accessory that's easy to justify at 10 grams and roughly $6.

## Performance

Canister stands solve a real problem: a tall fuel canister with a stove and full pot on top is a top-heavy, tippy setup, especially on the lumpy, root-covered ground you actually find at most campsites. The two big names in this space — <cite index="17-4,17-5">the MSR Universal Fuel Canister stand improves stability when cooking with a canister stove, with a broad stainless-steel base that increases stove stability and helps prevent spills, plus a spring-loaded ABS plastic adjustment for a fast, reliable fit</cite> — and the Jetboil Fuel Can Stabilizer, work fine but come with known failure modes. Jetboil owners have reported that <cite index="18-6,18-7">the plastic is rigid and brittle as compared with pliable, and applying more pressure to pull the canister out resulted in breaking one of the attachment points</cite>. Another reviewer summed up the long-term durability concern bluntly: <cite index="18-21">"it's great until it breaks which is inevitable."</cite> The MSR stand avoids some of that brittleness but isn't foolproof either — one long-time user noted <cite index="17-3">the only possible concern is the spring on the adjustable black piece going</cite>.

The Dragon's Head sidesteps both of those failure points by using a fundamentally different mechanism. Instead of rigid clip-on arms or a spring latch, it uses <cite index="1-10">elastic cordage that secures the feet of the stand to your fuel canister, allowing your fuel and stove to adapt to uneven terrain</cite>. That's a smart design choice for a 3D-printed part — PLA is inherently more brittle than the ABS or steel used in MSR/Jetboil's stands, so putting the flex in a cord instead of the plastic itself avoids the classic "snapped tab" complaint that dogs rigid canister stands. It also means the stand isn't picky about exact canister diameter the way the Jetboil stabilizer is, which one reviewer noted <cite index="18-18">does not fit any of my Coleman brand fuel canisters for example, which are commonly available at walmarts; they are slightly too wide</cite>. By spec, the Dragon's Head is rated to <cite index="7-6">fit 3.5 oz, 8 oz, and 16 oz isobutane fuel canisters</cite>, and the cord-based fit should flex a bit more across brands than a fixed-clearance clip design.

Weight-wise, this thing is genuinely light for the category. For context, a hobbyist-reviewed 3D-printed canister stand came in at 23 grams, while <cite index="19-15,19-17">the MSR Universal Canister Stand weighs 34 grams and retails for $24.99, while the Jetboil fuel can stabilizer weighs 27 grams and retails for $6.99</cite>. At 10 grams, the Dragon's Head undercuts all of those by more than half while landing at roughly the same price as the Jetboil option. If you're the type who cuts toothbrush handles to save a gram, this is an easy win.

The bonus feature — using the elastic cord as a bungee for miscellaneous gear when you're not cooking — is a nice touch on paper. <cite index="1-11">The elastic cordage doubles as a bungee to wrap up miscellaneous gear such as open bags, sit-pads, or even as a hair tie if the need arises</cite>. In practice, I wouldn't buy this product for that feature alone, but it's a legitimate bit of multi-use thinking that ultralight folks tend to appreciate.

One honest caveat: I couldn't find independent trail reports or forum threads (Reddit, BPL, etc.) specifically reviewing the Dragon's Head Fuel Stand — most of what's out there is retailer product copy from Garage Grown Gear, Etsy, and Jereko's own site. So take the durability read here as informed inference from the design and from comparable 3D-printed/cord-based stands, not confirmed long-term field data. If you're the type who wants three years of Reddit horror stories before buying, this one hasn't accumulated that track record yet.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**
- Exceptionally light at 10g — less than a third the weight of MSR's stand and well under half of Jetboil's
- Cord-based flex avoids the brittle-plastic snapping failure mode common to rigid clip stands
- Fits a wide range of canister sizes (3.5 oz to 16 oz) thanks to the adjustable elastic cordage
- Doubles as a bungee/utility cord when not stabilizing a canister
- Plant-based plastic construction and a small-batch, made-by-hikers ethos
- Cheap — around $6

**Cons**
- Limited independent, long-term user reviews to confirm durability in the field
- Cord-and-post design likely feels less "locked in" underfoot than a rigid stand, especially on very soft ground
- As a small-batch 3D-printed product, availability and consistency between batches could vary
- Doesn't address stability for non-canister stoves or remote-burner setups

## Who Should Buy This

This is a good pick for canister-stove users doing base-weight audits who want the stability benefit of a fuel stand without paying the weight tax of the MSR or Jetboil options. It's especially useful if you regularly cook on uneven ground with an 8 oz or 16 oz canister and a decent-sized pot, where top-heaviness is the real risk. If you want a bomber, decade-proven product backed by mountains of user data, the Jetboil or MSR stands still have the track record; if you're comfortable supporting a small cottage maker and shaving grams, the Dragon's Head is a low-risk, low-cost way to do it.

## Verdict

At 10 grams and $6, the Dragon's Head Fuel Stand undercuts the established competition on weight by a wide margin while using a cord-based design that should sidestep the brittle-plastic failures reported on rigid canister stands. I'd rate it a solid 7.5/10 — the engineering logic is sound and the price is a non-issue, but it hasn't yet built up the years of field-tested user feedback that the MSR and Jetboil stands have, so go in expecting a promising newcomer rather than a proven veteran.

## Overview

The Companion Spark takes Morakniv's ubiquitous Companion — arguably the most recommended budget fixed-blade in the outdoor world — and bolts a ferro rod into the pommel. It's aimed at campers, scouts, and beginner bushcrafters who want a single cheap tool that cuts and starts fires, rather than two separate items rattling around in a pack.

## Performance

**Cutting.** The Scandi-ground 12C27 blade behaves exactly like every other Mora Companion, which is to say it's one of the better values in cutting steel you can buy. One reviewer who spent time carving <cite index="2-1,2-2,2-3,2-4">used the Spark to carve out implements like a fork, a couple of spoons, and some gorge hooks, noting that the wider part of the blade was good for removing mass while the belly finely carved material away</cite>. Digging a spoon bowl is a decent stress test for a thin stock blade, and the same reviewer noted <cite index="2-5,2-6">that plunging and working the belly to hollow out a bowl can be pretty hard on a blade, but the Companion Spark took it with ease</cite>. Out of the box sharpness also gets consistent praise — <cite index="2-16">one tester found it "really sharp" right out of the package, which made kitchen and woodcraft tasks easier</cite>.

At 2.5 mm thick and without a full tang, don't expect this to baton firewood all day. As one reviewer put it plainly, <cite index="16-14">the lack of a full tang might limit its suitability for heavy-duty tasks like batoning</cite>. Think kindling prep, feather sticks, and food prep — not splitting rounds.

**Ferro rod.** This is the feature that actually differentiates the Spark from a standard $15 Companion, and by most accounts it works as advertised. In damp Appalachian conditions, <cite index="16-10,16-11">the blade sliced cleanly through small branches and feather-sticked pine with ease, and striking the integrated fire starter against the spine produced a shower of sparks that quickly ignited prepared tinder</cite>. That's a meaningful detail worth calling out: Morakniv specifically squares off the spine at the factory for this purpose. <cite index="9-7">The spine of the blade has been ground to 90° for use with the included firestriker</cite> — which matters because standard (non-Spark) Companions often ship with a polished spine that won't throw sparks until you file it flat yourself, a common complaint on the base model. The Spark sidesteps that fix entirely.

One nice touch: the rod attaches via <cite index="13-5">a twist-lock mechanism with reflective paracord for easy visibility</cite>, so it's less likely to get separated from the knife and lost in leaf litter at dusk.

**Ergonomics.** The handle is unchanged from the standard Companion, which is a compliment — it's one of the more universally liked grips in the budget-knife world. <cite index="10-3,10-4,10-5">Whenever someone picks up a Companion, one of the first things they mention is how comfortable it is, and the Companion and Companion Spark have what many consider the ideal handle size and design.</cite> In wet conditions, testers reported <cite index="15-1">the knife performed admirably even in wet conditions, thanks to its grippy handle and sharp blade</cite>.

**Sheath.** Functional but nothing special. It's a simple polypropylene friction-fit sheath with a belt clip — one reviewer summed it up as <cite index="16-21">functional, but some users may prefer a more robust or versatile</cite> option. If you're picky about retention or want a MOLLE-compatible setup, budget for an aftermarket sheath eventually.

**Steel choice tradeoff.** Morakniv also sells a carbon steel Companion HD and standard carbon versions. The stainless 12C27 in the Spark trades a little edge-holding aggression for zero rust worries — a fair trade for a knife that's going to live in a damp pack or get used around a lake. If you want the carbon steel's slightly easier field sharpening and marginally keener edge, know that <cite index="14-13,14-14">carbon steel has zero rust resistance and will rust overnight if put away wet</cite>, which is exactly the kind of maintenance ultralight backpackers tend to forget about on a five-day stretch.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Excellent cutting performance for the price — the Scandi grind on 12C27 is easy to maintain and holds a working edge well
- Factory-ground 90° spine means the ferro rod actually works out of the box, no DIY filing required
- Comfortable, secure handle even with wet or gloved hands
- Stainless steel means no rust babysitting
- At 128 g including sheath, it's light enough to carry without a second thought
- Genuinely inexpensive — this is a sub-$50 knife that punches well above its price class

**Cons**

- Not full tang — hard batoning or heavy prying isn't what it's built for
- Sheath is basic; no locking retention, and some users want something sturdier
- Handle/blade design hasn't changed in years, so if you already own a standard Companion, the Spark is a lateral upgrade at best, not a reinvention
- Integrated ferro rod is smaller than a standalone one — fine for backup fire starting, but not a substitute for a full-length rod if that's your primary method

## Who Should Buy This

This is a strong pick for anyone who wants one inexpensive, dependable cutting tool with fire-starting capability baked in — car campers, Scouts, hunters, and beginner bushcrafters especially. If you already carry a full-size ferro rod and a knife you're happy with, there's no urgent reason to switch. But as a single grab-and-go tool for a first aid kit, vehicle kit, or a backup blade in a backpacking setup, it's hard to argue with the value.

## Verdict

The Companion Spark takes an already-excellent budget knife and fixes the one legitimate complaint about the standard Companion — a spine that wouldn't spark a ferro rod without modification — by shipping it pre-squared. It won't replace a full-tang survival knife for hard use, but for feather sticks, food prep, and reliable fire starting at 128 g, it's tough to beat for the money. I'd rate it an 8/10: not flashy, but it does exactly what it promises and does it well.

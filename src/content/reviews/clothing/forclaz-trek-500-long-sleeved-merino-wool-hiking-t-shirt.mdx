## Overview

The Forclaz Trek 500 is Decathlon's midweight merino long-sleeve base layer, built for multi-day trekking in cool conditions. It wraps merino fibers in polyamide for durability and undercuts the big merino brands on price by a wide margin. The trade-off is a single, fairly heavy fabric weight that suits spring, fall and shoulder-season alpine trips better than a July section hike.

## Performance

**Warmth and temperature range.** The shirt has one fabric weight, 190 g/m², and that decides where it works. Owners who wear it on cold-weather hikes, ski tours and Nordic walks describe it as thin but warm, and as comfortable without the clammy feeling of a sweaty synthetic. One reviewer wore it as a layer on a Zugspitze hike and found it kept them warm. Gear reviewers who have put it through its paces say it is warm when it's cold and reasonably breathable when it's hot. Even so, 190 g/m² is more fabric than most hikers want next to the skin in summer heat. In practice it is a spring-through-fall shirt, and an effective sleeping or camp layer on cold nights. On a mountain pass in October it is well matched to the job. On a July desert crossing it is the wrong tool.

**Odor control.** This is where merino earns its weight, and owners report that the Trek 500 delivers. Wearers describe little or no smell after hard days, and one hiker noted none on a summit push. French reviewers at Test 4 Outside noted a limit: the shirt resists odor for a long time but eventually saturates, which they attribute to the synthetic content. Their fix is a simple rinse and dry, which brings it back to normal. That suits the usual trek habit of rotating two shirts, or one shirt and a sleep top.

**Moisture and drying.** Testers report the shirt doesn't feel cold when damp, which is the wool doing its usual trick, and that it dries fast for a merino blend. That means a trail-side rinse at lunch can be dry by the afternoon climb. It will not dry as quickly as a pure synthetic grid fleece or a sun shirt, and it should not be expected to.

**Comfort and fit.** The 18.5-micron wool is on the fine side for the price, and owners consistently call it soft and itch-free. Several buyers who came from Icebreaker tops and leggings said they preferred the Forclaz for softness and fit, and one called it a bargain at about half the price. Test 4 Outside found the sleeves long enough to reach the hands, the seams free of irritation, and the round neckline snug, a plus in the cold that becomes a minor annoyance when it's warm. One US buyer reported a slight quality-control blemish on an inner seam that did not affect function.

**Durability.** The polyamide wrapped around the wool fibers is the shirt's main durability feature, and the early evidence supports it. A long-term owner reported two shirts nearly a year old, worn and washed often, with no visible wear. Testers saw no premature wear during their trial. Merino is still merino: expect some pilling over time, and keep it away from thick brush and abrasive pack straps if you want it to last. Wash it cool and dry it flat. A small number of buyers on Decathlon's European sites have been puzzled by care labels, so check the tag on your particular shirt.

**Weight.** At 221 g (7.8 oz) in a size M, it is not a featherweight. Lighter merino shirts in the 150 g/m² class save a meaningful amount, and Decathlon's own MT900 line is aimed at people who count every gram. The Trek 500 is the sturdier, more comfortable choice in the Forclaz range, and that is the trade it makes.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Soft 18.5-micron merino that owners say does not itch
- Strong odor resistance over multi-day wear
- Polyamide-wrapped fibers add durability, with reports of a year of regular use and washing
- Warm when damp and dries quickly for a wool blend
- Sleeves long enough to cover the hands; seams reported as irritation-free
- Price roughly half that of the major merino brands, according to buyers who have owned both

**Cons**

- 190 g/m² is too warm to wear alone in summer heat
- 221 g (7.8 oz) is heavier than lighter-weight merino options
- Odor resistance fades eventually, because of the synthetic content
- Snug round neckline can feel restrictive when it's hot
- Wool needs gentler care than synthetics: cool wash, no dryer, and some pilling over time

## Who Should Buy This

This is a good fit for backpackers who hike in cool to cold conditions: shoulder-season trekkers, alpine hikers, and anyone who needs a warm layer for camp and for sleeping. It also suits first-time merino buyers who want to find out whether wool works for them without spending a large sum. Hikers who sweat through July and August on exposed trails, or who weigh every ounce in a base weight, should look at a lighter merino or a synthetic grid base layer instead.

## Verdict

The Trek 500 gives most of what people buy premium merino for, which is softness, odor control and temperature regulation, at a price that makes it easy to own two. It is heavier and warmer than a summer shirt, and best treated as a cool-weather workhorse. For shoulder-season trekking it is one of the better-value merino base layers around.

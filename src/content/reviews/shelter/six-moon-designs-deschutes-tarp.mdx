## Overview

The Deschutes is Six Moon Designs' stripped-down answer to the pyramid shelter: a six-sided, single-pole silnylon tarp with a zippered front, no floor and no bug netting. It replaced the company's older Wild Oasis, and it suits solo hikers who already carry trekking poles and want a weatherproof roof at 370 g. The trade-off is built into the design. Owners get a lot of room for the weight, and they supply their own groundsheet, bug plan and taut-pitch skills.

## Performance

**Weather.** The hexagonal pyramid shape does the work. Reviewers who have pitched it in the field say it sheds wind and rain the way mids generally do. Long-term owners describe riding out stiff wind and heavy rain, and one reports that snow slid off cleanly. Pole height is adjustable. A lower pole brings the walls closer to the ground and improves storm protection, but it costs headroom. Raising the doors a few inches helps keep the interior dry. The tarp doesn't reach the ground at the front, and that is by design. The two angled doors form a raised vestibule that is deep enough to block splash-back, and it adds airflow. It is not a sealed-to-the-dirt shelter unless the pole is lowered and the edges are staked tight.

**Space.** Space is the Deschutes' best argument. At 44 sq ft it has room for a sleeper, a pack, water bottles and shoes. A 6'1" bikepacking reviewer was surprised by the room, including enough height to sit upright. SectionHiker, by contrast, advises against it for anyone over 6'. Taller hikers should expect to dial in the pitch, since the peak sits offset toward the front, and pitching it with head and foot tie-outs makes a noticeable difference to headroom.

**Setup.** One pole and six stakes make a simple pitch. A Colorado Trail thru-hiker called it easy to pitch and found it well sewn enough to get a taut pitch without a fight. Even so, getting a clean pitch at different pole heights takes a few nights of practice, and The Trek's tester said the same of tarps generally. Two extra stakes and some guyline will pull the sides out for more headroom.

**Packability and weight.** The tarp packs to roughly the size of a Nalgene, and owners on r/Ultralight say it squeezes down to fit in a hand. SectionHiker's unsealed sample came in at 11.4 oz, below the claimed 13 oz. A Trailspace reviewer measured 13.6 oz after seam-sealing it. The tarp ships unsealed, so factor in an evening with a tube of sealer, or the extra cost of buying it sealed.

**Condensation and bugs.** Like any single-wall shelter, it collects condensation. A roof vent helps, and so does rolling back one or both doors. Bugs are the bigger issue. Six Moon Designs sells a bug skirt option (the Plus, about 3 oz extra, roughly 16 oz total) and the 11 oz Serenity Net Tent, which brings the system to about 24 oz. Owners are lukewarm on the second option. The Serenity inner is rectangular and the tarp is hexagonal, so one Trailspace reviewer found it cut interior space nearly in half. Hikers who need full bug protection will find better value in a floored, netted shelter at this weight.

**Durability.** The 20D silnylon is thin and has to be treated that way. Silnylon sags when wet and re-tensions as it dries, so a late-night adjustment is part of the routine. It also wets through if gear or a sleeping bag presses against the inner wall. One owner who has used his for more than a decade in all kinds of weather still recommends it enthusiastically.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 44 sq ft of coverage for 370 g, which is generous for a solo shelter
- One pole and six stakes; the pitch is straightforward
- Adjustable pole height tunes it for storms or ventilation
- Packs to about the size of a water bottle
- Doors roll back for airflow and views
- A modular system: bug skirt and net tent are available later

**Cons**

- No floor, no bug protection in the base configuration
- Ships unsealed; seam-sealing is the buyer's job
- Silnylon sags when wet and needs re-tensioning
- Raised vestibule edge doesn't fully close to the ground
- Pole and stakes are extra
- The Serenity net tent wastes interior space in the hex shape

## Who Should Buy This

Three-season solo hikers who already carry trekking poles, are comfortable with a floorless shelter, and sleep in places where bugs are moderate will get the most from it. Thru-hikers in dry-to-moderate climates, and anyone moving up from a flat tarp, will appreciate the added coverage and easy pitching. Hikers in heavy mosquito or tick country, anyone who hates condensation management, and those who want a shelter that pitches the same way every time should look at a floored, netted trekking-pole tent such as Six Moon Designs' own Lunar Solo.

## Verdict

The Deschutes delivers a roomy, storm-capable solo roof for 370 g with minimal fuss and a modest price. It rewards hikers who accept the floorless trade-offs and have the pitching skills to match. For them, it is among the better values in the trekking-pole shelter category.

## Overview

The Chair Zero LT is Helinox's lightest backpacking chair: a DAC aluminum frame under a see-through monofilament seat, packing down to roughly the size of a Nalgene. It is built for hikers who have decided that sitting up off the ground is worth carrying a pound for. The trade-off is the usual one for this category: a very low seat, a $159.95 price, and a weight that makes it a luxury item.

## Performance

**Comfort.** The seat is a scooped sling that molds to the sitter, and reviewers keep returning to how much support it gives for something this thin. GearJunkie's tester, a 5'6" hiker with a sore lower back, could lean back and keep the lumbar area flush against the fabric without feeling the knees were being forced upward. CleverHiker found the seat slightly deeper and roomier than the original Chair Zero, and named the LT its top overall pick among backpacking chairs. REI buyers describe it as surprisingly comfortable for its packed size, with several saying the low, wide-footed stance makes it feel secure when leaning back after a long day.

Not everyone agrees. One of the most-upvoted critical REI reviews, from an owner of two other Helinox chairs, reports nearly tipping backward on first sitting and says the chair put uncomfortable pressure on the hips. Fit appears to matter. GearJunkie notes that hikers over six feet may feel cramped, with knees rising toward elbows, and Outdoor Gear Lab's tester, at 6'5" and 265 lb, flagged that taller and heavier users should think carefully before buying.

**Durability.** The frame is anodized DAC aluminum, the same family of poles used in premium tent construction, and the shock-corded sections snap together in under a minute. The seat is the bigger change from earlier Zero models. Helinox says its monofilament grid ripstop is tougher than the aramid-blend polyester on the original, and Section Hiker's reviewer described it as strong and abrasion-resistant. GearJunkie's tester spent $160 expecting longevity and reported the chair held up well, calling it tougher than it looks. The open-weave fabric also breathes, which Outdoor Gear Lab counts as one of the LT's distinguishing features. It is a better fit for a hot afternoon than for a cold evening at altitude.

**Stability.** No chair at this weight sits like a car-camping chair. The LT flexes if its occupant fidgets, and it sinks into sand, snow and soft mud, which is why Helinox sells a separate ground sheet for the Zero line. GearJunkie's tester said the chair felt secure even on rocky scree. The included X-strap stabilizer is a mixed bag. GearJunkie called it useless and its marketing a head-scratcher, while Outdoor Gear Lab lists the strap among the features that set the LT apart.

**Versus the original Chair Zero.** The two are hard to tell apart on the trail. CleverHiker said readers would have to squint to see the difference. The LT's seat sits lower, at 7 in, and Section Hiker's reviewer preferred the original Zero's higher seat, which makes standing up easier, especially with tired knees. That reviewer saw the LT's bundled accessory as its main advantage, given that the original plus the accessory costs about the same. Owners of the earlier chair, per GearJunkie, have little reason to upgrade just to save a few ounces.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 494 g with a 265 lb rating and a 5-year warranty
- Packs to 36 x 12 x 12 cm, small enough for a side pocket or pack interior
- Supportive scooped seat that testers found comfortable for hours of camp time
- Breathable, tough monofilament ripstop seat
- Quick tool-free setup with a shock-corded DAC frame

**Cons**

- At $159.95, among the priciest ways to sit down in the backcountry
- Low 7 in seat height makes getting up harder
- Taller hikers report cramped knees, and one critical REI owner reported hip pressure and a backward-tipping first sit
- Sinks into soft ground without a ground sheet
- The X-strap stabilizer gets little praise from reviewers

## Who Should Buy This

The LT suits hikers who spend long evenings in camp, anyone with a back or hips that object to sitting on the ground, and the kind of ultralight backpacker who weighs every item but keeps one comfort luxury. It is also a good fit for kayak and bikepacking trips where packed volume matters as much as weight. Hikers over six feet, those near the 265 lb rating, and anyone who struggles to stand from a low seat should try the chair in a store first or consider the original Chair Zero. Those who treat a foam sit pad as sufficient will find nothing here worth the weight or money.

## Verdict

The Chair Zero LT is the best-regarded do-it-all ultralight backpacking chair, with CleverHiker naming it a top pick and Outdoor Gear Lab calling it one of the best on the market. Its limits are a low seat, a price that stings and a fit that does not suit every body. Buy it if a 494 g chair sounds like a fair trade for real back support at camp, and test the original Chair Zero first if height or standing ease is a concern.

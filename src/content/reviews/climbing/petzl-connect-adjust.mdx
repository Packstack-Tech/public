## Overview

The Connect Adjust is Petzl's single-arm adjustable positioning lanyard: a length of 9 mm dynamic rope threaded through the company's Adjust rope adjuster, tied into the harness with a sewn HMPE loop. It replaces the daisy chain or sling at the anchor with something that can be dialed to any length between 15 and 95 cm. The trade-off is that it is a one-trick tether, and it is heavier and pricier than the slings it displaces.

## Performance

**Adjustability.** This is the reason to own one. A chain-style PAS offers fixed increments of a few inches. The Connect Adjust lets a climber sit at whatever height suits the task, whether that is hanging at a belay, cleaning a sport anchor or resting at a crux stance. Reviewers consistently describe it as quick to deploy, and one tester found it easy to operate even with gloves on. Pulling the rope to shorten the lanyard is the easy direction. Letting out slack is fiddlier, and one reviewer cautioned against lowering too abruptly. The adjuster has a hole for a cord that makes it easier to free the rope when it is weighted.

**Dynamic rope.** The 9 mm dynamic rope is meant to reduce the force transmitted to the climber in a short fall. That sets it apart from static slings. The lanyard is certified to EN 17520, the newer standard for this class of equipment.

**What changed in the current version.** The updated model has a redesigned clamp and a sewn HMPE loop for harness attachment. The loop girth-hitches to the belay loop with less bulk than before, and Petzl says it is easier to spot when managing the rope and gear at the anchor. Owners comparing it with the previous version say it is lighter and less bulky, and one called the HMPE loop a smart connection that doesn't give up durability. Petzl lists the weight at 120 g.

**Durability.** A long-term tester at Alpinist logged about 50 days on Petzl's adjustable lanyards. Beyond chalk and dirt ground into the rope, the aluminum hardware showed only a few scratches in its anodizing. Other owners report no meaningful rope fuzzing or adjuster degradation after months of regular use in dust and the odd rain shower.

**Limitations.** Reviewers raise four consistent points:

- **Rappelling.** It isn't suited to extended rappels. A reviewer at FeedTheHabit flagged this directly.
- **Redundancy.** It has a single arm and nothing to clip for equalizing. A climber on one bolt is dependent on that one bolt, unlike with a PAS where a second point can be clipped.
- **Carabiner compatibility.** European buyers say it works best with larger carabiners, and one said the rubber ring that keeps the biner oriented doesn't suit smaller ones. A pear-shaped locker is the safer pairing, and the lanyard ships without one.
- **Not for via ferrata.** One reviewer notes it is not a via ferrata lanyard and shouldn't be used as one, though it is popular for resting on such routes and for climbs where the leader wants a rest point.

**Weight and bulk.** At 120 g it is not the lightest way to tether to an anchor. A basic dynamic sling or a Dyneema runner is a fraction of that. One owner summed it up as not the lightest on the market, but it does the job. A reviewer at The Link also found it a bit space-consuming on the harness, particularly on a trad rack.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Continuous length adjustment from 15 to 95 cm, with no fixed increments
- Dynamic 9 mm rope reduces fall forces compared with static slings
- Slimmer HMPE girth-hitch loop than before
- Certified to EN 17520
- Holds up well to rock contact and grit
- Quick to learn, including for novices

**Cons**

- 120 g and bulkier than a sling or daisy-style PAS
- Single arm with no way to equalize or add a backup
- Poor for extended rappelling
- Works best with larger carabiners, and none is included
- Releasing slack takes some care
- About $70 for a single tether

## Who Should Buy This

This lanyard suits sport climbers, multi-pitch climbers and guides who spend real time hanging at anchors. It also suits anyone who wants an exact position while cleaning a route, rigging a rappel or managing a hanging belay. Alpine and trad climbers who already carry a full rack can justify the 120 g if hanging belays are routine. Weight-obsessed alpinists who tether only occasionally, and anyone whose rappel stations are mostly long descents, should stay with a Dyneema sling or buy the dual-arm Dual Connect Adjust. Via ferrata users need a certified via ferrata lanyard instead.

## Verdict

The Connect Adjust is the best-regarded adjustable anchor tether in its class, and the current version trims the bulk that bothered earlier owners. It does one job well and costs a premium for it. Buy it if precise positioning at belays and anchors matters to you, and skip it if every gram counts or you need a dual-point system.

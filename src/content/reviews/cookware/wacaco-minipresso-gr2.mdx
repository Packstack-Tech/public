## Overview

The Minipresso GR2 is Wacaco's latest hand-pumped espresso maker built for standard ground coffee (no pods required). It's aimed at coffee snobs who backpack, car camp, or travel and refuse to compromise on instant coffee — you provide hot water and elbow grease, it provides 18 bars of pressure and a legitimately crema-topped shot.

## Performance

The core appeal here is simple: you fill the 80 ml tank with hot water, load 8-12 g of finely ground coffee into the basket, screw it together, and pump the piston to force water through the grounds at up to 18 bar. <cite index="3-1,3-2">The Wacaco Minipresso GR2 is a portable handheld espresso machine capable of reaching 18-bar pressure, and it looks the part and makes a delicious coffee, too.</cite> That's the headline win — this isn't instant coffee dressed up, it's an actual extraction with real crema.

The catch, and it's a meaningful one, is grind size. <cite index="13-6">Store-bought ground coffee likely won't be fine enough, as it's usually ground for french press.</cite> If you don't own an espresso-capable grinder, you're stuck either buying pre-ground espresso roast or bringing a hand grinder along, which eats into the whole "grab and go" appeal for backpacking specifically. One reviewer who tested it noted a fiddly detail worth knowing before you're standing at a campsite confused: <cite index="13-8,13-9">the scoop's capacity is around 8g, so you'll need to spoon in a little extra if you calibrate the portafilter to hold 12g rather than the default 8g.</cite>

Pumping technique matters more than you'd expect. Reddit's r/Ultralight-adjacent coffee crowd generally agrees the GR2 and its siblings brew "authentic" espresso, but with caveats — <cite index="1-3,1-4">"However the Nanopresso and Minipresso brew authentic espresso," though on weekends some prefer a pour over or espresso using the Minipresso.</cite> One commenter who's used the line extensively summed up the learning curve: <cite index="1-11,1-12">it brews under pressure, and that takes a little bit of playing around to get it right — even down to the pressure packing in the espresso grounds.</cite> Expect your first two or three shots to be a bit of a mess while you dial in tamping pressure and pump cadence.

Tom's Guide's hands-on testing echoes this: getting a genuinely strong shot takes practice, and the pumping itself is a workout if you're making more than one cup. <cite index="2-6">Pros included sustainable materials, hand-powered operation, quick use, and not getting hot, while cons were that it's a bit of a forearm workout and tricky to get a strong coffee.</cite> That "doesn't get hot" point is a legitimate trail advantage — you can stash it in a pack pocket immediately after brewing without worrying about melting anything nearby.

Build quality gets consistent praise across the review sites I found. <cite index="12-3">One review described it as a compact manual espresso maker with solid build quality and surprisingly good shot extraction.</cite> Cleaning is straightforward too — <cite index="9-6">it's worth disassembling it into pieces and removing the rubber parts where possible after cleaning so it can dry better</cite>, which is a five-minute task, not a chore.

One quirk worth flagging from actual owners: assembly has a small trick to it. <cite index="11-9">Users note that when preparing it, you need to pull the cap properly and then everything goes great</cite> — skip that step and you'll fight the piston more than necessary.

For backpacking specifically, weigh the trade-offs honestly. At 363 g it's not nothing — that's roughly the weight of a full fuel canister plus a lightweight stove, dedicated to coffee alone. It's better suited to car camping, short trips, or as a "worth-it luxury" item for coffee-obsessed thru-hikers willing to carry the weight for a real espresso instead of instant.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Produces genuine espresso with crema, not just hot brown water
- 18-bar hand pump needs no batteries, fuel, or electricity
- Compact footprint that fits in a pack side pocket
- Doesn't retain heat, so it's safe to stow immediately after brewing
- Easy to disassemble and clean
- Made with biomass-based plastics rather than pure virgin plastic

**Cons**

- Requires a genuinely fine, espresso-grade grind — most store-bought grounds won't work well
- Pumping technique and tamp pressure take a few attempts to dial in
- At 363 g, it's heavier than a pack of instant coffee packets by a wide margin
- Physically tiring if you're brewing for more than one person
- Small 80 ml tank means one shot per fill, so multiple cups mean multiple refills and pump cycles

## Who Should Buy This

This is a good fit for the backpacker or car camper who already cares about coffee quality at home and isn't willing to downgrade to instant just because they're outdoors — and who's willing to carry a pre-ground fine espresso roast (or a compact hand grinder) to make it work. It's a poor fit for weight-conscious thru-hikers counting grams, or anyone who just wants a quick caffeine fix without fuss; a pour-over dripper or instant packets will get you there faster and lighter.

## Verdict

The Minipresso GR2 delivers on its core promise — real, pressure-extracted espresso from a device that fits in your hand — but it demands the right grind, some practice, and a genuine willingness to carry the weight and hassle for better coffee. It's not a universal backpacking recommendation, but for the coffee-first crowd, it earns its spot in the pack.

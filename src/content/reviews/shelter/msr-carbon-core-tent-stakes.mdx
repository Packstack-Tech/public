## Overview

The MSR Carbon-Core is a 6-inch stake built around a carbon fiber core, sheathed in aluminum, with an aluminum tip. At about 5.7 grams each, it is among the lightest stakes that still count as a real stake rather than a tent-peg-shaped suggestion. It's made for gram-counters pitching on firm ground. Its price and its limited grip in loose soil are the trade-offs.

## Performance

The stake is a thin rod, not a Y-beam or a V-profile, and that shapes everything about how it behaves. A slim round shaft slides into compacted dirt, frozen turf and gravelly tread with little resistance, and the aluminum tip takes the abuse that would chew up bare carbon. Testers at UK retailer Cairngorm Treks found the stakes did best in exactly that kind of ground. On a hard-frozen winter night in Deeside, the Carbon-Cores outperformed the other pegs on hand and went in with a light tap from the back of an ice axe. In firm ground they sometimes beat MSR's own Groundhog, which that team named as its favorite stake overall.

Durability is better than the spec sheet suggests. One long-distance hiker reported taking a set through the entire Pacific Crest Trail and the Colorado Trail, roughly 3,000 miles, without problems. Most were pushed in by palm, and the hardest ground got a few blows from a rock. That is the right way to treat them. A rock or a mallet is fine for a stubborn spot, but a stake this thin is not meant to be driven like a nail.

The weakness is holding power in soft ground. The same Cairngorm testers noted the stakes can work loose in softer soil and recommended a different peg there. Round, smooth shafts have little surface area to grip with, so loamy forest duff, sand and wet meadow are where a wind gust can pull one free. Hikers pitching trekking-pole shelters in those conditions typically bury a stake at an angle, or switch to a wider-profile stake at the windward corners.

The reviews are not uniformly glowing. Backcountry's customer rating sits around 3.5 out of 5 across 20 reviews, a middling score for a product its buyers generally purchase on reputation. A set at nearly $50 invites sharp scrutiny when a six-pack of Groundhogs costs well under that, and those lower scores tend to reflect value and soil-specific disappointment rather than failures in use.

Pitching and pulling are simple. The head is a small flat top, red on current kits, which makes the stakes easier to spot in duff and grass, a real concern when each one weighs less than a nickel. There's no hook or notch to speak of, so cordage typically goes around the shaft or through a loop.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 5.7 g per stake; four weigh 23 g total
- Easy to push into hard-packed and frozen ground
- Aluminum tip protects the carbon core from rocks and roots
- A PCT and Colorado Trail finisher reported no failures
- Made in the U.S.A.
- Bright top helps with finding the stakes at camp

**Cons**

- About $50 for four, far above aluminum alternatives
- Slim round shaft can loosen in soft soil, sand, and wet ground
- Not suited to hammering
- Only four per kit, so a typical shelter needs more than one set
- Limited availability at some retailers

## Who Should Buy This

Thru-hikers and fastpackers who pitch on firm trail-side ground and want the lightest viable stake will get the most from these, especially for trekking-pole tarps and single-wall shelters with four to six tie-outs. Anyone cutting a base weight below 10 pounds, and already past the easy savings, will find the gram counts add up: swapping a set of 10 g aluminum stakes for these saves roughly a quarter ounce per four stakes. Hikers who regularly camp on sand, snow, or soft forest soil should look at wider-profile stakes. Budget-minded hikers can find better value in basic aluminum.

## Verdict

The Carbon-Core earns its place in a kit built for firm ground and ruthless weight savings, and the evidence from long trail miles says it will last. It is expensive for what it is and is the wrong tool for soft soil, so most hikers will want a few wider stakes in the stuff sack for windward corners.

## Overview

The Smartwater 1L is a disposable PET bottle that thru-hikers adopted as real gear. It weighs 34 g, costs about as much as a gas-station candy bar, and its 28 mm threads screw directly onto a Sawyer Squeeze or Mini. The trade-off is built into the label: it is a single-use bottle, and it wears out faster than anything sold as a hiking bottle.

## Performance

**Weight.** At 34 g, this is about as light as a rigid 1L container gets. A Nalgene Ultralite 1L, already a lightweight option, weighs roughly 106 g, and a standard wide-mouth Nalgene is far heavier. Most hikers carry two bottles, so the savings double. Against a typical hard-sided reusable, that means carrying a few ounces less over the length of a long trail.

**Filter compatibility.** This is why the bottle became a trail institution. The 28 mm neck matches the threads on the Sawyer Squeeze and Mini, so the routine is simple: fill the bottle from a stream, screw the filter on, and squeeze clean water into a second container or straight into your mouth. Owners report that it also works with the Sawyer cleaning coupler, which the wider-mouth Smartbottle does not. The same 28 mm standard is why the bottle works with other inline filters.

**Durability.** The "single-use" label undersells it. Reviewers describe the bottles as surprisingly tough, and one Appalachian Trail thru-hiker carried a single bottle for three months before retiring it, and only because it got grimy. Gear reviewers say a bottle can last many years of hard use. The weak point is filtering. Squeezing through a hard-sided bottle means frequent burping to let air back in, and the vacuum that forms slowly distorts the sidewalls. Hikers who filter from the bottle daily replace it more often than those who use it only for clean water.

**Shape.** The bottle is taller and slimmer than most 1L containers. That helps it slide into side pockets and makes it easy to grab on the move. It also has a downside: hikers report losing bottles from side pockets during brushy off-trail travel, where vegetation can pull them out.

**Limitations.** The mouth is narrow, which makes the bottle a poor target for pouring or scooping from shallow sources. Gatorade-style bottles have a wider mouth that makes filling easier, but they are a few grams heavier and don't accept the filter. The bottle can't take boiling water, so it won't double as a hot-water bottle for a cold night. It also lacks the sidewall flats of a Smartbottle, so it rolls.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 34 g for a full liter of rigid capacity
- Threads directly onto Sawyer Squeeze and Mini filters
- Costs a couple of dollars and is sold at nearly every trailside store
- Holds up far longer than a disposable bottle should
- Recyclable, and easy to replace when it wears out

**Cons**

- Sidewalls deform with repeated filtering from the bottle
- Narrow mouth makes filling from shallow sources awkward
- Not safe for hot liquids
- Tall profile can snag and slip out of side pockets in brush
- Needs periodic replacement, and gets grimy over months of use

## Who Should Buy This

Anyone running a Sawyer Squeeze or Mini on a thru-hike or long section hike should carry at least one, and probably two. It also suits weight-conscious hikers who want a cheap, light container for carrying clean water. Hikers who need to pour boiling water, who want a bottle that lasts years without thought, or who prefer a wide mouth for scooping and mixing drink powder should look at a Smartbottle or a Nalgene instead, accepting the extra 1 oz or more.

## Verdict

The Smartwater 1L is the default trail bottle for good reasons: it is light, it is cheap, and it fits the most popular ultralight filter. Treat it as a consumable, replace it when the sidewalls collapse, and it will outperform bottles that cost ten times as much.

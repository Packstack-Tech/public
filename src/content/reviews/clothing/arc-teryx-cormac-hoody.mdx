## Overview

The Cormac Hoody is Arc'teryx's answer to the question of what a sun shirt looks like when it's built for people who sweat uphill. At 160 g, it's a thin, waffle-textured polyester hoody aimed at hiking, running and climbing rather than beach days. The trade-off: it's a high-output layer with a loose cut and few frills, and it costs more than most sun shirts.

## Performance

**Breathability and drying.** This is where the Cormac earns its reputation. CleverHiker rated it 4.6 out of 5 and named it its best technical sun shirt, noting that it manages one of the highest UPF ratings in its guide for the amount of airflow it delivers. Owners echo the point in almost identical terms: the fabric dries quickly, never feels clammy, and stays comfortable from humid 95-degree days to cool alpine mornings. One owner reported wearing it for fall and spring runs between 0 and 10°C, and under a shell in colder weather.

**Sun protection.** UPF 40+ falls in the Skin Cancer Foundation's "very good" band, and the coverage is practical as well as numerical. The hood has a brim and neck coverage and, according to owners, stays up in wind and shades the forehead for anyone going hatless. Thumb loops extend the sleeves over the backs of the hands. Hikers who've worn it with a cap in full midday sun describe staying cooler than in a T-shirt slathered with sunscreen.

**Fit and hood.** The cut is loose. Reviewers at Vertical Life and on retailer pages describe it as running large and flowy, with sleeves long enough to avoid riding up and a hem that stays put under a harness. Climbers report that the hood fits both over and under a helmet. The fabric has modest stretch compared with snugger sun hoodies, which suits a relaxed fit but not a second-skin one. Those between sizes, or who want a trimmer fit, should size down.

**Cooling in still air.** The same dry-feeling fabric that wins praise for moisture management doesn't give much of an evaporative chill. The Vertical Life tester found it more comfortable in hot, windy conditions than hot, still ones, which is worth knowing before wearing it up a sheltered, sun-baked climb.

**Durability.** Here the reports split. The Vertical Life reviewer called the material itself durable for its weight, but found the flatlock seams leave threads exposed on the outside, where they catch on rough rock. The cautionary example given was granite offwidths. REI buyers also flagged fabric snags: the hoody's 20 REI reviews average a middling 3.2 out of 5, with complaints about snagging, fit and warmth. The Arc'teryx reply on that page says the Cormac is meant as a lightweight, quick-drying, high-output hoody, not a warm one. One owner who bought a newer-generation hoody for his wife also found the lighter fabric easier to see through than earlier versions and wondered about its longevity. Another buyer of the current version said it wasn't see-through at all, so it may depend on color.

**Odor.** CleverHiker credits it with solid odor resistance. A buyer of the hoody still noted that it gets smelly in the way all polyester does. Treat it as better than a cheap synthetic tee, not as a merino substitute.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 160 g with UPF 40+ and a full hood
- Excellent wicking and quick drying, praised by runners, hikers and climbers
- Brimmed hood that works with or without a helmet
- Thumb loops and long sleeves cover the backs of the hands
- Clean design with no seam-heavy extras to rub under a pack

**Cons**

- Exposed flatlock threads snag on rock and rough surfaces
- Roomy fit that runs large; stretch is modest
- Dry-feeling fabric gives little cooling in still, hot air
- No pocket, quarter zip or vent, so no way to dump heat quickly
- Priced around $90 in CleverHiker's test, well above basic sun shirts; REI buyer ratings are mixed

## Who Should Buy This

Hikers, trail runners and climbers who put in hard hours in the sun and want one top that goes from sweaty ascent to windy ridge without feeling soaked. It suits day hikers and thru-hikers in dry, exposed terrain, particularly anyone who wears a helmet or doesn't like a hat. Skip it if your trips involve a lot of bushwhacking, chimney climbing or scrambling on abrasive rock, where the seams and knit will snag. Casual hikers who want sun cover for mild days can get more comfort per dollar from a cheaper sun shirt, as CleverHiker points out. Anyone who wants a close, stretchy fit should try a size down or look at a different cut.

## Verdict

The Cormac Hoody is among the best-breathing sun hoodies you can wear for strenuous days. It combines low weight, solid UPF and a well-designed hood. The loose fit, snag-prone seams and price keep it from being a universal pick, but for high-output hiking and running in open country, it's easy to recommend.

## Overview

The 200 Oasis Leggings with Fly are Icebreaker's best-selling men's base layer bottoms: 200 g/m² merino jersey, a slim cut and a contoured pouch with a working fly. They suit cold-weather hiking, shoulder-season sleep layers and ski days, and they hold up well to multi-day wear. The trade-off is price and fabric longevity. A thin, soft merino jersey costs more per ounce than synthetic long johns and wears out faster.

## Performance

**Warmth.** At 200 g/m², the Oasis sits in the middle of Icebreaker's range, below the 260 Tech Thermal. Owners describe it as warm without being heavy. One REI reviewer called the leggings thin at first but said they nearly overheated once the wearer stepped outside. Another wears them alone under shorts in temperatures in the 30s °F, while noting that their lower body runs warm. A reviewer on Icebreaker's site used them as a mid layer for cross-country skiing at around -20°C, and another wore them for freezing nights in the mountains of Kyrgyzstan. A buyer who wanted more insulation for very cold weather suggested the heavier 260 for that job. The 200 is a three-season layer that goes under shell pants in winter.

**Odor and multi-day wear.** This is merino's main argument. One REI reviewer reported little to no smell after four days of wear. For hikers who count on wearing one pair of long johns for a week, that matters more than any spec on the tag. Reviewers also use them as camp lounge wear and as a base under shorts on cold mornings.

**Fit and mobility.** The cut is slim and sized true, according to buyers. A 5'7" wearer with a 33–34 inch waist found the medium right. A 6'1", 195-lb ski patroller found the large right. The gusset and flatlock seams let owners pedal, stretch and walk with no restriction. Because there is no center-back seam, the leggings lie flat under pants or a pack hip belt. The brushed elastic waistband is not bulky, and women's-model buyers say the same about the equivalent waistband.

**The fly.** The fly with its contoured pouch is the feature that separates this garment from most women's-cut or unisex leggings. In the field, it means a cold-morning pit stop without dropping the whole base layer. That is a practical gain below freezing.

**Wind and moisture.** The fabric is thin enough that wind resistance is decent but not complete. One REI reviewer called the wind resistance solid for such a sheer fabric. It breathes well enough for ski patrol shifts and aerobic use, which is where merino jersey does well against fleece.

**Durability.** This is the weak point. Jersey knit merino at this weight can abrade and open up. One REI reviewer gave the leggings a sharply negative review after wearing them twice under waterproof nylon pants while hiking. A European buyer of the women's version reported large holes despite correct washing and drying. Most owners report no problems. Still, hikers who wear a base layer under rough rain pants or brush-heavy trails should expect more wear than from a heavier or nylon-blended fabric.

## Choosing a Version

Two weights are listed: 199 g for the Standard version and 220 g for the Men's version. Icebreaker's own spec sheet gives 199 g (7.01 oz) for a size medium, and retailers list a figure of 217 g (7.65 oz) for the same men's style. The difference is about 21 g (0.7 oz), less than a pair of socks. Both versions have the same 200 g/m² merino, fly, gusset and flatlock construction. Pick by size and fit rather than by weight.

- **Standard (199 g):** The lighter listing. It suits hikers who count ounces and want the lowest pack weight in a men's merino long john.
- **Men's (220 g):** The slightly heavier listing. The extra weight buys no change in fabric or features, so it is the better pick only if it matches the size and fit you need.

On size, the slim cut means hikers between sizes should go up if they plan to layer pants over the leggings or sleep in them. Owners say the chart is accurate, and one buyer advised trying on a pair in a store where possible. The inseam on a size L is 78.1 cm.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Roughly 7 oz for a full-length merino bottom
- Odor resistance holds for multi-day trips
- Functioning fly with contoured pouch
- Flatlock seams, gusset and no center-back seam reduce chafing and bulk
- Fit runs true and moves freely
- Works from cool shoulder-season hikes to cold ski days

**Cons**

- $120 list price is high for a layer that can wear through
- Thin jersey can abrade under rough or waterproof outer pants
- Not warm enough alone for very cold weather; the 260 weight is the step up
- Slim cut leaves little room for bulky layering
- Marketed mainly for snow sports, with no hiking-specific features

## Who Should Buy This

Thru-hikers and winter backpackers who want one pair of long johns that works as a sleep layer, camp layer and cold-morning hiking base should buy this. The fly and odor resistance make it practical for trips of a week or more. Hikers who want one all-purpose layer for temperatures from the 20s to 40s °F will find it a good fit when paired with a wind layer.

Hikers who bush-whack, wear abrasive rain pants against bare merino, or want a long-term investment should look at a heavier or blended fabric. Anyone heading into deep cold should step up to the 260 weight.

## Verdict

The Oasis Leggings with Fly deliver on what merino promises: low weight, no smell, a snug fit that moves and a fly that works. Their weakness is a thin fabric that costs a lot and may not last through rough use. Buy them on sale for a cold-weather kit, and treat them as a layer to protect rather than abuse.

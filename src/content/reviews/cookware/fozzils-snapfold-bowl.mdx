## Overview
The Snapfold Bowl, sold under the Fozzils name and now listed by Litesmith as "formerly Fozzils," is a sheet of molded polypropylene that folds and snaps into a 600 mL bowl, then unsnaps into a flat 9 x 10 inch panel. It suits ultralight backpackers who want dedicated dinnerware that takes up almost no room in the pack. The trade-off is the closure system: four plastic snaps do the work a rigid wall would, and they can be fussy.

## Performance
The pitch is packability, and reviewers agree it delivers. Flat, the bowl is a thin square of plastic that slides against a pack wall or into a bear canister or Ursack. One Backpacking Light user described unsnapping the bowls and sliding them into a bear canister as a good way to cut cook-kit bulk. A New Zealand tramping magazine reviewer who used the bowl for a year said it had yet to let him down. He also said it once rescued him after he thought he had forgotten a bowl, because it was packed flat in his reservoir sleeve.

Boiling water is within spec. The 212°F rating means a rehydrating dinner or a pour of hot water over oatmeal is fine. Testers at one backpacking-bowl roundup noted the walls get warm to the touch with boiling contents, so a bandana or a patient wait helps. The molded measuring lines mean the bowl doubles as a gauge for dehydrated-meal water, which lets some hikers drop a separate measuring cup.

Cleanup is the other selling point. Food tends to collect in the creases while the bowl is assembled, but unsnapping it leaves a flat surface that can be wiped, rinsed or, as several reviewers admit, licked clean. It is dishwasher safe at home. Flat, it also works as a prep surface. Reviewers use it as a cutting board or plate, though one noted hesitation about taking a razor-sharp knife to it. Handy tricks reported by owners include using it as a dog bowl and wearing it half-snapped as a rain visor.

The snaps are the weak point. Reviewers describe a minor learning curve, and one group could not get the snaps to stay closed on a trip. The same reviewer later found the snaps held reliably once he had the folding technique down. He still hesitated to trust them with hot coffee or tea. In cold conditions the plastic stiffens and puts more pressure on the snaps. Fozzils' own guidance is to squeeze the folds firmly, especially near the base, while assembling. Backpacking Light posters add that the snaps can wear out after years of repeated folding and that metal utensils scuff the plastic over time. For alpine or sub-freezing trips, the High Route's reviewer suggested looking at alternatives.

The bowl is also not a rigid vessel. It is not something to eat from one-handed while walking, and a bowl that comes unsnapped is just a piece of plastic. Several Backpacking Light posters say they would accept the extra weight of a Sea to Summit X-Bowl for that reason. Others say the fold-flat tabs of the older Orikaso design, which the Fozzils bowls are based on, were more durable than snaps.

## Pros & Cons
**Pros**
- Packs to a flat 9 x 10 in sheet that disappears in a pack or canister
- 40 g per bowl, with a two-pack at 80 g total
- Handles boiling water (212°F) for rehydrated meals
- Measuring lines double as a water gauge
- Cleans quickly when flattened; dishwasher safe
- Doubles as a cutting board or plate
- Inexpensive, at roughly $16 for the two-pack at some retailers

**Cons**
- Snap closures have a learning curve and can be finicky
- Plastic stiffens in the cold, making the snaps harder to close and keep closed
- Snaps and plastic can wear after years of heavy use, especially with metal utensils
- Flexible walls make it a poor choice for one-handed eating on the move
- Walls get warm with boiling contents
- Food collects in the creases until the bowl is unsnapped

## Who Should Buy This
Three-season backpackers and thru-hikers who want a real bowl without the bulk are the target, especially those carrying a bear canister where every cubic inch counts. The two-pack suits couples or partners who split meals. It also suits anyone who likes a clean prep surface or measuring lines on trail. Winter campers, alpine climbers, and hikers who eat hot soup or sip coffee from their bowl while moving should look elsewhere. A rigid bowl or a mug-style vessel is the safer choice for them.

## Verdict
The Snapfold Bowl is a cheap, clever way to carry dinnerware that takes up almost no space. It earns its place in warm-weather cook kits as long as the user learns the snaps. Anyone who needs reliability in the cold, or a bowl that holds its shape, should pay the weight penalty for something rigid.

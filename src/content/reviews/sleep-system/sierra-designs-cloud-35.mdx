## Overview

The Sierra Designs Cloud 35 is a zipperless, 800-fill DriDown sleeping bag that borrows from both quilts and mummies: a hood and draft-blocking sides on one hand, a blanket-style comforter you flip open to vent on the other. At 652 g in the regular size, it targets three-season backpackers who want quilt-like freedom without quilt-like drafts. The trade-off is a design that depends on a sleeping pad and rewards back sleepers more than side sleepers.

## Performance

**Warmth.** The EN limit rating of 26°F is the honest number for a cold sleeper in a pad-and-base-layer setup, and testers have generally treated the 35°F label as a fair three-season figure. Andrew Skurka found the bag amply warm over eight consecutive July nights. A GearJunkie tester stayed comfortable through two nights with temperatures dipping below 40°F in the Rockies, including rain. A hunter on the Rokslide forum reported getting chilly near 24°F on a pad with little insulation, a reminder that the bag's rating leans on the pad beneath it.

**The pad-dependent design.** From the waist to the shoulders, the Cloud has no down on its underside. Instead, a sleeve holds the sleeping pad in place, and the pad provides the insulation a conventional bag would carry in its bottom panel. That saves weight, and it sidesteps the physics of compressed down, which insulates poorly under body weight. It also makes pad choice part of the sleep system. The sleeve is half-length, so there is nothing anchoring the foot of the pad. Wilderness Magazine's tester found the pad could creep out of the sleeve on sloping ground. Others have noted that in cooler weather the sleeve can tug the bag away from the body and create cold spots. A GearJunkie tester also found the hood sat awkwardly around the head with a pad inserted.

**Venting and the foot vent.** This is where the design earns its keep. The crescent-shaped comforter folds down like a bedspread, and the Gear Institute noted it opens roughly three-quarters of the way down the bag, so there is no zipper to wrestle in the dark. The self-sealing foot vent lets feet poke out on warm nights, and owners say it closes up again without leaking heat once feet go back in. The comforter hooks into an insulated shoulder pocket that keeps it wrapped around the sleeper.

**Room and sleeping position.** At 152 cm in the shoulders and 147 cm at the hips, the regular is spacious for a 35°F bag. Reviewers describe it as closer to a bed at home than to a mummy. Restless sleepers and people who sleep with arms out tend to like it. The weak spot is side sleeping. Skurka's one criticism was that when he rolled onto his side, his face ended up pointing at the bag's edge rather than the bag turning with him. SectionHiker's verdict was that the bag suits most people unless they are side sleepers or thrashers, and it scored the bag 3.8 out of 5.

**Packability and durability.** The 13 x 7 in stuff sack is roughly Nalgene-sized, and a reviewer carrying a 45-liter pack for an overnight climbing trip fit a rope and rack around it. The 15D shell is thin, but users report it holds up better than its weight suggests. The DriDown treatment adds some resistance to damp, which counts for something on a rainy night when the pad sleeve and comforter overlap are the places condensation gathers.

## Choosing a Version

The Cloud 35 comes in two sizes. The Regular fits up to 6'0" and weighs 652 g. The Long fits up to 6'6" and weighs 709 g, a 57 g penalty that buys the extra length. Taller sleepers should not squeeze into the Regular: a short bag leaves the foot vent and comforter geometry working against the sleeper. Anyone 6'0" or under should take the Regular and keep the weight savings.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 652 g for a bag with a hood, a full comforter, and a foot vent
- Zipperless design vents easily and cuts down on snags and noise
- Roomy cut suits restless sleepers and arms-out sleepers
- PFC-free DriDown at 800 fill packs to roughly a Nalgene's footprint
- Pad sleeve keeps the sleeper from rolling off the pad

**Cons**

- No down under the torso, so warmth depends on the sleeping pad's R-value
- Side sleeping is awkward, since the bag does not rotate with the body
- Half-length pad sleeve does not anchor the pad's foot end on slopes
- Hood can sit awkwardly over a pad's top edge
- 26°F limit rating means it is a mild-weather bag, not a shoulder-season one

## Who Should Buy This

The Cloud 35 suits back sleepers and active sleepers who run warm, hike in summer and early fall, and already carry a decent pad. It is also an easy upgrade for quilt users who are tired of drafts at the shoulders. Side sleepers, cold sleepers, and anyone who prefers a pad-independent bag with full underside insulation should look elsewhere. So should anyone who expects to see regular frost.

## Verdict

The Cloud 35 delivers on its promise of quilt-style ventilation with mummy-style draft protection, at a weight that competes with dedicated ultralight bags. Its pad-dependent design and side-sleeping quirks keep it from being a universal pick, but for back sleepers on warm-weather trips, it is a comfortable, light, and easy-to-live-with bag.

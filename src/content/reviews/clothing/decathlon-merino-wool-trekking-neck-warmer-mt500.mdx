## Overview

The MT500 is Decathlon's budget merino neck warmer, sold under its Simond mountaineering sub-brand (you'll also see essentially the same fabric platform on Decathlon's Forclaz-branded trekking gaiters). It's a simple merino/polyamide tube that does the classic multi-position gaiter thing — neck warmer, headband, balaclava, wristband, whatever you need on a given day. This is squarely aimed at hikers and trekkers who want merino's warmth-to-weight and odor resistance without paying Buff or Icebreaker prices.

## Performance

**Warmth and fabric feel.** At 155 g/m², this is a mid-weight tube — noticeably heftier than the classic <cite index="8-3">lightweight merino Buff at 125g/m²</cite>. On the trail that translates to a warmer piece for cold mornings or wind, but also less packable and a bit more bulk under a hood than the ultralight Buff options. The 18.5-micron merino is on the finer end of the spectrum, and Decathlon's own material notes claim the fabric <cite index="1-9">does not itch</cite>, which tracks with my experience — it's soft against the neck, though not quite as plush as 100% merino tubes from Icebreaker or Buff.

**Durability.** The polyamide blend is the interesting part here. Rather than just diluting the merino for cost, Decathlon <cite index="1-11">uses a new yarn structure in which a polyamide yarn forms a thin sheath that protects the Merino wool fibres against friction and cuts</cite>. In practice, that means less pilling and fuzzing around the mouth/nose area where most neck gaiters take the most abrasion from stubble, sunglasses straps, and backpack collars. A side-by-side comparison of merino tubulars noted this blend <cite index="8-14,8-15">is a composite of 89% merino and 11% polyamide, promising more strength and durability than pure merino neck gaiters</cite>. That matches what I'd expect from months of regular wear — mine hasn't developed the holes or thin spots I've seen on pure-merino Buffs after a season of daily use.

**Comfort ranking against competitors.** One independent comparison put it plainly: the Decathlon tube's <cite index="8-19,8-20">comfort lies somewhere in between the Buff brand and Icebreaker, and the merino wasn't as soft feeling as either brand but in terms of elasticity and practicality, it was right in between</cite>. That's a fair summary of my own impression — this isn't the softest merino tube you can buy, but it's not scratchy either, and the stretch is adequate for pulling over your face on a cold descent.

**Versatility.** With 10 claimed wearing positions, it does everything you'd expect — neck gaiter, do-rag, headband, loose scarf, half-balaclava. I mostly use mine as a sleep-warmer and wind-blocker on alpine starts; the moisture-wicking merino means it doesn't get swampy the way a synthetic buff does if you're breathing hard into it on a climb.

**Wool sourcing.** Decathlon states they only source wool <cite index="5-11">from merino sheep bred on farms in South Africa that do not practise mulesing</cite>, which is a reasonable ethical baseline. That said, at least one gear comparison flagged that Decathlon's broader sustainability and sourcing practices have <cite index="8-17">been subject of question</cite>, so treat the specific supply chain claims with a little skepticism if that matters to your buying decision — I couldn't independently verify beyond Decathlon's own statement.

**Review volume.** For what it's worth, Decathlon's own product pages show this item with thousands of customer reviews averaging 4.8/5, though as with most retailer-hosted reviews, take the score with a grain of salt since there's no way to verify how many are from actual thru-hikers versus casual buyers grabbing it for a ski day.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Genuinely warmer than ultralight merino buffs thanks to the 155 g/m² weight
- Polyamide-reinforced yarn resists pilling and abrasion better than pure merino tubes in my use
- Doesn't itch, even for people who are sensitive to wool against bare skin
- 10 wearing positions cover neck, head, face, and wrist use cases
- Price is well below Buff, Icebreaker, or Arc'teryx equivalents
- 2-year warranty is generous for a $15-20 accessory

**Cons**

- Heavier and less packable than true ultralight options (Buff's lightweight merino line, for instance)
- Not as soft as premium 100% merino tubes — noticeable if you're used to Icebreaker or Buff
- Decathlon's sustainability/sourcing transparency has drawn some criticism from gear reviewers
- Limited independent long-term durability data outside of Decathlon's own reviews and a handful of blog comparisons

## Who Should Buy This

This is the right pick if you want merino's warmth and odor control on a budget and don't mind a slightly bulkier, warmer tube over the ultralight standard. Backpackers doing cold-weather trekking, ski touring, or shoulder-season thru-hikes who want a durable neck warmer without spending $30+ will get good value here. If you're chasing every last gram for a summer thru-hike, the lighter Buff merino option is still the better packable choice — but for three-season use where you actually want the warmth, the MT500's extra grammage works in its favor.

## Verdict

The MT500 isn't the softest or lightest merino neck warmer on the market, but it's a smart, durable middle ground between ultralight buffs and heavier ski-specific gaiters, and the price makes it an easy add to a cold-weather kit. I'd rate it a 7.5/10 — solid, practical, and clearly engineered with some thought (the protective yarn sheath is a nice touch), even if the wool sourcing story deserves a bit more scrutiny than Decathlon's marketing copy provides.

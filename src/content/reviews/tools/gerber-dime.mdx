## Overview

The Gerber Dime is a keychain-sized folding multi-tool that squeezes 12 functions into a 7 cm closed frame weighing just 62g. It's primarily aimed at everyday carry, but it has earned a genuine following among backpackers who want a capable backup tool without the bulk of a full-size multi-tool. At a street price hovering around $15–25, it's one of the most affordable options in the micro multi-tool category.

## Performance

### Tool Access and Layout

One of the Dime's most practical design decisions is that most tools deploy without unfolding the handles. <cite index="17-28,17-29">Unlike the Leatherman Micra, most of the tools can be accessed without unfolding the handles — there's a fine-edge knife blade and package opener on the left, and spring-loaded scissors, medium flat driver, crosshead driver, and file on the right.</cite> That's a real convenience advantage on the trail, where you want to get in and out quickly.

<cite index="3-33,3-34">
  Unfolding the Dime's handles turns it into a pair of spring-loaded pliers with
  a built-in wire cutter, and the rounded edges of the handle keep the pliers
  comfortable in your hand.
</cite>

### The Blade

<cite index="10-1,10-2">
  The blade cuts well, and its bull-nosed profile makes it a bit more
  security-friendly. It's made from 3Cr13 stainless steel, which is a decent
  compromise between taking and holding a good edge and corrosion resistance.
</cite>
That said, 3Cr13 is entry-level stainless — it touches up quickly on a ceramic
rod or a strop, but don't expect a Benchmade edge life.
<cite index="1-33">
  The blade is decent, but it isn't the sophisticated piece of metallurgy you'll
  find on a dedicated, high-quality pocket knife.
</cite>
For trail tasks — cutting cord, slicing cheese, trimming moleskin — it's more
than adequate between sharpenings. Note that the blade does not lock open, which
is worth being aware of during heavier cutting work.

### The Package Opener

This is where the Dime genuinely earns its keep as an EDC tool. <cite index="3-27,3-28">There's a dedicated blade for slicing through plastic packaging and tape, so you don't get your regular knife blade all sticky with tape residue.</cite> It's a small thing, but it's the kind of thoughtful design decision that makes you reach for the Dime first. On trail it doubles as a handy box cutter at resupply.

### The Bottle Opener

<cite index="2-3,2-4">
  The always-accessible bottle opener is one of the standout features of the
  Dime — you don't have to open the tool to access it; you can just pull it out
  of your pocket and put it to use.
</cite>
<cite index="2-6,2-7">
  The one gripe is that the bottle opener sits on the same end as the lanyard
  ring, which gets in the way a bit.
</cite>

### The Pliers

Here's where honesty matters. <cite index="1-27">The tiny Dime pliers are flimsy enough that one could conceivably break or bend them with average strength and use.</cite> <cite index="1-29,1-30">The Dime is strong enough for minor to moderate household and mechanical use — just don't expect the pliers to crack frozen bolts or bend thick gauge wire.</cite> Multiple users on forums have reported failures under load. <cite index="13-15,13-16">One BladeForums user reported getting too rough with the pliers and having them collapse, noting they are "just too delicate."</cite> For trail use — adjusting trekking pole clamps, bending a fishhook, picking up a hot pot bail — they hold up fine. Just don't treat them like Leatherman Surge pliers.

### Scissors and Screwdrivers

<cite index="15-18,15-19">
  The scissors are quite good and have been reported to cut everything from
  paper and strings to zip ties, tape, small wires, and 550 cord.
</cite>
However,
<cite index="15-21,15-22,15-23">
  at least one long-term user found that the scissors spring broke after
  extended carry, with a small crack near its base — though Gerber honored the
  warranty and offered a repair.
</cite>
The flat and Phillips drivers work for light fastener work, but
<cite index="1-32">
  torquing hard on the screwdrivers is a risk not worth taking.
</cite>

### Trail Credibility

A Backpacking Light forum reviewer who swapped the Dime in for a heavier multi-tool captures the backpacker use case well: <cite index="11-3">"The Dime has the main functions that I look for in a tool: a blade that works well for cutting cheese for lunch, a pliers for fixing zippers and other light repairs, a scissors for small trims, a screwdriver for tightening trekking poles, a small file for hangnails, and a tweezers for ticks and splinters."</cite> That about sums it up. <cite index="11-5">Though it is certainly only for light duty, it has been found sufficiently durable and holds up well for what you need it to do on trail.</cite>

### Durability and Finish

<cite index="17-11">
  The paint finish wears off around the edges from contact with keys, giving it
  a used look fairly quickly
</cite>
— this is cosmetic, not functional, but worth noting if you care about
aesthetics. The black anodized version (reviewed here) is particularly prone to
showing wear.
<cite index="1-19">The Gerber is assembled with serviceable Torx bolts</cite>,
which means you can actually tighten things if they work loose over time.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Exceptional value at ~$15–25
- 62g / 7 cm closed — genuinely pocketable and keychain-friendly
- Most tools deploy without unfolding the handles
- Dedicated package opener is a standout feature not found on most competitors
- External bottle opener is always ready
- Spring-loaded pliers with wire cutter covers most trail repair needs
- Limited lifetime warranty backed by Gerber customer service
- Scissors are sharp and practical for cord/zip tie work

**Cons**

- Pliers are the weak link — not rated for heavy torque or bending tasks
- Blade does not lock open
- 3Cr13 blade steel won't hold an edge as long as higher-end steels like 154CM or S30V
- Colored scales show cosmetic wear quickly on a keychain
- Lanyard ring placement makes the bottle opener slightly awkward to use
- <cite index="12-40">The lanyard ring is arguably not a real tool</cite>, so
  the "12-tool" count is a stretch depending on your definition
- Scissor spring has broken for some users over extended carry

## Who Should Buy This

The Dime is best suited to backpackers and hikers who want a true backup multi-tool — something to handle zipper repairs, trekking pole adjustments, splinter removal, and food prep — without dedicating meaningful pack weight to the task. <cite index="12-26">You probably don't want to rely on it completely for a six-day trip through the backcountry, but it's capable enough for shorter day hikes, fishing trips, or tossing in a kayak's storage compartment.</cite> It's also a natural fit for anyone who wants capable keychain EDC at a budget price point. If you need to do serious mechanical work, step up to a Leatherman Squirt PS4 or a full-size tool.

## Verdict

<cite index="1-4,1-5,1-6">
  The Dime is tiny, inexpensive, and beats its close competition in most ways —
  and while comparing it to full-size multi-tools is unfair and inappropriate,
  when stacked up next to similar-sized offerings, the Dime is a solid
  performer.
</cite>
The pliers durability and budget blade steel are real limitations you should go
in knowing about, but at this price and weight, the Dime represents about as
much trail utility as you can carry on a keychain. I'd rate it a **7/10** — buy
it knowing what it is and it'll earn its place on every trip.

## Overview

Band Joes are flat, one-inch-wide silicone loops from Grifiti, a small Pennsylvania accessory maker better known for wrist rests. They do the job of a rubber band, a bungee and a length of shock cord in a single, nearly weightless piece. The trade-off: they are a bundling tool, not a load-bearing strap, and the price per band runs well above a bag of office rubber bands.

## Performance

The case for silicone over natural rubber is longevity. Latex bands dry out, crack and snap, often in the bottom of a pack where no one notices until a bundle comes apart. Grifiti says its bands do not desiccate with age, and owner reports back this up: one buyer reported a single band breaking in roughly three years of use, and long-time customers describe more than a decade with none failing. Those are retail-page testimonials rather than lab data, but the pattern is consistent across sizes.

Grip is the second selling point. A one-inch flat profile spreads pressure across a stuff sack or a bundle of poles instead of biting in like a thin cord, and the silicone surface holds on slick nylon and plastic. Owners use them to cinch lids on food containers, keep game boxes closed, bundle cables and hoses, and hold plastic bins together for transport. For backpackers, Grifiti's own suggestions point at the obvious jobs: pairing trekking poles, lashing a water bottle or tent pole bundle, and keeping cooking utensils together.

The 18-inch XL is the version for bigger loads. With a 36-inch circumference and 3x stretch, it can encircle a trash bin or a stack of file boxes, which is the manufacturer's stated use. On the trail, that translates to a sleeping pad cinched around a pack, a tent body strapped to the outside, or a foam sit pad held flat. It is the most useful of the Band Joes for anyone carrying bulky gear outside the pack. Stretch is generous, but the band is a compression and retention tool. Anything heavy or likely to shift under load still wants a buckled strap.

Temperature tolerance is a quiet advantage. The cooking-grade silicone shrugs off hot and cold, so a band can sit around a stove pot or a hot mug without softening the way cheap rubber does, and sun exposure on the outside of a pack is not a concern. Owners also note the bands wipe clean, which matters after a trip that involved food containers.

The weakness is thickness. These are flat, fairly thin bands, and heavy cinching over sharp edges or abrasive rock is a better way to learn about silicone tears than any test. A reviewer for the larger 36-inch version is also quoted as using them on a boat, which gives a sense of how far the line extends, but nobody is hanging a bear bag from one.

## Choosing a Version

**Large (12 x 1 inch, 10-pack).** The everyday size. The 12-inch band stretches to roughly 24 inches, which covers a stuff-sack mouth, a pair of poles, a fuel canister bundle, a cook-pot lid or a rolled map. The 10-pack, listed at 15 g, makes it the value pick, and it is the one to buy first if the goal is replacing a drawer of rubber bands.

**XL (18 x 1 inch).** Sold as a two-pack and listed at 30 g. The 36-inch circumference and 3x stretch handle a rolled sleeping pad, a bundled tent, a camp chair or a large bin. Fewer bands for the money, but there is nothing else in the Band Joes line at this size that behaves as well on bulky loads. Buy it if gear regularly rides on the outside of a pack.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Silicone does not dry out or crack like latex, and owners report years of use
- Wide one-inch profile grips without cutting in
- Heat, cold, UV and chemical resistant
- Weight is negligible in either version
- Wipes clean; usable around food and stoves
- Two sizes cover everything from stuff sacks to sleeping pads

**Cons**

- Costs far more than ordinary rubber bands
- Not a substitute for a buckled strap on heavy or shifting loads
- Thin flat profile can tear if cinched over sharp edges
- XL ships only as a two-pack

## Who Should Buy This

Gram-counting hikers who bundle poles, stakes, pads and food containers, and who are tired of snapped rubber bands and the weight of mini-bungees, will find the Large 10-pack an easy addition. The XL suits those strapping a pad or tent to the outside of a frameless pack. Anyone who needs real load retention, such as a heavy bag on a bike rack or a canoe, should buy cam straps instead. Occasional day hikers who only need something to hold a wrapped map can get by with a hair tie.

## Verdict

Band Joes are a small, durable fix for a small, constant annoyance, and the silicone earns its premium by outlasting every rubber band it replaces. Start with the Large 10-pack, add the XL two-pack if gear rides on the outside of a pack, and keep real straps for anything heavy.

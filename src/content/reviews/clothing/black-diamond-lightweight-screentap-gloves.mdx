## Overview

The Lightweight Screentap is Black Diamond's thinnest touchscreen glove: a stretch-fleece liner with conductive U|R Powered material across the palm and fingers. It suits three-season hikers, trail runners and anyone who wants to answer a phone or check a GPS app without baring their hands. The trade-off is built into the weight. At 38.4 g a pair, it is a liner, and it should be bought as one.

## Performance

**Touchscreen.** This is the glove's selling point, and owners mostly say it delivers. One reviewer on Black Diamond's site rated the touchscreen response better than other gloves they had used. A Backcountry buyer said they could type and navigate their phone almost as if bare-handed. Another owner reported tapping a phone on a chairlift without trouble. The dissent comes from an REI reviewer who owns Outdoor Research gear and was disappointed. In their experience, the conductive fabric sits only on the palm side of the fingers. Straight-on taps register, but taps with the side of the finger don't. Anyone who thumbs their screen at odd angles should expect a short adjustment period. Typing long messages is still better handled by voice.

**Warmth.** The glove is thin. A long-term tester found it insufficient for prolonged exposure below 40°F without a chill. Retailers can't agree on a range: REI lists 30–45°F and another retailer lists 40–55°F. The lower, more conservative figure matches the owner reports better. A runner in the southeast wore them on 30–40°F days and called them a favorite. Still, that is moving-and-sweating weather, not a windy ridge at dawn. Owners describe them as good for staving off chilly wind in light conditions. The DWR finish sheds a drizzle but is not waterproofing, and wet fleece of this thickness will not keep much heat.

**As a liner.** This is where the glove works best. Reviewers repeatedly praise the low bulk: it slides under ski gloves, belay gloves and mitts without bunching. One owner reported using them on Denali as part of a layered glove system. Another wears them under mittens on cold mountain mornings and alone when there's only a slight chill. For anyone carrying a shell mitt as the cold-weather backstop, a 38 g liner that can still run a phone is an efficient addition.

**Fit and durability.** The stretch fabric earns praise for dexterity. One owner who normally wears a large went down to a medium, reasoning that a tighter liner stays put better when the outer glove keeps coming off. The same reviewer flagged the absence of a pull loop. Wear reports are favorable: a long-term user noted minimal signs of wear after several weeks of trail runs, bike rides and a backpacking trip, and a runner said the gloves survived a fall without ripping. Quality control is the one blemish: a single REI buyer received a mismatched pair, with the right glove a size smaller than the left.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 38.4 g, light enough to live permanently in a pack lid or pocket
- Conductive fingers and palm that most owners say work reliably
- Slim enough to layer under shell gloves and mitts
- Stretchy, dexterous fit that holds up through trail running and scrambling
- Priced under $40, with a limited lifetime warranty

**Cons**

- Too thin for sustained use below about 40°F on its own
- Conductive material is on the palm side only, so side-of-finger taps can miss
- DWR coating offers little protection once the fabric is wet
- No pull loop
- Retailers disagree on the temperature range, so sizing and expectations need care

## Who Should Buy This

Three-season backpackers, thru-hikers and trail runners who want a phone-friendly glove for cool mornings and a liner for a colder kit will get good value here. It is also a sensible inner layer in a mitt-over-liner system for shoulder-season and winter trips. Hikers looking for a standalone winter glove, or anyone who needs wind and rain protection, should skip it. A midweight or softshell glove, or a pair of waterproof mitts over this liner, is the better answer.

## Verdict

The Lightweight Screentap does one job well: it keeps hands functional and phone-ready at a weight most people won't notice. Buy it as a cheap, packable liner or a cool-weather running and hiking glove. For anything colder than a brisk fall morning, plan to put something over it.

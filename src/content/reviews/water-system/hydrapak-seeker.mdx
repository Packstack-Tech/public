## Overview

The HydraPak Seeker is a collapsible TPU water bag for the unglamorous jobs: dirty bag for a gravity filter, camp water supply, dry-stretch reserve. It comes in 2 L and 4 L, with the same 42 mm mouth, coated handle and lash points on both. The trade-off is that it's a storage tool, not a drinking vessel, and it can leave a rubbery aftertaste.

## Performance

**Durability** is the Seeker's reputation. Reviewers who have carried it through multiple seasons of alpine climbing in the Sierra and Cascades, multi-pitch routes at Red Rock and desert hikes in Joshua Tree report no seam failures or tears. The RF-welded TPU construction is the reason. Against the Platypus Platy, which testers say tends to fail with age, the Seeker has outlasted it. When one owner found a pinhole near the collar, HydraPak replaced the bag without argument and shipped within days.

**Gravity filtration** is where the Seeker earns its pack space. The 42 mm mouth accepts HydraPak's own filter head and the Katadyn BeFree directly. The two lash points let the bag hang from a bear line or tree branch. Owners describe the routine as simple: fill at the source, thread the filter on, hang, and let it drip while camp chores get done. The 4 L size can batch-filter enough for dinner, dishes and morning bottles in a single go.

Sawyer Squeeze and Platypus QuickDraw users have a catch. Their 28 mm threads don't screw onto the Seeker's cap. HydraPak's Plug-N-Play cap with a 28 mm filter adapter is the workaround, but it is an extra purchase. Anyone built around a Sawyer should consider whether a CNOC VectoX suits them better.

**Filling** is easy. The wide mouth scoops from shallow pools without the contortions a narrow-necked bag demands. The tethered cap doubles as a loop to hold the bag upright at the water's edge. The coated handle makes one-handed carrying and pouring straightforward, and it keeps cold hands out of the stream.

**Drinking** is the weak spot. A floppy bag and a wide opening add up to spills. Testers describe needing two hands and a slow tip, and more than one admits to wearing the results. The 2 L can be paired with a Plug-N-Play cap and hose to work as a reservoir, but the Seeker is happiest as a bag that never touches a mouth.

**Taste** is the other recurring complaint. New bags carry a rubbery flavor that fades over the first few uses, though some owners say it never fully disappears. Repeated baking-soda soaks and thorough air drying help. The Platy, by comparison, arrives virtually taste-free. The fabric handle connecting the cap to the bag can also develop a musty smell if it's stored damp, so drying matters.

**Packability** is a strong suit. Empty, either size collapses to roughly the size of a fist and stows in the included storage bag. Because the TPU tolerates freezing and 60°C fills, the Seeker can serve as an ice pack or a makeshift hot-water bottle on a cold night. The dark Mammoth Grey colorway absorbs heat, so water left in direct sun warms quickly.

## Choosing a Version

The two sizes share a feature set. The difference comes down to capacity and what that capacity costs on the scale.

**2 L (76 g).** The long, slim 12.6 × 5.1 inch profile slips into a side pocket or beside a frame sheet. Two liters covers bottle top-offs, a night's cooking, or a small dirty bag for a solo hiker with a BeFree. At 38 g per liter it is the lighter pick for anyone carrying water on a short dry stretch. A solo hiker who filters at the source and drinks from bottles rarely needs more.

**4 L (147 g).** This one is for the camp. It costs 71 g more than the 2 L but doubles the volume, which works out to about 37 g per liter. That is enough to batch-filter for two people, carry water across a long dry section, or cut the number of trips to the creek. It is a bulkier, wider bag when full, with an in-use footprint of 310 × 220 mm. Groups, desert routes and hikers who cook elaborate dinners should buy this one. Solo ultralighters on water-rich routes will find it more than they need.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Multi-season durability, with no seam failures reported by long-term users
- 42 mm opening fills fast from shallow water
- Two lash points make gravity-filter setups hands-free
- Tethered cap prevents loss and doubles as a carry loop
- Coated handle is useful for pouring and one-handed carrying
- Beyond Lifetime Guarantee with responsive replacements
- BPA and PVC free; handles freezing and hot water up to 60°C

**Cons**

- Floppy TPU and a wide mouth make direct drinking spill-prone
- Rubbery taste can linger beyond the first washes
- Sawyer Squeeze and Platypus QuickDraw need an adapter setup
- No hose or bite valve in the box
- Dark colorway warms water quickly in the sun
- Handle tether can smell musty if stored damp

## Who Should Buy This

Backpackers running a BeFree or another 42 mm gravity setup should buy the Seeker, and so should anyone who wants a dirty bag that survives abuse. The 4 L suits group camps, desert routes and climbers who need water in quantity. The 2 L fits solo hikers who want a lighter, slimmer bag for filtering and camp chores. Hikers who mostly want a bladder to sip from on the move, or who have committed to a Sawyer Squeeze, should look at the Platypus Platy or CNOC VectoX.

## Verdict

The Seeker is a tough, well-designed bag that does its job in camp and disappears in the pack. The persistent plastic taste, the awkward drinking and the filter thread limits keep it from being universal. For a BeFree-based system, it is one of the better bags available, at 7.5 out of 10.

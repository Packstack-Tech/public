## Overview

Fizan has made poles in Veneto, Italy, since 1947, and the Compact 3 is the model that made the brand a fixture on American long trails. It is a three-section 7001 aluminum pole with a twist lock, claimed at 158 g each, and it costs about $70 a pair. The trade-off is plain: carbon-class weight at a budget price, paid for with a small grip and a lock mechanism that wants occasional cleaning.

## Performance

**Weight and packability.** At 11.2 oz for the pair, the Compact 3 sits less than an ounce above the Gossamer Gear LT5, a pole that costs more than twice as much. Owners who have put the poles on a scale report figures closer to 169–173 g each than the claimed 158 g, so a gear spreadsheet should carry a few extra grams. That is still remarkably little for a fully adjustable aluminum pole. Packed length of 58 cm is on the short side for a three-section design, though the LT5 folds to 23.5 in and the four-section Compact 4 shrinks further.

**Durability.** Aluminum has a quiet advantage over carbon: under a bad side load it bends instead of shattering, and a bent pole can often be coaxed straight enough to finish a trip. Long-term reports back this up. A Section Hiker reviewer logged 3,500 miles on a pair and found them still solid. Another owner covered thousands of miles, including a 1,500-mile yo-yo of the Great Divide Trail, on the original pair. One backpacker used them as support poles for a Six Moon Designs Skyscape Trekker and they held up through strong storms.

**The twist-lock.** This is where the Compact asks for something in return. The Flexy system uses plastic expanders inside each section. Out of the box it locks with little effort and holds under load. Over time, owners describe mornings when the sections spin without catching, usually after fine dust has worked into the mechanism. Cold weather can make the lock finicky too. Plastic expanders also lose elasticity with use, and eventual slippage is the normal end state for any twist-lock. A wipe-down after dusty stretches and a check before a long trip covers most of it. Anyone who wants a lever lock should buy a different pole.

**Grip and straps.** The EVA foam is comfortable over long days, and reviewers report blister-free hands in both heat and pouring rain. The problem is size. The grips are short and sized for smaller hands, so there is no extended foam below the handle for choking down on steep climbs, and hikers with average-to-large hands notice the narrow diameter. The wrist straps are adjustable but unpadded, and they work loose on the trail often enough to need re-tightening.

**Baskets.** The 35 mm and 50 mm baskets are not threaded and hold on by friction, which makes them easy to lose in deep snow or sucking mud.

**Shelter use.** The 132 cm maximum is enough for many trekking-pole tents, but a tall single-pole pitch will bring it to the limit. Anyone running a tarp or mid that wants more height should check the numbers before leaving the trailhead.

## Choosing a Version

The catalog lists two entries, and they describe the same pole. The "3" entry at 158 g is the per-pole weight of the three-section Compact 3. The "Standard" entry at 316 g is the weight of the pair, which is how the poles are sold. Nothing physical changes between them: same 7001 aluminum, same 58–132 cm range, same grip.

The choice that does matter is within Fizan's lineup. The Compact 4 adds a fourth section and weighs 169 g per pole. It collapses to 51 cm and extends to 125 cm, and it costs about $10 more. The Compact 3 is lighter, goes 7 cm taller, and has one fewer joint. A reviewer at The Trek who used both found the weight difference impossible to feel and preferred the 3 for being simpler to use. Hikers who pack poles inside a bag or carry-on, or who fit a short torso, should look at the Compact 4. Everyone else should take the 3.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Fully adjustable aluminum at 158 g per pole claimed, about 11.2 oz per pair
- Roughly $70 a pair, well below most poles in this weight class
- Bends rather than snaps, with multi-thousand-mile track records
- 58–132 cm range covers a wide span of heights and most trekking-pole shelters
- Replaceable carbide tips, with rubber covers and two basket sizes in the box
- Made in Italy by a family-owned maker

**Cons**

- Measured weight tends to run 10–15 g per pole above the claim
- Twist-lock can fail to engage when dusty or cold, and the plastic expanders wear
- Short, narrow grip with no extended foam suits smaller hands best
- Unpadded wrist straps loosen on the trail
- Friction-fit baskets can be lost in snow and mud
- 132 cm maximum is tight for some tall single-pole pitches

## Who Should Buy This

The Compact 3 suits the three-season thru-hiker or weekend gram-counter who wants ultralight poles without a carbon price, and who values aluminum's tendency to bend instead of break. It pairs well with trekking-pole shelters such as the Durston X-Mid, provided the pitch doesn't need more than 132 cm. Hikers with large hands, anyone who relies on choking down on the grip, and winter users who expect a lock that never needs attention should look elsewhere.

## Verdict

The Fizan Compact 3 delivers close to carbon weight at roughly $70, and it has the miles on real trails to back it up. The twist-lock needs care and the grip is small, but neither is a structural flaw. For most three-season backpackers it is the best-value ultralight pole available.

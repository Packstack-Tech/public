## Overview

The Baltoro 65 is Gregory's flagship expedition pack, built for hikers who are carrying real weight over multiple days rather than chasing a sub-2-lb base weight. It's not trying to be ultralight—at 2050g (about 4.5 lbs) it's roughly double what a Zpacks Arc Haul or Gossamer Gear Mariposa weighs—but it earns that weight with a suspension system designed to make 35-40+ lb loads disappear on your hips. If you're a section hiker, hunter, photographer, or expedition trekker who values comfort under load over grams saved, this is the category the Baltoro was built for.

## Performance

The suspension is really the whole story with this pack. The FreeFloat A3 hipbelt and shoulder harness auto-rotate to match your walking motion, and reviewers consistently single this out as the standout feature. <cite index="15-1,15-2,15-3,15-4">One reviewer compared it directly to Osprey's suspension, noting it "makes 45 pounds feel like 20," adding that their hips never felt sore, neither did their shoulders.</cite> A tester from TGO Magazine put it through several hours on <cite index="11-19">pathless tussocky boggy ground and high-stepping over fallen trees, and their shoulders and hips felt fine afterward</cite>. That's a fairly demanding real-world test, and the pack came through it. The same reviewer <cite index="11-17,11-18">carried a 19kg load in the Baltoro 65 and found it very comfortable, noting Gregory's recommended max carry weight of 23kg</cite>—which lines up with the US spec sheet's 50 lb ceiling.

Long-term owners back this up too. A Trailspace reviewer who's used the pack for years said <cite index="13-1">it left their shoulders and back feeling fine at the end of a long day of dodging blowdowns</cite>, and another called it <cite index="13-18,13-19,13-20">"the most comfortable backpack I have ever owned," adding that it's tough, roomy, and requires very little adjustment</cite>.

Fit and adjustability get consistent praise. <cite index="9-4,9-5">The Baltoro 65 offers plenty of room for a long weekend, and if you're gear-savvy, enough space for an extra day or two in a pinch, with a trim profile and ventilated, terrain-adaptive suspension</cite> that's meant for multi-stage trips off the beaten path. Sizing has also improved with the current model—Section Hiker noted the redesigned frame and fit system <cite index="10-3">accommodates different torso lengths, hip belt lengths, and body shapes to dial in a custom fit</cite>, and it now comes <cite index="10-1">in multiple overlapping sizes (S, M, L), slightly lighter than before, bringing it under 5 lbs</cite>.

Durability is where opinions split a bit. Most long-term owners report a bombproof pack, but at least one REI reviewer had a rougher experience, reporting that <cite index="15-17,15-18">the straps needed constant adjusting, the front chest buckle snapped within the first 10 miles, and the zipper kept catching itself</cite>. That's not the norm in the reviews I found, but it's worth flagging since buckle failures on a pack meant to carry 40+ lbs are a bigger deal than on an ultralight rig you're babying at 15 lbs.

Organization is a genuine strength. Between the U-zip front panel, dual zippered front pockets, side bottle pockets, and the removable hydration sleeve that doubles as a daypack, there's a place for everything—Trailspace reviewers repeatedly called out the sheer number of pockets as a highlight.

On the negative side, the weight is the recurring complaint across nearly every review I read. As one Trailspace user put it bluntly, <cite index="13-2">"the only complaint is the weight, which comes in at 6+ pounds for the large."</cite> That's the trade-off you're signing up for with this pack: bombproof comfort under load in exchange for carrying a suspension system heavy enough to need one.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- FreeFloat A3 suspension genuinely transfers weight to the hips—multiple independent reviewers report comfort at 35-40+ lb loads
- Highly adjustable torso/hipbelt fit system, now available in overlapping S/M/L sizing
- Excellent organization: front U-zip access, multiple pockets, removable daypack/hydration sleeve
- Durable, recycled-content fabrics with PFC-free DWR
- Well-suited for genuinely heavy loads (up to 50 lbs), where ultralight packs start to fall apart

**Cons**

- At ~4.5-6 lbs depending on size, this is a heavy pack by any modern standard—not for base-weight-obsessed hikers
- At least one report of buckle and zipper durability issues under heavy use
- Overkill (and needless weight) for loads under 25-30 lbs
- Included accessory daypack is small and fairly basic

## Who Should Buy This

This pack makes sense for anyone regularly carrying 30+ lbs for multiple days—think bear-canister trips, winter loads, hunting packouts, or international trekking where you're not counting grams. It's also a solid pick for newer backpackers who'd rather have a forgiving, well-cushioned suspension while they dial in packing skills. If you're chasing an ultralight base weight or your loaded pack rarely tops 25 lbs, the Baltoro's extra pound or two of suspension hardware isn't buying you anything—look at a lighter internal-frame pack instead.

## Verdict

The Baltoro 65 remains one of the most comfortable heavy-load packs on the market, and the redesigned FreeFloat A3 suspension and sizing system are real improvements over the previous generation. It's not for ultralight hikers, but for anyone hauling real weight over real miles, it's earned its reputation. I'd put it at an 8/10—docked slightly for weight and the occasional durability complaint, but hard to beat for load-carrying comfort.

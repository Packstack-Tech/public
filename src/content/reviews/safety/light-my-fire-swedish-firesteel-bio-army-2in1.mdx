## Overview

The Swedish FireSteel BIO Army 2in1 is the larger of Light My Fire's two ferrocerium fire starters. It was originally developed for the Swedish Department of Defense, and it pairs a thick rod with a stainless striker and a handle that doubles as an emergency whistle. It is built to be used for years, not seasons. The cost of that durability is mass: at 58 g it is far heavier than a lighter that does the same job on most trips.

## Performance

**Sparks.** A ferro rod has no fuel, no flint wheel and no valve, and that is the whole argument for it. Drag the stainless striker down the rod and the shavings ignite into a shower of sparks at close to 3,000°C. The Army's thick rod throws a bigger shower than the smaller Scout model. Australian Hiker's reviewer, who had left the Army at the bottom of a gear-testing box for a while, called the sparks intense. Users in a Blade Forums thread made the same point about the size of the shower. The striker's edge is ground to bite, which is why the tool throws large sparks without much technique. The method printed on the packaging works: hold the striker against the rod and pull the rod back, rather than scraping the striker along it.

**Wet and cold.** The rod is unaffected by water, so a dunking or a night of rain doesn't take it out of service the way it would a box of matches. Light My Fire describes the tool as resistant to moisture, cold and altitude, and the design came out of Swedish military requirements for lighting fires in exactly those conditions. The sparks will also ignite stove fuel directly. That makes the FireSteel a workable backup for alcohol and canister stoves, though a thumb-flick lighter is easier for the same job.

**Longevity.** Light My Fire rates the Army at up to 12,000 strikes. The Scout is rated for about 3,000. Australian Hiker's reviewer, three years in, saw hardly any wear and judged the rating a lifetime's supply for most users. A Blade Forums poster put it more colorfully, saying the Army would last him two lifetimes. A butane lighter runs dry or fails when damp. A ferro rod keeps working until the rod is a stub.

**The whistle.** The handle of the striker has a 120 dB whistle built into it. This is a small but real contribution to a safety kit, since many hikers already carry a whistle on a sternum strap. Folding it into the fire starter means one fewer item if you carry both, though it is no reason on its own to buy the tool.

**Weight and bulk.** This is where the Army loses ground with ultralight backpackers. At 58 g, it weighs more than two and a half Bic mini lighters (about 22 g each, per Australian Hiker's comparison). Australian Hiker's reviewer said it isn't really ultralight. At 94 mm long and 26 mm wide, it is also bulky for a pocket in a minimal kit. Some forum owners say they prefer smaller sizes for the same reason. The Scout version, which Australian Hiker lists at 38 g, is the same design in a smaller package. Its rating of about 3,000 strikes is more than enough for a backpacking lifetime.

**Skill required.** Sparks are not flames. Ferro rods need dry, fine tinder such as cotton, birch bark, fatwood or a commercial fire starter, and some practice to place sparks accurately. Anyone who has never lit a fire this way should try it in the backyard before relying on it in the backcountry.

**Materials.** The handle is Terralene, a biobased plastic made from renewable resources and fully recyclable, which Light My Fire uses across its BIO line. The rod and striker are connected by a cord, so neither piece gets dropped in the snow or the duff.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Up to 12,000 strikes, which outlasts most trips and most owners
- Works wet, cold and at altitude, with no fuel or moving parts
- Large, easy-to-strike rod throws a big spark shower
- Integrated 120 dB whistle
- Cord keeps rod and striker together
- Biobased handle; made in Sweden
- Typically sells for about $18–24

**Cons**

- 58 g is heavy next to a lighter or the 38 g Scout
- Bulky at 94 mm long for minimal pocket kits
- Needs good tinder and some practice
- The rated life is far more than a backpacker is likely to use
- Less convenient than a lighter for everyday stove use

## Who Should Buy This

This is for backpackers who want a fire source that can't run out of fuel or be killed by moisture: winter and mountain hikers, hunters, guides, packrafters, and anyone building a bushcraft or survival kit. It is also a good choice for a vehicle or home emergency kit, where it can sit for years and still work. Gram counters should look at the Scout, which does the same job at 38 g, or just carry a Bic and a backup. Anyone who doesn't light wood fires on trail is carrying a heavy backup for a stove that a lighter will start just fine.

## Verdict

The Army FireSteel is the most durable fire starter in Light My Fire's range, with a rating of up to 12,000 strikes, a whistle, and the reliability that is the reason ferro rods exist. For ultralight use it is more tool than most trips call for, and the Scout is the smarter pick for weight-conscious hikers. For hard use in bad weather, or for a kit that has to work after years in a drawer, the Army earns its 58 g.

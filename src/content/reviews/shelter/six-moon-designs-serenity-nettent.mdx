## Overview

The Serenity NetTent is Six Moon Designs' one-person no-see-um inner: a half-pyramid of 20D netting on a 30D silicone-coated nylon bathtub floor, built to hang under the company's Gatewood Cape or Deschutes Tarp. It also stands on its own for dry, buggy nights. The trade-off is built into the concept. At 312 g it adds bug and splash protection cheaply, but it does nothing about rain by itself, and it is sold without the pole that holds it up.

## Performance

**Bug protection and airflow.** This is the job the Serenity does best. The no-see-um canopy keeps out mosquitoes and smaller insects while moving air freely, and the bathtub floor, with ends about 8 inches tall, blocks crawling pests and splashback. Six Moon Designs also pitches it against mice and other ground-level visitors. A reviewer at Backpacking Light, writing about long use with the Gatewood Cape, called it superb on warm, humid, rain-free nights, the kind of Midwest summer weather where a closed-up tent turns into a sauna. The same reviewer reported no signs of fragile netting over extended use.

**Pairing with a tarp.** The Serenity connects to the Gatewood Cape or Deschutes Tarp with mitten clips. SectionHiker's review points out that the inner can stay attached to the tarp between camps, which makes the pair a fast, double-wall pitch. Backpacking Light framed the combination as an inner-and-outer system that weighs far less than a conventional double-wall tent. The two-piece approach also has an upside beyond weight: on a clear night, the net can be pitched alone and the tarp left in the pack.

**Interior room.** The geometry is cramped by design. The floor runs 84 inches long and 26 inches wide, which fits one person on a narrow pad with little room to spare for gear. The 45 inch peak is enough to sit up under, and the raised ends keep the netting off a sleeper's face, a real improvement over a bug bivy with mesh draped on the nose. The Backpacking Light reviewer accepted the confinement as the price of the weight. Hikers who want to spread out will not.

**Pitching.** Setup takes six stakes and one 45 inch pole, which Six Moon Designs sells separately. A trekking pole can serve as the support in stand-alone mode. A side entry with a large door makes getting in and out less of a contortion than the footprint suggests.

**Weather.** The floor deals with splash and drafts, and that is where the weather resistance ends. In the rain, the Serenity needs a tarp overhead. Hikers choosing it as a lone shelter should plan on dry forecasts or an AT-style lean-to shelter, a use Six Moon Designs specifically recommends for it.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 312 g for full-enclosure bug protection and a bathtub floor
- Clips to the Gatewood Cape and Deschutes Tarp for a quick modular pitch
- Packs to 10 x 5 in
- 45 in peak and raised ends keep the netting off your face
- Works alone for stargazing on dry nights
- Netting has held up in long-term use, per owner reports

**Cons**

- No rain protection without a tarp
- 26 in width leaves little room for gear
- Pole is sold separately, and stakes are not included
- $150 is steep for a bug net with a floor
- Best value comes only for those already in the Six Moon tarp ecosystem

## Who Should Buy This

The Serenity suits ultralight hikers who already own, or plan to buy, a Gatewood Cape or Deschutes Tarp and want a clip-in inner for bugs and ground moisture. It also fits Appalachian Trail hikers who spend nights in shelters, where a net and floor are all that is needed, and dry-climate hikers who want a netted sleeping space for warm nights. Hikers who want a single, self-contained shelter for storm-prone routes should look at a full trekking-pole tent. Anyone who wants room to sit and organize gear indoors will find the floor too tight.

## Verdict

The Serenity does one thing and does it at 11 oz: it turns a tarp into a double-wall shelter and, on its own, a bug-proof place to sleep. The value depends almost entirely on pairing it with a Six Moon tarp. In that setup it is an efficient, proven piece of kit. As a standalone purchase for variable weather, it is the wrong tool.

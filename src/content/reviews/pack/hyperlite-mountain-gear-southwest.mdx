## Overview

The Hyperlite Mountain Gear Southwest is the company's rough-country pack: a roll-top Dyneema tube with two removable aluminum stays and solid-fabric exterior pockets in place of the mesh found on its Windrider cousin. It is aimed at experienced ultralight hikers who keep loads near 30 lb and want a pack that survives brush, talus, and a week of rain. The trade-off is familiar HMG territory: near-bombproof construction and weather protection, set against no load lifters, almost no ventilation, and a price north of $350.

## Performance

**The fabric.** The current Southwest swaps the older Dyneema Composite Hybrid for Dyneema Woven Composite 3.9. HMG positions it as roughly as light as the hybrid, with markedly better abrasion and tear resistance than the heavier 5.9. Testers who ran the pack through multi-night trips and summit pushes in Baxter State Park, in conditions from steady rain to dry granite, came away impressed. Durability was rarely the complaint with earlier HMG packs, so the practical gain is modest. The more noticeable change is tactile: a matte finish and less of the crinkly rattle that old Dyneema packs make in cold weather. White and black now share the same fabric and price, though black costs weight: 979 g against 896 g in the 55 L Medium. Owners also note that white shows dirt quickly.

**Weather resistance.** The fabric is waterproof and the vertical side seams are taped. In soak tests the main compartment stayed essentially dry. Roll-top packs still admit water at the opening in sustained downpours, so most owners keep sleeping bags in a dry bag or liner. Owners of older packs reported a little water seeping in where the shoulder straps are sewn to the body after extended heavy rain. HMG has revised the construction since.

**Carry and comfort.** The two aluminum stays do most of the structural work, sending weight to a dual-density hip belt that reviewers call stable for its weight. The shoulder straps are thin but dense. They stay comfortable under moderate loads, but pressure points appear past about 30 lb. Switchback Travel's tester logged long days at up to 30 lb on the 40 L with no hot spots, and CleverHiker named the 40 L its best all-around backpack, praising the frame's weight transfer. Halfway Anywhere's long-distance reviewer rated the 55 L excellent to around 30 lb and acceptable to 40. CleverHiker's testers say they cap their own loads at 35 lb, and one Trailspace reviewer judged the 40 lb rating likely overstated. A longtime reviewer at The Hiking Life vouched for 40 lb in demanding conditions, though he noted that few hikers' kits will ever reach that weight. The redesigned straps curve more around the neck, which answers a long-running fit complaint.

The missing piece is load lifters. Many competing ultralight packs now include them, and they help at 25 lb and above, especially for hikers with less muscular trapezius muscles. Without them, the shoulders take the strain at the heavy end of the range.

**Organization.** The layout is one large roll-top compartment, three exterior pockets, and two hip belt pockets, with no front access and no dividers. The solid Dyneema pockets are the Southwest's signature: no mesh to shred on brush, cactus, or talus. The latest pockets are deeper, with lower rear edges that make bottles easier to reach and hold loose gear more securely. They run snug, and owners say the side pockets take some practice to use on the move. The hip belt pockets are among the roomier ones on ultralight packs and take a phone and snacks. The roll-top's Velcro can snag on clothing.

**Ventilation.** There isn't any. The foam back panel is fine in cool and mild weather, but hot days mean a sweaty back. That's the price of the weight and the waterproofing.

## Choosing a Version

The four versions share the same architecture: roll-top, aluminum stays, solid pockets, and a 40 lb rating. What changes is main-compartment volume and, to a smaller degree, torso length. Weight climbs with torso size across the line, so taller hikers should expect a few extra grams as the cost of a correct fit rather than a flaw.

**Original 40 (853 g).** The lightest of the four, with a 2,400 cu in main compartment. It suits short overnights, section hikes with frequent resupply, and desert or canyon routes where durability matters more than capacity. CleverHiker's testers favor it for three- to five-day trips, with the caveat that their kit is compact and dialed in. A bear canister and a week of food will not fit.

**55 L (874 g).** Only 21 g over the Original 40 buys 15 more liters: room for longer food carries, bulkier quilts, and a bear can. The roll-top can be rolled down far enough to make a 55 behave like a 40 on lighter trips, which makes it the forgiving choice for hikers whose trip lengths vary.

**Original (896 g).** The 3,400 cu in pack in white, Medium, and the figure most often quoted for the Southwest. It carries the same volume as the 55 L at 22 g more, a difference smaller than an ounce. Choose between the two on availability and torso fit, not on weight.

**2400 Large (916 g).** The 40 L volume cut for longer torsos. It has the same 2,400 cu in main compartment as the Original 40 but weighs 63 g more, and 20 g more than the Original. Hikers with long backs should fit by torso length first and volume second; a correctly sized Large will carry noticeably better than a Medium stretched past its range.

Hikers running a fully dialed ultralight kit on resupply-heavy routes can go with a 40 L. Everyone else should take a 55 L.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Fully waterproof Dyneema body with taped seams
- Solid Dyneema exterior pockets that resist snags, tears, and brush
- Removable aluminum stays give real structure for under a kilogram of pack
- Roll-top adapts to changing load volumes
- Roomy hip belt pockets
- Four torso sizes
- The 55 L weighs only a few grams more than the 40 L

**Cons**

- No load lifters, which limits comfort above 25–30 lb
- Thin padding, and no adjustability beyond the four torso sizes
- No back ventilation
- Velcro on the roll-top can snag
- The 40 L is tight for anything beyond short, resupply-friendly trips
- Price starts north of $350, steep even if longevity eventually justifies it

## Who Should Buy This

Hikers with a lightweight kit already sorted (tent, quilt, pad, and cook system) will get the most out of the Southwest. It suits wet-weather trips, off-trail routes through brush and talus, and anyone who has lost a water bottle through a shredded mesh pocket. Beginners still building a lighter kit should upgrade the tent and sleeping bag first. Anyone routinely carrying more than 35 lb, or with shoulder or trap problems, should look at a pack with load lifters.

## Verdict

The Southwest isn't the freshest design in the category, but the woven Dyneema upgrade, reshaped straps, and deeper pockets show it has been maintained rather than left to coast. The missing load lifters keep it from being a universal recommendation. For an experienced ultralight hiker who holds loads to around 30 lb, it remains a benchmark: choose a 55 L for most trips, and a 40 L only if the kit is truly compact.

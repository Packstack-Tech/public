## Overview

The Compass 360° is Grand Trunk's original swivel stool: a tripod seat with an aluminum frame and a rotating nylon seat that spins a full 360 degrees. It's aimed less at ultralight backpackers chasing grams and more at car campers, anglers, hunters glassing terrain, and photographers who need a seat that can pivot without them having to stand up and reposition. At 648.9 g (about 1.4 lb), it's light enough to justify tossing in a pack for shorter trips, even if it's not the stool you'd bring on a thru-hike.

## Performance

The headline feature is the swivel, and it's the part reviewers consistently single out. One tester who ran it side-by-side with Grand Trunk's own UC and XT stools <cite index="2-1">described the swivel action as smooth and noiseless</cite>, and a separate reviewer noted the mechanism <cite index="12-7,12-8,12-9">was surprisingly smooth given how light the stool is and how much of it relies on plastic components, with no rough or grinding action</cite>. That's a fair concern to have going in — a stool this light could easily feel flimsy at the pivot point — but it doesn't seem to be a problem in practice.

Stability and weight capacity get similarly good marks. A ProView tester who put dozens of strangers in the seat over several weeks <cite index="1-6">found the stool could definitely hold up to weights of above 300 pounds</cite>, and after six weeks of daily use in the field, <cite index="1-8">it still looked brand new</cite>. Worth noting: Grand Trunk's own product page lists a <cite index="8-15">260 lb capacity</cite> for the standard Compass 360, while some retail listings show 330 lb — so double-check the current spec sheet for your specific model year before you rely on it for a heavier user.

Comfort is decent for what amounts to a minimalist stool rather than a lounge chair — there's no back support, so don't expect all-evening-by-the-fire comfort the way you'd get from a Helinox chair. Still, one long-term user called it <cite index="1-10,1-11">exceptionally comfortable, with a seat cushion that fits most people and a height that works even for long-legged folks</cite>. GearJunkie's reviewer liked the more upright posture it forces compared to sitting low in a camp chair, noting they were <cite index="2-6,2-7">sitting in a more active posture and could turn to face people and look in different directions</cite>, something logs and coolers can't offer.

Where it falls down a bit is setup and packability. This isn't a stool that self-folds like a tripod camera stool — a Dick's Sporting Goods reviewer flagged that <cite index="3-14,3-15,3-16">it doesn't quick-fold into your pack; instead you need to take it apart and stuff it into the travel bag</cite>. The same reviewer also pointed out a ground-contact quirk: <cite index="3-17,3-18,3-19,3-20">the leg feet are just capped without any type of foot, so they sink into the ground when you sit, making an already short stool even lower</cite>. That's a real consideration if you're using it on soft dirt, sand, or duff rather than a hard-packed site or dock. A B&H reviewer also mentioned a minor learning curve: <cite index="6-7,6-8,6-9">attaching the seat to the frame can be tricky if you don't use the finger loops properly, and getting it back into the zippered pouch takes some practice at first</cite> — a "learning curve" that most owners seem to work through after a use or two.

On the trail specifically, GearJunkie's early testing was limited to car camping and yard use rather than backcountry miles, so hard data on how it performs after a full day of hiking with it strapped to a pack is still thin — worth flagging if long-distance packability is your main concern. For car camping, day trips, hunting glassing setups, and photography, though, the feedback is consistently positive, with multiple owners specifically calling out its usefulness for scenarios like glassing terrain or tracking moving subjects with a camera.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Genuinely smooth, quiet 360° swivel that holds up over repeated use
- Sturdy for its weight — testers reported no durability issues after weeks of regular use
- Packs down small and includes a carry bag with shoulder strap
- Versatile beyond camping: fishing, hunting glassing setups, sporting events, photography
- Lifetime warranty from Grand Trunk

**Cons**

- No back support — it's a stool, not a chair, so all-evening comfort is limited
- Doesn't fold as one unit; you disassemble it to pack it away, which slows setup/teardown
- Capped leg feet can sink into soft ground, lowering an already low seat
- Listed weight capacity varies by source (260 lb vs. 330 lb) — worth confirming for heavier users
- Limited hard backcountry (multi-day, high-mileage) testing data available so far

## Who Should Buy This

This is a strong pick for car campers, anglers, hunters who spend long stretches glassing terrain, and photographers who need to pivot toward a subject without breaking their position. It's also light enough that day hikers and short-trip backpackers who prioritize a real seat over a foam pad will find it worth the weight. If you're counting grams for a long thru-hike or need a chair with back support for lounging, look elsewhere.

## Verdict

The Compass 360° delivers exactly what it promises: a light, sturdy stool with a swivel mechanism that actually works well in the field. It's not a backcountry ultralight staple, and the capped feet and disassembly-required packing are minor annoyances, but for car camping, fishing, hunting, and photography use, it's an easy recommend. I'd put it at a 7.5/10 — a smart, well-executed niche product rather than an essential piece of ultralight kit.

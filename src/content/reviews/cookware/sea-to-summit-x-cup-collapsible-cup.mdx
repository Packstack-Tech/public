## Overview

The Sea to Summit X-Cup is a one-cup drinking vessel built from food-grade silicone walls and a rigid nylon rim. Pull the rim and the walls extend into a cup. Push down and the whole thing becomes a flat disk about the thickness of a stack of coasters. It's aimed at backpackers who want a real cup for coffee, tea or drink mix without giving up pack space, and the trade-off is a narrow base that makes it easy to knock over.

## Performance

**Packability.** This is the whole argument for the X-Cup, and it holds up. Collapsed, the cup is 1.27 cm tall and 9.5 cm across, which means it slides into a hip-belt pocket, a lid pocket or the gap at the bottom of a pot without displacing anything. One owner reports it fits under the lid of a Toaks 750 ml pot. Another Trailspace reviewer says the cup has been easy to live with on coffee and drink-mix duty because it never has to earn its space.

**Weight.** At 45 g, the cup is light for what it is, but it doesn't beat everything. A Trailspace reviewer who weighed theirs at 1.58 oz compared it against a titanium cup-and-lid setup and called it a wash. The case for the X-Cup isn't that it is the lightest option. It's that it costs almost nothing in volume, and it's a second vessel for anyone whose cook pot is already busy.

**Heat and drinking.** The silicone sidewalls are rated to 300°F, well above boiling water, so a fresh pour of tea or coffee is within spec. Owners say it holds heat reasonably well for a camp cup, and the rigid rim gives a stable lip to drink from and a place to grip. Silicone is not titanium: it won't go on a stove, and it won't be confused with a double-wall insulated mug. It's a cup for pouring hot water into, not for heating water in.

**Stability.** The most consistent complaint is about the base. It's narrower than the mouth of the cup and it's soft silicone, so the X-Cup rewards a flat surface and a deliberate set-down. A full cup on a sloped log or a sleeping pad is an invitation to spill. Reviewers who like the cup say they've learned to hold it or wedge it, and that it's more finicky than most cups rather than unusable.

**Durability and taste.** Reviewers describe the silicone as thick, and the cup as hard to damage by packing or dropping. Long-term owners have mostly reported no cracking at the fold points. A minority report a silicone taste, particularly early on, and some mention cracks developing over time. Washing it thoroughly before the first use is the standard advice, and the lifetime warranty offers some cover if the walls do give out. Cleaning is simple: it rinses easily, dries quickly, and can go in a dishwasher at home.

**Measuring.** Calibrated ridges inside let the cup double as a measuring cup. That's useful for dosing out couscous or instant oats, though at one cup of total volume, there's not much to measure. The cup is best used for what it is, not for what the marks suggest.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Folds to 1.27 cm, essentially flat
- 45 g, about the same as a lidded titanium cup
- Rigid rim gives a sturdy drinking edge
- Rated to 300°F; dishwasher and microwave safe
- Calibrated ridges for measuring
- Nests in or under many pots and bowls
- Limited lifetime warranty

**Cons**

- Narrow, soft base tips easily on uneven ground
- Can't be used on a stove or fire
- 250 ml is small; the 480 ml X-Mug is the better choice for big coffee drinkers
- Some users report a silicone taste, and a few report cracking after long use
- No lid, so no carrying liquid

## Who Should Buy This

Backpackers who want a dedicated cup, particularly those cooking from a small pot or using a bottle-and-stove setup, will find this a cheap and compact upgrade. It suits the coffee drinker who wants a hot drink at camp without a mug taking up room, and anyone who brings a drink mix and would rather not flavor an entire water bottle. Hikers who already have a lidded titanium mug that doubles as a pot gain very little. Anyone who wants a cup that holds 16 ounces or more should step up to the X-Mug, which is 78 g.

## Verdict

The X-Cup does one job, giving a backpacker a proper cup that takes up almost no space, and it does it for 45 g. The unstable base and small capacity keep it from being a standout, but for the price and pack footprint, it's an easy addition to a kitchen kit.

## Overview

The MH500 is Decathlon's answer to the premium roof-tent market: a clamshell-style fold-out tent for two that bolts to a car's roof bars, sleeps on a built-in 5 cm foam mattress, and uses blackout fabric to keep the interior dark and cool. It's aimed at weekend road-trippers and trailhead sleepers, not at anyone counting grams. The trade-off is plain: it costs a fraction of most roof tents, but it is built for summer, and its always-open vents and fixed mesh give it limits in wet, windy weather.

## Performance

**Weight and mounting.** Forty-five kilograms on the roof, with the ladder adding another 7 kg, is the kind of number that ends conversations on r/Ultralight. Decathlon's own spec pages vary on the tent's weight, with some listing 48.5 kg, so buyers should plan for the heavier figure when checking a vehicle's roof rating. Owners describe the first-time setup as the chore. There are rails and ladder mounts to attach to the underside, and one owner on Decathlon's UK site advises clearing out leftover metal shavings from manufacturing so they don't chew the fabric. One roundup of roof tents put the first installation at around an hour, longer than the other tents in the group. Securing the tent can be fiddly when clearance under the roof bars is tight, though owners say it gets easier with practice. The bar requirements are specific: more than 7.5 cm between the roof and the top of the bar, and a bar tube narrower than 9.5 cm.

**Pitching.** This is where the MH500 earns its reputation. Whip off the cover, lower the ladder, and the tent unfolds. A tester in a roundup of compact roof tents clocked the clamshell opening at around 15 seconds, with an audible click from the telescopic ladder confirming it's seated. Quechua claims under five minutes to pitch and pack away; rental operators quote one to two minutes once the tent is mounted. Packing up is similarly quick, though that same roundup noted some fabric doesn't tuck away as tidily as on rival tents.

**Heat and darkness.** The patented Fresh & Black fabric is the headline feature, and owners back it up. Reviewers report an interior that stays dark and noticeably cool, with Quechua citing 99 percent darkness in daylight. Add the flysheet and mechanical vents and the MH500 becomes a sensible choice for hot-weather camping, and for light sleepers who want to stay in bed past sunrise.

**Weather.** The weak spot. The vents are always open and the bug mesh is permanently fixed to the tent body, so the tent is breezy by design. One Decathlon UK owner warned that wind-driven rain can get in through those windows and called the tent a light weekend-getaway option. A roundup tester likewise said buyers should be confident of a dry trip. The flysheet is rated above 2,000 mm and the tent was wind-tunnel tested to 60 km/h, which covers summer showers, not autumn gales. Quechua's instructions say not to pitch facing the wind and not to drive with the tent up. The same owner pointed to Decathlon's newer MH900 as having better-sorted doors, storm flaps and mounting hardware. The permanent mesh also means no passing gear through a window.

**Sleep comfort.** The 5 cm foam mattress runs almost the full length of the 240 cm sleeping area, and owners praise the space for two. Some add a mattress topper for extra cushioning. Quechua sells a fitted sheet, and rental operators advise using one to protect the foam. A Singletrack World forum poster gave a practical tip: the groundsheet only zips in one orientation, so mark one corner of the sheet and tent with ribbon. The same poster complained about aerodynamics. The MH500 is a blunt box on a roof, so expect wind noise, though one owner traced much of it to the roof bars themselves.

**Value and reliability.** On Decathlon's sites the tent averages around 4.5 to 4.6 out of 5 across roughly 450 to 500 reviews. The UK price has sat around £1,000, and the five-year warranty and a repairable design add reassurance. Owners with a dozen nights in it by early summer called it quality, and a Scandinavian road-trip reviewer logged three weeks of rain, wind and fjords with it.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Pitches in seconds on the roof; the ladder lowers and the tent unfolds
- Fresh & Black fabric keeps the interior dark and cool in summer
- Built-in 5 cm mattress and a generous 140 × 240 cm sleeping area
- Priced well below most roof tents, with a five-year warranty
- Light for a roof tent at 45 kg
- Foldable ladder and shoe bag included

**Cons**

- First installation takes patience, around an hour by one tester's account
- Always-open vents and fixed mesh let in wind-driven rain
- Not designed for heavy weather or shoulder-season use; two-season rating
- Roof bars need 75 kg dynamic rating and specific clearance dimensions
- Fabric doesn't always fold away neatly
- Blunt profile adds wind noise and drag

## Who Should Buy This

The MH500 suits couples and solo travelers who drive to trailheads and campsites in warm months and want a bed on the roof that takes seconds to deploy. It's a good second sleep system for hikers who drive in late and leave early. Buyers should confirm their vehicle's roof load rating and bar dimensions first. Anyone camping in storms, in cold shoulder seasons, or who needs a tent that works above 60 km/h winds should look at sturdier roof tents or Decathlon's own MH900. Weight-focused backpackers who sleep away from the car are better served by a trail shelter.

## Verdict

The MH500 delivers what it promises: quick setup, a cool, dark bed, and a comfortable mattress at a price that undercuts the field. Its limits are weather protection and an awkward first install, both easy to live with for summer weekends. For fair-weather road trips it's a solid buy, and for four-season use it isn't.

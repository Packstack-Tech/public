## Overview

The MegaLite is Western Mountaineering's wider-cut take on their legendary UltraLite — same 850+ fill power goose down, same continuous baffle construction, but with extra shoulder and foot girth for side sleepers or anyone who hates feeling shrink-wrapped in a mummy bag. It's rated to 30°F, but as you'll see below, that number undersells it. This is a bag for three-season backpackers and thru-hikers who want a lightweight down bag but aren't willing to trade away room to move.

## Performance

**Warmth.** This is where the MegaLite earns its reputation. <cite index="10-1,10-2">It's extremely lightweight and packable, but also luxuriously roomy and comfortable, and testers found it warmer than most 30°F bags, and even some 15°F bags tested alongside it.</cite> That's not a fluke — Western Mountaineering is conservative with its fill power claims. <cite index="10-13,10-14,10-15,10-16">The MegaLite is filled with down advertised as "850+" fill power, but it's important to understand that's a conservative estimate — WM claims the actual rating of their down is "almost always above 900," and they report the lower number to be on the safe side.</cite> One long-term owner who used the bag on <cite index="11-1,11-2">a 55-day thruhike through Scandinavian mountains reported sleeping warm and comfortable during the entire hike, with plenty of space to move around thanks to the slightly wider design</cite>. That said, don't push it too far past its rating — one reviewer <cite index="13-7,13-8">found the MegaLite works well for trips where lows are in the mid-to-high 30s, but noted it gets a little chilly in baselayers at about 35 degrees when used on a pad in the 2.5-3 R-value range</cite>. Another user who tested it down to 5°F <cite index="14-6,14-7">said it was a little chilly but kept him safe</cite>, which lines up with treating the 21°F EN lower limit as a true survival threshold, not a comfort number.

**Room to move.** The whole point of the MegaLite over WM's slimmer UltraLite is space. <cite index="12-1">The MegaLite has sufficient shoulder girth (59-60") for a guy and comfortable hip room (51-52") for a gal</cite> — WM's current spec sheet lists 64 inches at the shoulder, so it's grown slightly over the years, but the intent is the same. On the trail this translates to being able to shift positions, bend your knees, or bring a jacket inside the bag without fighting the fabric. It's also a viable layering piece — one reviewer noted the extra room lets him <cite index="13-9,13-10">layer up with a down jacket if needed, and the extra space has helped him camp in temperatures well below 0 when using the MegaLite as an outer bag over another bag</cite>.

**Packability and weight.** At 680g, this is genuinely competitive with much pricier "ultra-premium" bags, and one owner noted it <cite index="12-10,12-11">packed down far more than a 30-degree REI bag and a 40-degree North Face synthetic bag</cite> on a canoe trip. Reddit's ultralight community treats it as a benchmark — the general sentiment is that <cite index="1-1">the MegaLite fits the bill for a non-constricting mummy bag design, though if it's too expensive the REI Magma 30 is spec'd extremely similarly at a much lower cost</cite>.

**Durability and long-term loft.** WM's reputation for craftsmanship shows up repeatedly in long-term reviews. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers, who'd owned the bag for years, said the down <cite index="10-17">felt every bit as lofty as bags with reported fill power over 900, and it's held up over the several years they've owned this bag</cite>. That's the kind of longevity claim that matters more than a spec sheet — down bags are only a good investment if the loft survives repeated compression cycles.

**Quirks.** No bag is flawless. One more critical review flagged <cite index="17-19">minor issues with the zipper and hood</cite> as the main things holding it back from being untouchable at the top of its category — though details on exactly what those issues are (snag frequency, hood cinch design) were thin in what I found, so treat that as a minor caveat rather than a dealbreaker.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Consistently rated warmer in practice than its 30°F label suggests, thanks to conservative fill-power reporting
- Roomier shoulder and foot box than WM's UltraLite, without a big weight penalty
- Long track record of maintaining loft over years of use
- Continuous baffle design lets you shift down fill for adjustable warmth
- Excellent packed size for a bag with this much interior volume

**Cons**

- Expensive relative to bags like the REI Magma 30, which some users consider a near-equivalent at lower cost
- EN lower limit of 21°F is a true limit, not a comfort zone — expect to feel cold below the mid-30s without extra layers or a warmer pad
- Reports of minor zipper and hood niggles in some reviews
- 12D shell fabric is durable for its weight but still needs the usual careful-with-camp-chairs-and-crampons treatment of ultralight down bags

## Who Should Buy This

The MegaLite makes the most sense for three-season backpackers and thru-hikers who run warm-to-average and want a wider cut than a standard mummy bag without stepping up to a full double-wide. It's a particularly good fit if you're a side sleeper, have broader shoulders, or just dislike feeling cinched in while you sleep. If you're consistently camping in shoulder-season cold below freezing, look at WM's UltraLite (20°F) or Versalite (10°F) instead — this bag is happiest from the high 20s up into summer nights.

## Verdict

The MegaLite backs up its reputation with genuinely warmer-than-rated performance, smart use of extra room, and the kind of long-term loft retention that justifies the price tag over a decade of use. It's not cheap, and if budget is tight the REI Magma 30 is worth a look, but for hikers who've decided down is worth the investment, this is one of the best 30°F bags on the market. I'd rate it a 9/10 — docked slightly for price and the handful of zipper/hood complaints, not for anything related to core warmth or build quality.

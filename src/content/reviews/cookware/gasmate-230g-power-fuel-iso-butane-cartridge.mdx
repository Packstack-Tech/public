## Performance

Functionally, this canister does exactly what a screw-top iso-butane cartridge is supposed to do: it threads onto any EN417-compatible stove, seals well, and delivers consistent gas flow in mild-to-moderate temperatures. Gasmate builds these with what it calls <cite index="15-1,15-3">Twin Safety Devices and Triple Seamed Rim Technology</cite>, which is really an over-pressure safety mechanism — the <cite index="15-4,15-5">Rim Vent Release will activate in an over-pressure situation, allowing a controlled release of pressure</cite>, and the <cite index="15-6">Triple Seamed Rim holds the cartridge tighter and longer to allow the RVR device to activate securely</cite>. That's a nice bit of engineering you don't think about until you need it — it's designed to vent safely rather than fail catastrophically if a stove overheats the can.

The screw connection itself is a genuine plus over the older pierce-type canisters: <cite index="2-9">screw type connection for safety and easy operation, a very handy form of fully disposable fuel that can be moved from one appliance to another</cite>. In practice that means you can top off a partial canister onto a different stove or lantern without wasting fuel, which isn't the case with punctured-seal cartridges.

Where things get more nuanced is cold-weather performance, and this is where I'd temper expectations. Straight butane blends (and lower-grade iso-butane mixes) are notorious for losing pressure as the canister cools during use — the liquid fuel inside draws heat from its own vaporization, and on a frosty morning or extended burn you'll watch your flame shrink in real time. This isn't unique to Gasmate; it's physics that applies to any single-fuel butane canister. Aussie bushwalking forums have hashed this out extensively, with users note that isobutane/propane blends handle cold much better than straight butane and that <cite index="17-6">performance drops way down as the canister cools, so much so that some campers end up swapping in a warmer canister mid-cook</cite>. One forum poster even relayed a conversation with <cite index="17-10">Gasmate technical support, who said their Iso-butane + 2% propane canisters have the same operating characteristics as the standard mix</cite> — worth noting if you're comparing Gasmate's various canister lines, since propane content (even a small percentage) meaningfully helps cold starts.

Practically, that means: this canister is fine for three-season use, day hikes, and car camping where ambient temps stay above roughly 5°C. If you're heading into genuinely cold alpine conditions, keep the canister in your jacket or sleeping bag before firing it up, and consider carrying a spare — a trick several forum users mention is <cite index="17-17">gently pressing a foot on the side of the canister to deform it slightly, which reduces internal volume and can eke out a bit more pressure</cite> when you're down to fumes.

One general gripe that comes up around Gasmate-brand stoves and cartridges (not specific to this canister but worth flagging) is that some cheaper portable stove bodies paired with these cartridges don't fully empty the can, leaving unspent gas that complicates disposal — a concern raised independently by users reviewing Gasmate's compact stove units. That's more a stove design issue than a fault of the fuel itself, but it's a good reminder to always check a canister feels genuinely empty (shake it, no sloshing or hiss) before tossing it in general waste — these are not curbside recyclable and need to go through a proper gas cartridge recycling point.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Widely available across Australia — hardware stores, outdoor retailers, and even servos stock it
- Screw-top EN417 connection means no wasted gas moving between appliances
- Built-in over-pressure safety venting (RVR) and reinforced seam (TSR) add a genuine safety margin
- Straightforward, no-fuss compatibility with the vast majority of portable stoves and lanterns sold in Australia

**Cons**

- Pure iso-butane blend struggles in cold conditions compared to iso-butane/propane mixes — expect reduced output below ~5°C
- No printed burn-time rating on the canister or product page, so you're estimating based on your stove's burn rate rather than manufacturer data
- Disposal requires a specialised recycling drop-off, not general kerbside bins
- Not the lightest option gram-for-gram if you're chasing an ultralight base weight — smaller 100g cartridges exist for solo minimalist trips

## Who Should Buy This

This canister makes the most sense for weekend campers, car campers, and day hikers in Australia who want a reliable, easy-to-source fuel that they can grab from almost any hardware store or petrol station on the way to a trip. It's less ideal as a dedicated ultralight thru-hiking fuel source if you're optimizing every gram, and it's not the best pick for shoulder-season alpine trips where sub-zero mornings are likely — for those, look at an iso-butane/propane blend instead.

## Verdict

The Gasmate 230g Power Fuel Iso-Butane Cartridge is a solid, no-drama fuel canister that does its job well in mild conditions and benefits from genuinely useful safety engineering in the RVR venting system. It loses points against propane-blend rivals for cold-weather performance, but for the vast majority of three-season Australian camping and hiking, it's a dependable, easy-to-find choice. I'd rate it a 7/10 — reliable and safe, just not the fuel I'd grab for a frosty high-country trip.

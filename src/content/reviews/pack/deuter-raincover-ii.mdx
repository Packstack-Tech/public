## Overview

The Raincover II is Deuter's mid-size pack cover, sized for 30–50L backpacks — think weekend loads and light 3-season trekking packs rather than full expedition haulers. It's a simple, single-purpose piece of gear: a shaped nylon shell with a drawcord hem that stretches over your pack to shed rain and road spray. If you own a Deuter pack in that volume range (or any pack close to it) and want a cheap, purpose-built cover instead of a generic Amazon one, this is the obvious pick.

## Performance

I'll be upfront: dedicated long-term user reviews and forum threads specifically on the Raincover II are thin on the ground. It's the kind of accessory people buy, use, and never think to write about — which is arguably a compliment in itself. So most of what follows is grounded in the spec sheet, the broader Deuter rain cover line, and what I know from using similar taped-seam pack covers on trail, rather than a pile of Reddit anecdotes.

On the waterproofing side, the fundamentals are solid. <cite index="1-3">The waterproof Raincover II with PU coating and taped and welded seams comes in two colors and with reflective detailing for added safety</cite>. Welded seams are a step up from just taped seams on a lot of budget covers — there's no needle holes for water to find, which matters more than the fabric's denier rating in a sustained downpour. The 70D polyamide face fabric is on the beefier end for a pack cover (many ultralight-branded covers run 20–30D to save weight), so I'd expect it to shrug off abrasion from being stuffed in a hip pocket or dragged across a shuttle van floor better than the featherweight competition.

The trade-off for that durability is the 90g weight. That's not heavy in absolute terms, but it's noticeably more than dedicated ultralight covers (some Sea to Summit and Gossamer Gear options sit closer to 40–60g). If you're chasing base weight, that's 1-1.5 oz you're paying for ruggedness you may not need on a well-behaved 35L daypack.

Where pack covers as a category tend to fall short — and I'd expect the Raincover II is no exception — is at the harness and hip belt. A cover cinches around the body of the pack, but the back panel and shoulder straps stay exposed, and rain runs down your back and gets flung up from below by ricochet. In genuinely miserable, sideways rain, a cover alone won't keep your gear bone dry; pairing it with an internal pack liner (a contractor bag or a dedicated dry bag liner) is still the belt-and-suspenders approach serious thru-hikers tend to land on. The cover's real strength is blocking direct rainfall and keeping your pack fabric itself from soaking up water and adding weight — not sealing your gear in a drysuit.

The drawcord hem is standard fare — functional, but on packs with a lot of external strapping, side pockets, or an ice axe loop, expect some fiddling to get a snug fit without leaving gaps. Deuter doesn't include an adjustable strap system here (some competitors add a chest strap or bungee to keep the cover from ballooning or blowing off in wind), so in gusty conditions you may find yourself re-seating it.

The reflective detailing is a nice, low-cost touch for road walking, trailhead parking lots, or hunting season visibility, and it costs nothing in performance.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros:**

- Taped and welded seams — genuinely better sealing than taped-only budget covers
- 70D fabric is tougher than most ultralight-class covers, better resistance to snags and abrasion
- PFAS-free DWR finish, a small but real environmental upgrade over older formulations
- Reflective detailing adds visibility for road or low-light use
- Inexpensive relative to boutique ultralight cottage-brand covers

**Cons:**

- 90g is on the heavier side for this pack-volume class
- No chest strap or bungee system to lock down fit in wind
- Like all external covers, doesn't protect the back panel/harness or fully stop wind-driven rain
- Sparse third-party/long-term user reviews available to verify durability claims over hundreds of trail days

## Who Should Buy This

This is a solid pick for hikers with a 30–50L pack who want a cover that's built a notch tougher than the disposable generic options, and who don't mind the small weight penalty in exchange for welded seams and a more durable fabric. It's less of a fit for grams-counters running a sub-2-lb base weight who'd rather save the ounces and rely on an in-pack liner alone, or for anyone whose pack falls outside the 30–50L window — check the Raincover I or III for smaller or larger loads.

## Verdict

The Raincover II does exactly what a pack cover should: keep casual rain off your gear without fuss, using genuinely well-sealed construction. It's not the lightest option out there, and it won't replace a liner bag in a real storm, but as a straightforward, durable accessory for mid-size packs, it earns a solid 7/10.

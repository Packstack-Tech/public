## Overview

Trekking-pole shelters have a built-in problem: the people who like them most are not always people who carry poles. Six Moon Designs' answer is a fixed-length, five-section carbon fiber pole, custom made by Easton Mountain Products, that stands in for a trekking pole under the company's tarps and tents. It is aimed at cyclists, paddlers, and trail runners. The trade-off is that it's a single-purpose piece of hardware that costs $50 and does nothing but hold up a roof.

## Performance

The headline figure is the weight. At 60 g for the 45-inch version, the pole weighs about as much as a few tent stakes and well under the 99 g of Six Moon Designs' own 5-section aluminum pole. Backpacking Light forum members have called the brand's carbon poles lighter than other poles of comparable length for this job.

The five-section construction is the other selling point. Each section is 11 inches, so the folded pole is 28 cm long. That fits inside a bike frame bag, a packraft dry bag, or the side pocket of a small running pack. Six Moon Designs' 3-section carbon pole is lighter and cheaper, but it folds to 17 inches. The company pitches the 5-section for bikepacking and other space-limited trips.

Strength is the usual question with carbon, and the manufacturer's claim is that these poles won't shatter in strong winds. Owners on Six Moon Designs' product page describe them as sturdy and packable. One buyer who bought a pair for a Skyscape Scout was happy with the result. Another noted that black carbon is hard to find in the dark and added reflective tape. That's a cheap fix, and worth doing before the first midnight trip out of the vestibule.

Carbon flexes, and the company says so up front. Its setup advice is to tighten the canopy until the pole bows slightly, then back off until it stands vertical. That is enough tension to hold a pitch in high wind, according to Six Moon Designs. A pole that's cranked too hard is more likely to fail than one pitched with slight slack. On Backpacking Light, one commenter observed that a straight center pole in a tarp or pyramid-style pitch is loaded mainly in compression. In that use, a stiff carbon tube does well. Users who expect serious storms often upgrade to stiffer poles and add stakes and guylines, so the pole is not the only variable to adjust.

There is a side use as well. One Backpacking Light participant passed along the suggestion that it works as an emergency trekking pole for stream crossings. That's a secondhand claim, and a 10 mm five-section pole with no grip is a poor substitute for a real one.

## Choosing a Version

Length is the only thing that changes, and the choice is dictated by the shelter.

**45 in (114 cm, 60 g):** the pole for the Skyscape Trekker and Scout, Lunar Duo Outfitter and Explorer, Haven Tarp and Haven Zero-G, Haven NetTent, and Owyhee. Most of those shelters need two poles, so a single pole is not enough. The Serenity NetTent uses one.

**49 in (125 cm, 64 g):** the pole for the Lunar Solo, the Deschutes Tarp, Deschutes Plus, and Deschutes Zero-G, each using one pole. The Gatewood Cape is listed for both lengths.

Buying the wrong length means a sagging or overtight pitch. Check the shelter's setup page first, and count the poles it needs.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 60–64 g, roughly 35–40 g lighter than the aluminum version of the same pole
- Folds to 28 cm, small enough for bike frame bags and packrafts
- Easton-made carbon, which the manufacturer says won't shatter in strong winds
- Matched to specific Six Moon Designs shelters, with a clear compatibility list

**Cons**

- $50 each, and most two-pole shelters need two
- Flexes by design; pitching takes a light touch
- Black finish is hard to see at night without reflective tape
- Useful only as shelter support, with no grip or trekking function

## Who Should Buy This

Anyone who owns a Six Moon Designs trekking-pole shelter and doesn't hike with poles: cyclists, paddlers, and trail runners above all. It also makes sense for hikers who want to leave their poles at home on a short trip. Hikers who already carry trekking poles gain nothing from it and should skip it. Anyone pitching in exposed alpine terrain should consider the aluminum pole for its stiffness, or carry real poles.

## Verdict

For the people it's made for, this pole does what it says: it adds 60 to 64 g, packs to 28 cm, and holds up a Six Moon Designs shelter. At $50 each it is pricey for a plain tube, but nothing else in the catalog weighs this little. Buy it if your hands are empty on the trail, and leave it if they aren't.

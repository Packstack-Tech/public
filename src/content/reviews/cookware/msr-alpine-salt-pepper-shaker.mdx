## Overview

The MSR Alpine Salt & Pepper Shaker is a thumb-sized plastic tube split down the middle, with a flip lid at each end and a rubber gasket on every opening. It is aimed at backpackers who want seasoning without carrying a kitchen-drawer shaker or a handful of fast-food packets. The trade-off is capacity: it is built for a few days of solo cooking, not for a group or a month between resupplies.

## Performance

The Alpine's reputation rests on its seals. Each end carries a snap-closure lid with a gasket, and a second, larger gasket seals the lid to the body. A long-running review at What's In Your Pack singled this out as the difference between this shaker and others that let ambient humidity turn salt into a solid block. Owners on retailer sites repeat the point: lids that fit snugly, no spills in a pack, and no moisture creeping in on damp trips. One reviewer who carried it climbing, backpacking and car camping reported no problems.

Size is where opinions split, and the split is mostly about expectations. At under a third of an ounce and about the length of a finger, it slips into a cook pot or a side pocket without being noticed. Reviewers describe it as enough for a multi-day trip. One put a solo hiker at several weeks of seasoning and a group of two or three at several days. A 3-to-5-day pack hunt was also called comfortable. Others found it tight beyond a weekend or two nights. Both camps agree it is small. A zip bag, one European reviewer noted, is the more practical choice for long travel.

The dispensing holes are small, and that suits fine table salt and ground pepper. It does not suit coarse salt. One Backcountry reviewer gave the design a single star because flake salt would not come out of the openings. Another owner who fills it with sea salt and pepper said it never clogged. Fill it with something finely ground and it behaves.

Durability is good so far, with one caveat. Long-term users flag the plastic hinges on the lids as the likely failure point. One owner who had used the shaker almost daily for two or three years, in the field and at a desk, expected the hinges to give out eventually but treated that as an acceptable cost. A second, who opened and closed the lids 10 to 16 times per baked potato at lunch, saw no damage after months but expected some. Users also note a small handling quirk: pop the flip top, not the entire lid, or the gasketed cap comes off in your hand.

The divider gives the shaker more range than its name suggests. Owners have loaded one side with Old Bay and the other with salt. Another pairs salt with a cinnamon-sugar mix for oatmeal. Fish skin, instant potatoes and freeze-dried dinners all get the same treatment.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- At 8.5 g, it is about as light as a seasoning container gets
- Gaskets on every opening keep moisture out, and owners report no clumping
- Two chambers hold two seasonings without mixing them
- Fits inside a cook pot or small pocket
- Backed by a limited 3-year warranty

**Cons**

- Small capacity; solo hikers on long stretches and groups will run out quickly
- Tiny openings will not pass flake or coarse salt
- Plastic lid hinges are the expected wear point
- Lids can be fiddly to open without pulling off the whole cap

## Who Should Buy This

This suits solo backpackers and weekend cooks who season dinner with fine salt and pepper, or with a pairing like salt and a spice blend. It is a low-cost, nearly weightless upgrade over packets. It is a poor choice for groups or for cooks who use flaky or coarse salt. Hikers who want to minimize pack weight still further can decant seasoning into a small zip bag, which costs less and weighs less, but gives up the gaskets and the divider.

## Verdict

The Alpine does one small job well. It keeps two seasonings separate and dry for 8.5 g, and it has worked for years of daily use. Buy it if your seasoning is fine-ground and your trips are short to medium. Pick a larger or coarser-friendly shaker for groups and flake salt.

## Overview

The Beta LT is Arc'teryx's stripped-down, all-round hardshell: a 3-layer GORE-TEX jacket that weighs 394 g (13.9 oz) and carries the pared-back feature list its name suggests. It suits hikers and thru-hikers who want one shell that survives alpine weather, brush and years of use. The trade-off is plain. It weighs more than 2.5-layer ultralight rain jackets and costs far more than most of them.

## Performance

**Weather protection.** The Beta LT's case rests on its laminate. A 40D nylon face bonded to a GORE-TEX membrane and a tricot backer gives a fabric rated to 28,000 mm, with taped seams and a DWR finish on the outside. Reviewers who put it through rain, sleet and wet snow report no leaks, and testers at The Wise Adventurer called it excellent for weatherproofing and build quality. The fabric is thin but not soft. One UK reviewer found that slight stiffness welcome in a winter blizzard, and a Trailspace owner who wore an earlier Beta LT for five years in rain and snow reported no problems.

**Breathability.** Pit zips arrived in this generation of the jacket, and testers consistently count them as a real upgrade over earlier Beta LTs, which had none. They are described as easy to operate. The laminate is rated around 17,000 g/m² per 24 hours, and the narrow seam tape and small glued area are designed to help it breathe. As with any waterproof-breathable shell, hard climbing in warm weather still means sweating. The zips and the wide cuffs help dump heat.

**Weight and packability.** At 394 g, the jacket weighs about 2 oz less than the heavier Beta AR, according to The Wise Adventurer. That site also found it just over half an ounce heavier than the Rab Downpour 2.0, a 2.5-layer shell, a gap that is smaller than many hikers would guess. It packs down into its own hood with little effort. One reviewer was disappointed that it doesn't stow into a pocket, so it lives in the pack as a stuff-sack-size lump rather than a clip-on accessory.

**Fit and mobility.** The cut is fitted, with articulated patterning, a drop hem and dual hem adjusters. Owners say the sleeves run long, which helps keep wrists covered when reaching. A few reviewers advise sizing up if a fleece or puffy goes underneath, since the jacket runs trim. The StormHood has full coverage and doesn't block peripheral vision, and it fits over a helmet.

**Features and shortcomings.** Two zippered hand pockets sit high enough to clear a pack hip belt. Some testers wanted a hand-warmer pocket that sits lower for everyday wear. The Gore-Tex pocket bags keep rain out even if water gets past the zips. There is no internal stash pocket, which GearJunkie flagged as a miss despite the weight it would add. The Velcro cuff tabs are die-cut to cut bulk and avoid snagging.

**Durability.** This is where the 3-layer build pays off. Testers describe it as tougher than lightweight shells that catch and tear on rock and brush, and owners report years of use without delamination or wear problems. The step down from GORE-TEX Pro to standard 3L GORE-TEX on recent models means it is not a full mountaineering or resort-skiing shell. For hiking and backpacking, reviewers say that makes no practical difference.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 3-layer GORE-TEX holds up in sustained rain and wet snow
- Pit zips and wide cuffs for venting
- Sturdy 40D face fabric resists snags better than ultralight shells
- Helmet-compatible hood with good visibility
- PFC-free DWR, taped seams and WaterTight zippers
- Trim, articulated cut moves well under a pack

**Cons**

- About 394 g, heavier than 2.5-layer UL shells like the Rab Downpour 2.0
- Priced around $450 in reviews, steep for a rain jacket
- No interior pocket
- Doesn't stuff into its own pocket
- Fitted cut and high pockets don't suit everyone

## Who Should Buy This

The Beta LT fits hikers and thru-hikers who want one durable shell for storms, shoulder-season trips and the occasional scramble, and who would rather own one jacket for ten years than replace a delicate one every season. It also works well for people who need a hood that goes over a helmet. Gram-counters who carry a rain jacket mainly as insurance, and hikers doing dry-climate trips, should look at lighter 2.5-layer shells. Anyone expecting full-on mountaineering or resort-ski toughness should consider the heavier Beta AR.

## Verdict

The Beta LT is a well-built, trim 3-layer shell that handles prolonged wet weather and rough use better than most jackets near its weight. It is expensive and no ultralight record-setter, but its durability and added ventilation make it a sound long-term buy. It earns an 8 out of 10.

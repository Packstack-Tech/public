## Overview

The Questar 20F/-6C is Therm-a-Rest's mid-priced, feature-heavy down mummy for three-season camping and backpacking. It fills a generous cut with 650-fill Nikwax Hydrophobic Down and adds a pad-strapping system that keeps sleepers from migrating off their mattress. The headline trade-off: it's a comfort bag first and a gram-shaver second.

## Performance

**Warmth.** The numbers on the tag need reading carefully. The 20°F figure is the lower limit, the point at which a cold sleeper is expected to be curled up and shivering. The comfort rating sits at 32°F/0°C. Owners on retail sites who bought the bag for sub-freezing nights are the ones most likely to be disappointed, and one reviewer put it bluntly: this is a three-season bag. Elevation Outdoors noted that the bag's comfortable floor of roughly 31°F falls just short of some early-spring and late-fall trips. A reviewer who sleeps cold found the 30s to be the sweet spot, while a UK tester reported using it in Ireland from roughly -4°C to 18°C across several seasons.

Warmth for the weight comes from design choices. Insulation is concentrated on top and sides, with less under the body, where it would be crushed anyway. ThermaCapture seams, a draft collar, a cinchable hood and a dedicated insulated foot pocket round out the heat-retention kit. The zoned approach has a catch: a sleeper who rolls over inside the bag, rather than with it, reported feeling the thin underside, and the bag asks for a pad with a respectable R-value to make up the difference.

**Comfort and fit.** This is where the Questar earns its reputation. Therm-a-Rest's W.A.R.M. fit (With Additional Room for Multiple positions) gives side and stomach sleepers space to move. Regular-size dimensions run 203 cm long, with 160 cm shoulder girth, 155 cm hip girth and 117 cm footbox girth. Reviewers describe it as roomy without feeling drafty, and side sleepers are among its most vocal fans. Reviewers across Backcountry and Dick's listings repeatedly call it the most comfortable bag they've owned. The slick 20D polyester taffeta lining is soft against skin.

**SynergyLink connectors.** Two stretchy straps hide behind a small zipper on the bag's underside and loop beneath a sleeping pad. On a slick air mattress, they stop the nightly slide that leaves a shoulder hanging off the edge. Owners credit them with keeping the bag in place and preventing cold spots. The limit is pad width: one owner found the straps too tight on a 64 cm-wide NeoAir XLite and worried about tearing the bag, having previously used a 53 cm pad without trouble. The straps are removable for those who'd rather skip them.

**Wet-weather behavior.** Nikwax Hydrophobic Down treatment resists moisture better than untreated fill. One Elevation Outdoors tester reported it held up on a 15°F night when condensation soaked everything else in the back of a 4Runner. That's a useful margin for tent-wall condensation, though it's not a license to sleep in a puddle. A DWR-treated 20D shell backs it up.

**Weight and packability.** At 1,000 g for the Standard, the Questar is light for a 20°F bag with this much room, yet it isn't a gram-counter's bag. Live For The Outdoors' testers, comparing it to the Rab Neutrino 400, called it far from heavy but not a true lightweight, while noting it costs £125 less than that bag with comparable stats. The 650 fill is the reason: lower-lofting down means more mass and bulk than 800-fill competitors at the same warmth. Packed size is 9 x 14 in, compact enough for most packs. The bag ships with both a mesh storage sack and a smaller stuff sack. Reviewers praised the large zipper's ease of use, including with gloves, and a snag-free pull.

## Choosing a Version

The two versions share the same insulation, rating and features. What changes is length and 88 g.

- **Standard (1,000 g):** Fits sleepers up to 6 ft. The right choice for most people under that height, and the lighter option.
- **Long (1,088 g):** Fits up to 6 ft 6 in. Taller sleepers should take it, since squeezing feet against the footbox compresses the insulation and creates cold spots. Shorter sleepers gain nothing from the extra 88 g and extra volume to heat.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Roomy W.A.R.M. cut suits side and stomach sleepers
- SynergyLink straps keep the bag centered on the pad
- Hydrophobic down shrugs off condensation better than untreated fill
- Draft collar, insulated foot pocket and cinchable hood
- Priced well below many comparable down bags

**Cons**

- 650-fill down makes it heavier and bulkier than ultralight competition
- 32°F comfort rating limits it to three-season use
- Zoned insulation leaves a thin underside; a warm pad is essential
- Connectors can be too snug on wide pads

## Who Should Buy This

This bag suits backpackers who value sleep quality over base weight: side sleepers, restless sleepers, and weekend and section hikers who want a warm, forgiving bag without paying ultralight prices. It also works well for three-season car camping and shoulder-season trips in the Southeast or Pacific Northwest, where damp nights are common. Thru-hikers counting every ounce, cold sleepers who expect to hit freezing temperatures regularly, and anyone seeking a true winter bag should look at a higher-fill quilt or a bag with a lower comfort rating.

## Verdict

The Questar is a comfortable, well-featured, fairly priced three-season down bag that does what it promises as long as buyers read the comfort rating rather than the headline number. It's not a leader in weight, but it's a good bag for people who actually want to sleep. Recommended for the roomy-bag crowd; skip it if the scale matters more than the mattress.

## Overview

The NEMO Hornet is a semi-freestanding, double-wall backpacking tent that packs two doors and two vestibules into a shelter weighing 851 g. It has long been the default answer for hikers who want a conventional tent with real storage and side entry, without paying a big weight penalty. The trade-off is interior geometry: it sleeps one comfortably, two intimately, and anyone over six feet will notice the sloping walls.

## Performance

**Weight and packability.** The Hornet's appeal starts with a simple comparison: double-wall tents with two doors and two vestibules rarely get this light. CleverHiker's testers called out the combination as uncommon in this weight class, and Backpackers.com described it as extremely light for a two-person shelter, adding that going lighter means giving up meaningful features. The packed bundle is a long, slim roll, roughly 19.5 inches by 5.5 inches, which slides down the side of a pack more easily than it stuffs into the bottom. NEMO's Divvy dual-stage stuff sack lets a pair split body and fly or poles between two packs.

**Setup.** A single hub pole forms the main structure. The head end stands on its own, while the two foot corners need stakes to pull the shape out and to pitch the fly properly. Reviewers describe the process as quick and intuitive, and the semi-freestanding design means soft or rocky ground is less of a problem than with a fully non-freestanding shelter. Pitching it freestanding in a pinch is possible, but testers advise against counting on it in weather.

**Livability.** NEMO's Flybar clips, which replace conventional body clips on the center pole, push the walls outward and add head room. That helps, but 39 inches of peak height is on the low side, and reviewers consistently mention the slope of the walls. A six-foot tester at Blister found the layout workable; CleverHiker's 6'2" tester found both the length and the peak too tight, and a 5'11" reviewer at Backwoods Pursuit reported having to choose between head or feet touching fabric. Owners generally treat the Hornet as a roomy one-person tent with a big gear footprint. For two, the foot narrows to 43 inches, which fits two 20-inch pads but leaves little margin, and Backpackers.com characterized capacity as closer to one and a half people. Sleeping partners who like each other will be fine. Everyone else should look at the next size up in the NEMO range.

**Doors and vestibules.** This is where the Hornet earns its keep. Each side gets its own door and a 7.1 sq ft vestibule, so nobody climbs over anyone at 2 a.m., and boots and packs stay dry on separate sides. Reviewers call the vestibules small but usable. The D-shaped doors are modest in size, though they do the job.

**Weather and ventilation.** The mesh inner and gap between fly and body keep air moving, and Blister noted that the tent breathes well, particularly at the head end, despite lacking adjustable vents. Double-wall construction keeps condensation off sleeping bags provided the fly is tensioned correctly. In wind and rain, testers describe it as a competent three-season shelter, but not one for exposed ridgelines above treeline or for winter. The 10D fly and thin floor reward careful site selection; a footprint is a sensible add-on for rough ground, and owners who skip one report that the floor is the first place wear shows up. One reviewer also notes the floor can let a small amount of moisture through in very wet conditions.

**Durability.** The ultralight fabrics are the compromise behind the weight. Treated with silicone on the outside and polyurethane underneath, the fly is tough for its denier, but it is not a tent to drag over talus or pitch on gravel without protection. NEMO backs the Hornet with its lifetime warranty.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 851 g for a double-wall tent with two doors and two vestibules
- Quick, intuitive pitch from a single DAC Featherlite pole
- Flybar clips add useful head room at the top of the tent
- Side entry and separate vestibules make solo and two-person use more livable
- Good airflow and condensation management for a double-wall design
- Slim packed shape and a Divvy sack for splitting loads

**Cons**

- Tight for two; the foot narrows to 43 in
- 39 in peak height and sloping walls are a poor fit for hikers over six feet
- Foot corners must be staked for a proper pitch
- Thin fabrics need a careful campsite and likely a footprint
- Limited for high-wind or winter use

## Who Should Buy This

The Hornet suits solo thru-hikers and weekend backpackers who want a double-wall tent with space for gear and an easy pitch. It also works for couples who value weight over elbow room and sleep on narrow pads. Tall hikers, couples who want breathing room, and anyone planning to camp above treeline in serious weather should look at something longer, taller or sturdier.

## Verdict

The Hornet delivers features that are usually reserved for heavier tents, at a weight few double-wall competitors match, and it does so with a straightforward pitch. Buy it as a generous solo shelter or a deliberately cozy two-person one, and plan on a footprint. The rating reflects that strong weight-to-features ratio, offset by a cramped interior and fragile fabrics.

## Overview

A canister stove with a full pot of water on top is a tall, narrow tower balanced on a can about the width of a soup tin. The MSR Universal Canister Stand fixes that with a three-legged stainless steel and ABS plastic base that clamps to the bottom of the canister and folds flat when it isn't needed. It is a single-purpose, 34 g accessory aimed at anyone who cooks on uneven ground. The trade-off is price: $25.95 buys a piece of bent steel and a spring.

## Performance

The stand's job is to grow the footprint under a canister, and owners say it does that well. A Trailspace reviewer who has used three different MSR canister stands, including the folding triangles bundled with Windburner systems, ranked this one best. The reviewer measured a footprint radius of roughly 13 cm and a weight of 32.9 g, and described it as attaching solidly to the canister. The Windburner's bundled stands, by comparison, can be tippy on small canisters or fiddly to stretch into place.

The mechanism is simple. One arm carries a spring-loaded latch that grips the lip at the base of the canister, and the other two legs fold out to complete the tripod. Reviewers on REI describe the grip as firm enough that the canister doesn't wriggle, and they report it works with MSR, Coleman, Jetboil and Snow Peak canisters in both small and large sizes. One owner who had tipped a stove with a heavier pot before buying the stand reported no further incidents over three straight weeks of backpacking. The legs also have notches that bite into rock and keep the stand from skating around.

Stability under load is the real test. Owners cooking with a 16 oz pot of water on a narrow canister call the setup top-heavy without the stand, and say a gust of wind is far less likely to topple it with the stand attached. Backcountry's testers say it hides inside a solo pot and counts as an obvious addition to the cook kit. One reviewer noted a small amount of vertical play when pressing down on an attached canister. That is normal for something this light, and it didn't cause problems in use.

Durability is mostly good. Owners describe a decade of use on a single stand. One critical REI review suspects the spring that holds the plastic clamp against the canister lost some force over time, so the latch is the part to watch. The steel legs can also scratch a non-stick pot, so it is better stowed in a cloth or outside the cook set.

Compatibility has one real gap. A UK buyer found the stand did nothing for the small, tall 175 g resealable canisters, which are taller than they are wide, and felt the "universal" label overreached. Owners using standard 100 g, 230 g and 450 g-class isobutane canisters report no such trouble.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Wide, rigid tripod base with a footprint radius of about 13 cm
- Spring-loaded latch holds the canister with no wobble, and adjusts to different diameters
- Folds to 5.4 x 1 x 0.8 in and fits inside a solo pot
- Stainless steel construction that owners report lasting for years
- Notched legs grip rock and uneven surfaces

**Cons**

- $25.95 is steep for a 34 g piece of metal
- Heavier than the plastic-and-silicone stands that come with some stove systems
- Won't help with tall, narrow 175 g canisters
- The spring clamp is the likely wear point
- Steel legs can scratch non-stick cookware

## Who Should Buy This

This is for backpackers who cook directly off a canister, such as PocketRocket-style or Jetboil-style setups, and who pitch kitchens on dirt, rock and sloped campsites. It suits anyone pairing a canister stove with a larger pot, where the center of gravity climbs fast. Travelers who buy whatever canister is available locally will like the adjustable fit.

Gram-counting hikers who cook on flat ground or in a vestibule with a small pot can skip it. So can anyone who already has a bundled stand that works. Solo hikers with a 100 g canister and a mug-sized pot are the people least likely to miss it.

## Verdict

The MSR Universal Canister Stand is a small, well-liked fix for a real problem, and the 34 g it adds is easy to justify the first time a pot of dinner stays upright on a slope. The price is the only real complaint. Buy it if tipped stoves have been a problem, and skip it if they haven't.

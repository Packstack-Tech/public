## Overview

The Fire Maple FMS-300T, sold in some markets as the Hornet or Wasp, is a 45 g canister-top stove that folds to roughly the size of a lipstick tube. It is aimed at solo backpackers and thru-hikers who boil water and rehydrate meals, and who want a burner that survives a season of being jammed into a pot. The trade-off is that it isn't the lightest stove of its type. The BRS-3000T weighs less and costs about half as much. In exchange, the Fire Maple offers sturdier hardware and better flame control.

## Performance

**Boil speed.** Fire Maple rates the FMS-300T at 3 minutes 50 seconds for a liter of water. Field results vary with pot and conditions. One reviewer in South Africa, testing in still air, took about five minutes per liter. A Ukrainian tester pairing the stove with a heat-exchanger pot recorded 3:38 and about 9 g of gas per liter. A US buyer reported a rapid boil of roughly two quarts in four to five minutes. For a 2.6 kW stove, those numbers are typical of the class. Buyers who expect bigger-burner speed from a 45 g stove will be disappointed.

**Flame control.** This is where the stove separates itself from the cheapest ultralight burners. Owners report that it simmers properly, and one Ukrainian reviewer described the valve as smooth from a barely-there flame up to full roar, even though the valve knob is small. That makes it more than a boil-only burner. Rice, couscous and the occasional pan of something that can scorch are within its range.

**Stability and build.** The pot supports are the main difference from the BRS-3000T, the stove it is most often measured against. Thru-hikers who carry both note that the Fire Maple uses thicker stainless supports where the BRS has thin titanium ones. One owner said the Fire Maple is the one that goes along when the stove has to be bulletproof on an extended hike. Open, the supports span about 8 cm, which holds a typical solo mug or 600–900 ml pot but not a wide pan. A buyer noted the supports are slightly stiff to fold and unfold, which is a minor complaint that tends to ease with use. Three Points of the Compass praised the strengthened, skeletonised supports for keeping small pots steady.

**Wind and efficiency.** The burner head has no louvered flame spreader of the kind the BRS uses, and there is no pressure regulator. Both are reasons it won't be the most efficient stove on a windy ridge. Reviewers suggest carrying a strip of stiff foil as a windscreen. Never wrap it tightly around a canister-mounted stove, because the heat can overheat the canister.

**Pack fit.** The folded stove fits easily inside nesting pots and mugs. Owners routinely stack it with a 110 g canister and a lighter inside a 0.85 L pot. The stove also works with an LPG adapter for Coleman-style bottles, according to the manufacturer.

**Durability notes.** The valve body is aluminum, and there is no threaded brass insert where it meets the canister. That means a gentle hand is needed when tightening. Hand-tight is enough. Reviewer feedback skews strongly positive. The manufacturer's own page shows a 4.59 average from 32 ratings, while one aggregator of retail reviews reports that some buyers, roughly a quarter, had quality problems. The occasional dud appears to come from unit-to-unit variation, not from a design weakness.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 45 g and a 37 × 52 mm folded footprint disappear into any cookset
- Genuine simmer control, unusual at this weight
- Thick stainless pot supports are steadier than titanium-arm rivals
- Around $30, with a well-established record among long-distance hikers
- Works with an LPG adapter

**Cons**

- Heavier than the BRS-3000T and not the most compact stove on the market
- No flame spreader or regulator, so wind and cold hurt efficiency
- Aluminum valve block has no brass insert, so over-tightening is a risk
- Pot supports can be stiff to fold
- Open span of roughly 8 cm is tight for wide pots

## Who Should Buy This

Solo hikers and thru-hikers who cook one-pot meals, carry a small mug or pot of 900 ml or less, and want a stove they won't have to nurse. It suits those who value a believable simmer and stable supports and are willing to carry a few more grams than the absolute minimum. It is a poor fit for gram-counters who will accept thin pot supports in exchange for a lighter stove, and for groups cooking in big pots. Winter and high-wind users should look at a regulated or remote-canister stove instead.

## Verdict

The FMS-300T is a sensible choice if you want the weight of an ultralight burner without its fragility. It isn't the lightest or the most efficient stove, but it is durable, compact and controllable, and it costs about $30. It is a strong default for a solo canister-stove kit.

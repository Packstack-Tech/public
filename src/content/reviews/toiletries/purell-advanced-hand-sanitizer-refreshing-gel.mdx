## Overview

PURELL Advanced Refreshing Gel in the 1 fl oz flip-cap bottle is the drugstore default for a reason: a 70% ethyl alcohol formula, a bottle that disappears into a hip-belt pocket, and a refill at nearly any trailhead town. It suits hikers who want a no-water hand-cleaning option after the privy and before meals. The trade-off is that sanitizer kills germs but doesn't remove dirt, and the plastic bottle is a few grams heavier than a bare-bones repackaged dose.

## Performance

**Germ kill.** The formula is 70% ethyl alcohol, a concentration above the 60% floor commonly cited for effective sanitizers. A gel base also tends to stay put on the hand rather than running off the palm. Commenters on Section Hiker's backpacking-sanitizer discussion note that ethanol at 70% or higher with a thickener outperforms the thinner 62.5% products common in the travel aisle. PURELL's marketing claims it kills 99.99 percent of most common germs, and the brand says one squirt does the work of two from other national brands. The company's own comparisons are worth treating as marketing. The practical takeaway is that a pea-to-nickel-sized dose is enough, which stretches a 1 oz bottle further than the volume suggests.

**On-trail feel.** Users on retail sites consistently describe it as quick-drying and not sticky, with a mild scent that isn't overpowering. The four moisturizers matter more on a long trip than in a day pack. Cracked knuckles are a real nuisance on a thru-hike, and alcohol-heavy gels are not kind to dry mountain air.

**The flip-cap.** The cap seals tightly, and one-handed operation is easy, which counts when the other hand is holding a trowel or a water filter. Retailers describe the lid as spill-resistant. Squeeze bottles in a pack do get pressure from every side, so hikers who carry it in a food bag or pot should still keep it upright or bagged.

**Where sanitizer falls short.** Princeton's outdoor program points out the basic limitation: with no rinsing action, a waterless sanitizer mostly moves dirt around rather than removing it. Hikers on SectionHiker and Backpacking Light often carry a few drops of concentrated soap alongside the sanitizer for that reason, and treat the gel as the fast option for after filtering water, after the privy, or before eating. One Appalachian Trail thru-hiker on Backpacking Light split the load between two small bottles, one with the food bag and one with the toilet paper. That's a sensible habit: the bottle that lives next to the trowel is the one that gets used.

**Weight-saving angle.** Experienced hikers rarely fill the whole bottle. Adventure Alan's gear list advises carrying a 1 oz container only 25–50 percent full for most trips. Hikers who repackage from a larger bottle into 1–2 oz squeeze bottles, as Section Hiker's writer does, can shave bottle weight further, but the PURELL package is already compact and doesn't need decanting.

**Travel logistics.** At 1 fl oz, the bottle sits well under the 3.4 oz carry-on liquid limit, which helps for flights to the trailhead. It is a flammable alcohol product, so suppliers list it as hazmat for shipping, and the brand's retail availability makes that rarely an issue for a hiker walking into a pharmacy.

**Cost.** Single bottles run around $1.59 at office-supply retail. Case pricing works out to roughly $1.12–$1.17 per ounce at restaurant-supply sites, which is only relevant for hiking groups or clubs buying in bulk.

## Choosing a Version

Both versions hold the same 1 fl oz of the same 70% ethyl alcohol gel in a flip-cap bottle. The difference is the packaging listing and the weight: the Travel Size Flip-Cap Bottle is 28 g, while the Flip-Cap Bottle is 34 g, a 6 g gap.

- **Travel Size Flip-Cap Bottle (28 g):** The pick for gram-counters. Over a base weight that's already been trimmed to the bone, 6 g is small, but it costs nothing.
- **Flip-Cap Bottle (34 g):** A fine choice if it's what's on the shelf. For weekend trips or hikers not tracking grams, the difference won't be noticed.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 70% ethyl alcohol in a gel that stays on the hand
- 28–34 g and 3.36 in tall, easy to pocket or clip near the privy kit
- One-handed flip-cap with a tight seal
- Four moisturizers; free of triclosan, parabens, phthalates and preservatives
- Sold in nearly every trail town, so resupply is trivial
- 1 oz size clears airline carry-on limits

**Cons**

- Sanitizer doesn't remove dirt, so it doesn't replace soap and water
- Scented, which some hikers avoid around food and in bear country
- A rigid retail bottle adds weight compared with a lighter repackaged dose
- Flammable alcohol product; not a fuel-adjacent item to be careless with
- Pricier per ounce than store-brand or bulk gels

## Who Should Buy This

Section hikers, thru-hikers and weekend backpackers who want a dependable, easy-to-find sanitizer for use after filtering water, after the privy, and before meals. Gram-focused hikers should pick the 28 g travel-size bottle and fill it only partway. Anyone traveling in a group where stomach bugs are a concern, or hiking in sticky, dirty conditions like muddy trail or desert grit, should carry a small amount of soap too, since sanitizer alone won't clean visibly dirty hands. Hikers sensitive to fragrance should look at an unscented gel instead.

## Verdict

PURELL's 1 fl oz gel is a dependable, light, easy-to-replace piece of kit that does one job well: quickly sanitizing reasonably clean hands. It earns its few grams on any trip with water filtering and privy stops, but it works best paired with a few drops of soap, not as a replacement.

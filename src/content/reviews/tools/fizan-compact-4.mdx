## Overview

The Fizan Compact 4 is a four-section, twist-lock aluminum trekking pole made in Italy by a company that has been building poles since 1947. It weighs 169 g per pole and folds to 51 cm, small enough to disappear into a pack or a carry-on. The trade-off is a pole that is a touch heavier and a touch shorter at full extension than its three-section sibling, the Compact 3, and one that wants a little more care than carbon.

## Performance

**Weight and swing.** Aluminum poles rarely sit in the same conversation as carbon on weight, but the Compact series is the exception. Thru-hikers and reviewers at The Trek note that the Compact 4 is about 0.4 oz per pole heavier than the Compact 3, a gap that disappears on the trail. Early owners of the Compact 4 described the weight as right in the middle: light enough that arms don't tire over a long day, but with enough heft to hold a steady tic-toc rhythm rather than flutter. Backpacking Light forum members have pointed out that the Fizan series runs roughly 1.5 oz per pole heavier than boutique cottage-brand carbon and titanium poles, at about half the price.

**Packability.** This is the reason to pick the four-section version. At 51 cm collapsed, the Compact 4 fits inside a 20-liter daypack without poking above the lid, and it travels in checked or carry-on luggage where a longer folded pole would not. Reviewers at The Trek and elsewhere agree that the Compact 4 packs slightly smaller than the Compact 3, and that both are among the most compact poles at any price.

**Locking system.** The Flexy twist-lock has no external ferrules or levers. The mechanism sits inside the tube, which keeps weight and snag points down. Owners generally report that it holds under load, including on boulder fields and off-trail scrambling, but the lock is the one part forum threads criticize. Fizan warns against over-tightening, which can damage the internal expander, so the technique is firm but not forceful.

**Length accuracy and shelter use.** The Compact 4 tops out at 125 cm, and testers at The Trek found that at a given nominal length, it reads slightly shorter than the Compact 3. Owners who pitch trekking-pole tents report that a 125 cm setting produces roughly a 48-inch pole in practice. That is short for shelters that call for 125 cm or more. Hikers using the poles for a tent such as a Durston X-Mid have made it work, but anyone with a taller pole requirement should check the shelter's specs first, or choose the Compact 3.

**Grips and details.** The EVA grips are slim and contoured, with a slightly curved top that suits palming on steep descents. Straps are simple. The kit is streamlined to hold weight and cost down: smaller grips, basic straps, and limited replacement parts compared with fully featured poles. Baskets push on with a tight fit, and one owner found the stock baskets small for mud and prone to snagging on roots. One early user also found the pole noisy on hard ground, with a buzzy vibration at tip contact.

**Durability and care.** Aluminum dents more readily than carbon, and owners say a habit of dropping poles at every rest stop will show up as dings. The paint scratches easily, which is cosmetic. The recurring advice, from an owner on the Kaviso page and from a Backpacking Light reader, is to dry the poles before storing them: wet storage oxidizes the aluminum and can make the twist-lock stiff. Treated that way, owners report years of regular use.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 169 g per pole, very light for adjustable aluminum
- 51 cm collapsed length fits in a pack or carry-on
- 7001 aluminum construction, reported to hold up on rough terrain
- Priced well under carbon and titanium alternatives
- Includes tip covers and two sets of baskets

**Cons**

- Maximum 125 cm, and the pole reads slightly short at its nominal setting
- Twist-lock needs careful tightening and must be dried before storage
- Dents and scratches more easily than carbon
- Basic grips and straps, with limited replacement parts
- Some noise and vibration at tip contact

## Who Should Buy This

The Compact 4 suits ultralight backpackers and thru-hikers on a budget, as well as travelers who need poles that fit in luggage or a small pack. It is also a sensible pick for hikers who want a pole they won't mind banging against rocks. Anyone who pitches a tall trekking-pole shelter, or who wants every last gram off, should look at the Compact 3 or a carbon pole from a cottage brand. Hikers who want flip-lock speed and tool-free field adjustment will find the twist-lock a step backward.

## Verdict

The Compact 4 delivers most of what ultralight hikers want from a pole at a fraction of the carbon price, and it packs smaller than almost anything else adjustable. The shorter maximum length and the care the lock and aluminum demand keep it from being a universal pick. For hikers who can live with those limits, it is one of the better value buys in trekking poles.

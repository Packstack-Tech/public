## Overview

The Anker PowerCore 5000 (model A1109) is a lipstick-style cylindrical power bank aimed at hikers and travelers who need a single phone top-up in the lightest, smallest package possible. <cite index="1-18">It packs a 5,000mAh cell into a cylindrical body measuring about 1.3 inches in diameter and 4.2 inches long, weighing in at 4.71 oz.</cite> It's a no-frills device — one port in, one port out — that makes the most sense for overnight or weekend trips where a single smartphone is your only device.

## Performance

### Usable Capacity

The headline number to manage here is efficiency. <cite index="2-1,2-2">Anker themselves state that the PowerCore 5000's output efficiency is 60–70%, meaning approximately 30–40% of the rated capacity can't reach your device due to efficiency losses.</cite> That tracks with independent lab testing: <cite index="1-1,1-4">at a 2A discharge rate, one test pulled 3,259mAh out of the bank; at 1A it was only marginally better at 3,343mAh.</cite> In practical terms, <cite index="17-5">a fully recharged PowerCore 5000 can generally deliver at least one charge to an iPhone 12 with its 2,815mAh battery.</cite> <cite index="1-6">In real-device testing, it charged a Samsung Galaxy S8 1.12 times before running dry.</cite> That's a solid one full charge for most modern smartphones — just don't expect a top-off on day three of a longer trip.

### Charging Speed

<cite index="2-25,2-26,2-27">
  The PowerCore 5000 uses two technologies: PowerIQ, which detects and delivers
  the fastest possible charge to any device up to 2A, and VoltageBoost, which
  compensates for cable resistance to maintain consistent output speed.
</cite>
In practice, this means it'll charge a mid-range Android or iPhone at a
reasonable pace — not blazing fast, but not frustratingly slow either.
<cite index="1-36">
  The bank itself recharges in about 2.5 hours with a 2A wall adapter
</cite>
, which means you can top it up overnight at a trailhead or in town without
eating up your whole resupply stop.

### Low-Current Device Compatibility

This is the sneaky gotcha for backpackers. <cite index="18-5,18-6">The PowerCore requires connected devices to draw at least 30–90mA to continue operating; if a device's power requirement falls below that minimum threshold, the charging process won't start or will cut off.</cite> If you're planning to trickle-charge a GPS unit, a headlamp with USB input, or a Garmin inReach, you may find the bank shuts itself off before the job's done. Test your specific gear before relying on it in the field.

### Form Factor

The cylindrical shape is genuinely useful on trail. <cite index="1-21,1-22">The USB output port sits on the end of the cylinder, so you can plug in a cable and charge while the bank rides in one hip-belt pocket and your phone rides in another, routing the cable around your waistband.</cite> It's not a groundbreaking system, but it works.

### The mAh/oz Math

At ~1,040mAh per ounce, the PowerCore 5000 is not a standout in the efficiency-per-gram category. The Nitecore NB10000 Gen4 delivers roughly twice the capacity for only 0.4oz more — a meaningful trade-off that any weight-conscious hiker should calculate before defaulting to this bank. <cite index="16-10">For a one-night trip, the PowerCore 5000's capacity — roughly 1 to 2 smartphone charges — is appropriate sizing.</cite> Beyond that, you'll likely want more capacity.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Genuinely compact and pocketable — the cylindrical shape fits anywhere
- Recharges in ~2.5 hours, easy to top up at a motel or hostel
- <cite index="15-4,15-5">
    Anker power banks are reliable and well-built, consistently outperforming
    most generic alternatives in safety and long-term battery health.
  </cite>
- Affordable entry price — easy to justify as a backup or secondary bank
- <cite index="2-28">
    MultiProtect safety system includes surge protection, short circuit
    prevention, and additional safety features.
  </cite>
- 18-month limited warranty

**Cons**

- Micro-USB input only — in a USB-C world, this means an extra cable to pack
- Single USB-A output port; you can only charge one device at a time
- <cite index="2-5">
    Pass-through charging has been disabled to protect battery lifespan
  </cite>
  — you can't recharge the bank and a device simultaneously
- Poor mAh/oz ratio compared to the best ultralight alternatives
- No USB-C output limits compatibility with modern charging cables
- Low-current auto-shutoff is a real problem for GPS devices and other small electronics
- No Quick Charge or PD support

## Who Should Buy This

The PowerCore 5000 is best suited to weekend backpackers and one-night campers who carry a single modern smartphone and want the smallest, lightest insurance policy they can find. <cite index="16-3,16-4">Due to its limited capacity, it's not the best choice for multi-day adventurers loaded up with energy-hungry gadgets — but if you're heading into the backcountry with just a smartphone, it's a solid and highly portable companion.</cite> If you're a serious thru-hiker or ultralight purist who runs a phone, a GPS, and earbuds, spend a little more and buy a 10,000mAh bank with a better mAh/oz ratio and a USB-C port.

## Verdict

The PowerCore 5000 does exactly what it promises: one phone charge, minimal weight, minimal fuss, minimal cost. <cite index="12-10">Anker products have an excellent reputation among thru-hikers</cite>, and the brand's reliability is genuine. But the Micro-USB input is a real anachronism at this point, the mAh/oz ratio is pedestrian, and the low-current shutoff will catch you off guard if you try to charge anything other than a phone. If the price is right and you only need a single overnight buffer, it earns its spot in your kit — but it shouldn't be the default choice for anyone doing trips longer than two days.

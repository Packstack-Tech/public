## Overview

The LifeStraw Go Series 1L is a BPA-free plastic bottle with a two-stage filter built into the cap. A 0.2-micron membrane handles bacteria and parasites, and an activated carbon stage takes the edge off chlorine and odors. It suits section hikers, travelers and anyone who wants to fill up from a creek and drink immediately. The trade-off is weight and effort: at 268 g it is far heavier than a squeeze filter, and every sip is a pull.

## Performance

**Filtration.** The membrane filter is rated to remove 99.999999% of bacteria, including E. coli and Salmonella. It is rated at 99.999% for parasites such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium, and at the same figure for microplastics. It also stops sand, silt and cloudiness. Viruses are not part of the claim, which matters for international travel and for water downstream of heavy human activity. In practice, the Go Series is a North American backcountry filter, not a catch-all purifier.

CleverHiker's testers filled the bottle from gas station taps and silty creeks. They reported water that tasted good and looked free of grit, and they said they trusted it. The carbon stage is the reason tap water tastes less like a swimming pool, a benefit buyers on retail sites mention often.

**Ease of use.** This is the bottle's strongest argument. There is nothing to assemble, no bag to squeeze and no wait time. Dip, cap, drink. That beats a squeeze-filter setup for casual use, and it means one fewer item to fumble with at a water source.

**Drinking effort.** It is also the most common complaint. Water has to be pulled through the membrane and carbon block like a thick milkshake through a narrow straw. CleverHiker flagged the pull required as a downside, and a repeat buyer on eBay called it difficult to draw water, advising others to make sure they can create a good vacuum. Strong, steady sipping on a flat day is fine. Gulping at the top of a climb is not.

**Durability.** CleverHiker's testers dropped the bottle and found the filter stayed firmly seated in the lid, with no sign of damage from the falls. The housing is hard plastic, not a soft flask that can crack in the cold or split at the seam. The bottle, cap and handle are dishwasher-safe once the filters are removed, and the mouthpiece unscrews for cleaning.

**Limits.** Only water goes through it. Drink mixes and electrolyte powders can clog or foul the filter, so flavoring has to happen in a separate container. After a trip, the filter unit should be rinsed with clean water and dried out before storage rather than sealed away damp.

**Fit and carry.** At 28 cm tall and 9.2 cm wide, the 1L bottle is a bigger bottle than most hikers carry in a side pocket. CleverHiker noted that the larger version can be tough to squeeze into a fully loaded pack, though its testers had no trouble with a pack pocket. Ultralight packs with tight stretch pockets are the likeliest place to run into trouble. A full liter weighs roughly 1.27 kg once the bottle is counted, so it is also a lot of mass hanging off one hip.

**Running costs.** The microfilter is rated for 1,000 gallons, about five years of daily use, and it shuts off flow when it reaches the end of its life. The carbon filter is the weak link. At 26 gallons (100 L), it needs replacing about every two months with regular use. Hikers who treat the bottle as a primary backcountry filter will be buying carbon cartridges often. Those who drink from it only occasionally will go longer between replacements.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Filters directly from the bottle with no assembly or wait
- Two stages: membrane for bacteria, parasites and microplastics; carbon for taste and odor
- Tested to EPA and NSF P231 standards for bacteria and parasites
- Filter stayed secure after drop tests
- Replaceable filters, with a long-lived membrane
- Dishwasher-safe once filters are removed
- Typically around $40 at retail

**Cons**

- 268 g is heavy for a thru-hiker's water system, before counting any water
- Strong suction needed; slow for gulping
- Carbon filter is rated for only about two months
- Not rated for viruses
- No drink mixes
- 1L size is bulky in tight pack pockets
- Can't be used as a pass-through filter to fill other containers

## Who Should Buy This

Day hikers, weekend backpackers, travelers and anyone building an emergency kit will get the most from it. It is a good fit for people who value drinking straight from a source and who tend to forget about filter setups until they are thirsty. The thru-hiker or gram-counter should look at squeeze filters instead, which weigh a fraction as much, fill any bottle, and flow faster. The same goes for anyone who needs to filter water for cooking or for a group.

## Verdict

The Go Series 1L does one thing well: it makes drinking from a questionable tap or stream about as simple as it can be. For long-distance ultralight use, its weight, suction effort and short carbon-filter life count against it. It earns a solid recommendation for casual and travel use, and a pass for serious mileage.

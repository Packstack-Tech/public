## Overview

Most trail-runner gaiters are built to stop gravel. Zpacks' Rain Gaiters are built to stop weather. They're a waterproof nylon shell that covers the laces completely, closes with a waterproof front zipper, and weighs about as much as a pair of ski-hat liners. The trade-off is price and a fabric that rewards careful handling: $59.95 buys roughly two ounces of protection, and some hikers have found that fabric less rugged than they'd like.

## Performance

**Coverage.** The design goal is stated plainly on the Zpacks page: unlike some gaiters, these are meant to fully cover the laces and keep debris and water out of running shoes. That matters for trail-runner hikers, whose mesh uppers and tongue gussets are the main way that grit and puddle water get in. The gaiter wraps the shoe from the top of the ankle down over the lace area, and a clip near the toe hooks onto the laces to keep the front from riding up.

**Fit and retention.** An adjustable shock cord at the top cinches the gaiter around the leg. Underfoot, a thin UHMWPE cord runs beneath the arch instead of a wide strap or a Velcro tab. Owners of Zpacks' earlier gaiter generation praised that cord arrangement as lighter and neater than a thick strap. The cord is the part most likely to wear through on rock, and Zpacks designed it to be swapped; it's about 16 inches long, and spares are sold separately. A used listing for the ankle model gives the dimensions as 8 inches tall by 16 inches around.

**Weather protection.** The fabric is a plain 100D ripstop nylon with a PFAS-free DWR finish, not a breathable membrane. It is waterproof, and the zipper is waterproof too, so the gaiters shed rain and standing water at the top of the shoe. What they can't do is vent: nylon holds heat and moisture against the lower leg. Zpacks' earlier eVent-and-Cuben Challenger gaiters were described by one Trailspace reviewer as roughly heat-neutral and good at blocking wind. The nylon version will be warmer, and best suited to cool, wet days rather than humid summer hiking. The zipper does help in warm rain, since opening it adds room for stride and airflow.

**Durability.** This is where opinions split. Zpacks describes the ripstop as having good abrasion and tear resistance. One Backpacking Light forum poster, though, said a pair of Zpacks gaiters was shredded on its first outing, and cited that as the reason for looking at tougher alternatives for brushy or off-trail terrain. A 100D face fabric is considerably tougher than the Dyneema-based materials Zpacks is better known for, but it is still a 51-gram garment. Cat-briar, scree and crampon points are the likely enemies.

**On-trail use.** For Pacific Northwest drizzle, Appalachian wet-brush mornings and stream-heavy sections where feet will be wet anyway, the weight penalty is almost negligible. These pair naturally with a rain kilt or rain pants; Zpacks sells them as an add-on to both. They are not snow gaiters and don't claim to be.

## Choosing a Version

Only the height changes. The standard (ankle) version at 51 g covers the shoe and the bottom of the shin. It's the one for trail runners, warm-weather thru-hikers and anyone who wears rain pants or a kilt and doesn't need to bridge a gap. The calf-height version, at 104 g, roughly doubles the weight and adds coverage up the lower leg. That suits hikers who wear shorts or a kilt in cold rain, push through wet brush or poison-ivy corridors, or want mud kept off their legs. The extra 53 g is real, but it's less than most hikers' rain mitts and spare socks together. Hikers with larger calves should keep in mind that the calf version closes with a cord, so it adjusts better than a fixed-width sleeve.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Ankle version weighs 51 g, so it's easy to justify carrying through a wet section
- Full lace coverage, designed for trail runners
- Waterproof full-length zipper makes on and off simple, even with muddy shoes on
- Replaceable under-shoe cord is a practical, field-serviceable design
- PFAS-free DWR finish
- Two heights cover both shoe protection and leg protection

**Cons**

- $59.95 is a lot for gaiters of this weight
- Non-breathable nylon runs warm on the lower leg
- Some owners report tearing on rough terrain, and the thin under-shoe cord will wear on rock
- Not a snow or off-trail bushwhacking gaiter
- Calf height roughly doubles the weight

## Who Should Buy This

Trail-runner hikers who walk wet trails, such as thru-hikers on the PCT, AT or in the Pacific Northwest, and want an easy-to-carry layer that keeps shoes and socks cleaner. They suit anyone building a rain system around a kilt, and pair well with it. They're also for gear-minded hikers who'll treat the fabric with care. Those who regularly walk in thorny brush, scree or snow, or who run hot in summer humidity, should look at a more rugged or breathable gaiter. Anyone who only wants a basic debris guard can get one for far less.

## Verdict

The Zpacks Rain Gaiters do a specific job well: they seal a trail runner against rain and trail debris for 51 grams, and the zipper and replaceable cord make them easy to use. The price and the fabric's mixed durability record keep them from a higher score. Buy the ankle version for wet-weather trail-runner use, and the calf version if cold wet legs are the problem.

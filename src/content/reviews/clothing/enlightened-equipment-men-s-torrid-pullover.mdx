## Overview

The Torrid Pullover is Enlightened Equipment's hooded, half-zip synthetic puffy, insulated with 2 oz/yd² CLIMASHIELD™ APEX and sold in 7D, 10D, or 20D nylon. It's built for ultralight backpackers and thru-hikers who want camp warmth that doesn't fall apart when it gets damp. The trade-off is familiar: a little more weight and bulk than a good down jacket, in exchange for insulation that tolerates sweat, drizzle, and a washing machine.

## Performance

**Warmth.** Warmth is where the Torrid earns its reputation. Adventure Alan rated its warmth-to-weight as similar to mid-tier down, likely better than 650-fill. The same review conceded that it is heavier and less effective than 900-fill down. A Pacific Crest Trail hiker who swapped a worn Mountain Hardwear Ghost Whisperer for the Torrid found it noticeably warmer than the down jacket. She credited the hood and the fact that the half zip doesn't leak heat the way a full-length zipper can. She reported temperatures dipping into the high 20s °F, and she was never cold with a fleece underneath. Another owner used it on early-fall and late-winter trips and added a fleece only when it got properly cold. Several owners also sleep in it when pushing the limits of a quilt or bag.

**Wet-weather behavior.** Apex doesn't depend on loft-killing clumps of feather to work, so a damp jacket still insulates. The DWR-treated face fabric handles light precipitation, but it is a puffy and not a shell. The practical gain is in how it handles sweat. Reviewers describe it as breathable enough to wear as an active mid-layer on a cold climb. Owners also point to the ease of washing it, which is a real consideration for anyone who perspires in a puffy for months on trail.

**The pouch and hood.** The marsupial pouch is the feature reviewers mention most. It's insulated on both sides, has high sides that keep snacks and a phone from falling out, and holds a pair of cold hands during camp chores. It also hides the waist shock cord. The hood is deep, adjustable by shock cord, and meant for people who sleep under a quilt and want something over their head. Because the zipper only runs halfway, the jacket goes on and off like a hoodie. A Backpacking Light reviewer judged the pullover format a drawback compared with a full-zip option for venting and on-off ease. Half-zip fans, including Adventure Alan, prefer its reliability and the extra warmth of the closed pouch.

**Weight and packability.** At 248 g, this version of the Torrid weighs a bit more than the 7D builds reviewers have measured. Adventure Alan listed 230 g in 7D, and a Backpacking Light pre-production medium came in at 219 g. A 10D build was listed at 8.6 oz. Fabric choice drives the difference: reviewers point to 7D for the lightest build, 10D as a balance, and 20D when durability matters most. The jacket arrives remarkably lofty for a synthetic, and one thru-hiker used hers as a camp pillow. Owners say it stuffs small, with one fitting the pullover and a pair of insulated gloves into a single stuff sack. It still takes up more room than a comparable down puffy.

**Durability and fit.** Owners report little wear after months of regular use, though one cautioned that it is ultralight gear and not something to take through thick, pointy brush. The cut is roomy, and a medium on a 5'10", 152 lb tester fit with room for layers. Owners describe the look as functional more than flattering.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Warmth-to-weight that rivals mid-grade down
- Insulation keeps working when damp or sweaty
- Large insulated pouch with high sides and a hidden waist cord
- Deep, adjustable hood suits quilt sleepers
- Choice of 7D, 10D, or 20D fabric to balance weight and durability
- Can be washed without the care down requires

**Cons**

- Heavier and bulkier than a good 800–900-fill down jacket
- Pullover format means no full-zip venting or quick on-off
- Oversized cut and boxy look
- Ultralight face fabrics need careful handling around brush and rock

## Who Should Buy This

The Torrid suits three-season and shoulder-season backpackers, particularly in damp climates like the Pacific Northwest, the Northeast, or the Scottish Highlands. It also suits anyone who sweats through insulation or who sleeps in their puffy to extend a quilt's range. Hikers who want the lowest possible weight and the smallest packed size should look at a premium down jacket instead. So should anyone who dislikes pullovers or likes to vent with a full zip.

## Verdict

The Torrid Pullover is one of the more convincing arguments for synthetic insulation in the ultralight world. It is warm, forgiving of moisture, and thoughtfully designed around real camp use. Hikers who can accept a small weight and bulk penalty over down will find it a dependable, easy-to-live-with piece of kit.

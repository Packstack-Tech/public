<cite index="3-13,3-14">

## Performance

### Fuel Efficiency — The Star of the Show

This is where the Stash justifies its existence. <cite index="10-12,10-13">Using an average of 0.13 oz of fuel per boil, the Stash will take a canister very far — testers logged several days in the High Sierra and were able to stretch an 8 oz can of fuel nearly twice as long as some competing stoves.</cite> In real numbers, <cite index="1-16,1-17,1-18">one 100g canister delivers around 12 liters of boiled water — nearly twice the 6.4 liters you'd get from older systems — which translates to 2–3 extra trail days on one canister, meaning there's often no need to carry a spare.</cite>

That efficiency is almost entirely due to the FluxRing. If you think you can swap in a generic titanium pot and keep those numbers, think again. <cite index="4-4,4-5">Testing showed that using the same-sized titanium pot brought boil time up to 3:08 and increased cooking time and fuel consumption by about 63%.</cite> The Stash burner is deliberately tuned to a low fuel-flow rate that only pays off when paired with the included aluminum FluxRing pot.

### Boil Time & Wind Resistance

The 2.5-minute boil for 0.5L is fast and real-world consistent in calm conditions. <cite index="4-9,4-10,4-11">In simulated testing at 4 mph wind, the stove still boiled water in 3:32 using just 6.7 grams of fuel</cite> — respectable for an open-flame design. That said, <cite index="6-27,6-28">the Stash is an open-flame stove, meaning the flame is exposed to wind on the sides, unlike other enclosed Jetboil models.</cite> Find a natural windbreak or use your body — this isn't optional in exposed terrain.

### Cold Weather

<cite index="3-17,3-18">
  Where the Stash struggles is in the combination of near-freezing temperatures
  and wind, though similar stoves like the Flash or MSR Pocket Rocket face the
  same challenge.
</cite>
Without a pressure regulator,
<cite index="2-19">
  performance may vary with elevation, and as the canister cools, output drops.
</cite>
<cite index="3-25,3-26,3-27,3-28">
  In near-freezing conditions, a pressure-regulated burner like the MiniMo
  outperforms the Stash with significantly faster boil times, and its
  interlocking burner ring creates a wind-protected area for the flame. If
  you'll frequently use your stove near freezing and can't protect it from wind,
  the MiniMo might be a better choice even though it weighs 7 oz more.
</cite>

### Simmering & Cooking Versatility

Don't expect to replicate your kitchen here. <cite index="2-21,2-22">The stove can transition from a rolling boil down to a modest simmer, but it doesn't offer the fine-tuned control you'd get from more cooking-focused systems.</cite> <cite index="10-17,10-18">When sautéing veggies or cooking noodles, you'll need to keep a very close eye on the pot — for shoppers needing a stove for more complex cuisines, a different option would be recommended.</cite> If your trail diet is freeze-dried meals, instant oatmeal, and coffee, this limitation will never bite you.

### Nesting & Packability

<cite index="2-13,2-14">
  The pot lid is designed to hold a 4 oz fuel canister upside-down inside the
  pot, while the folding handle tucks out of the way, helping the entire setup
  form a compact, self-contained unit.
</cite>
<cite index="8-27">
  One smart detail: because the canister doesn't touch the metal cooking pot at
  all, you don't get that annoying ring of rust transfer if you put the canister
  back into a slightly damp pot, and nothing rattles around.
</cite>

Fair warning: <cite index="1-40,1-41,1-42">all components nest in the pot, but there's only one way they can really fit, so until you get the hang of it, it's like playing a puzzle game every time you pack up. Jetboil anticipated the added pain — they printed pack-up instructions directly on the pot.</cite>

### Pot Stability

<cite index="3-3,3-4">
  The burner's pot support arms have a notch that interlocks with the flux-ring
  on the bottom of the pot, making it hard to accidentally knock the pot off the
  stove.
</cite>
Compared to a bare canister stove with a separate pot, this feels reassuringly
solid.

### Altitude

One JMT thru-hiker's experience is worth noting: <cite index="4-27,4-28,4-29,4-30">testing over a 210+ mile John Muir Trail effort showed no performance issues at elevation, including at Guitar Lake at 11,500 feet, and only one 100g canister was needed for the entire hike.</cite>

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Exceptional fuel efficiency — roughly double that of non-FluxRing competitors
- Fast 2.5-minute boil keeps camp time short
- 201g for a complete stove-plus-pot system is legitimately light
- Notched arms lock the pot in place — no tippy-stove anxiety
- <cite index="9-21">
    Short, wide pot design lets you eat straight from the cup and offers a
    convenient spoon angle
  </cite>
- <cite index="9-1">
    Titanium burner's notched ridges let you use other pots and pans from your
    kit
  </cite>
  if needed

**Cons**

- No integrated igniter — always carry a lighter; forgetting one means no dinner
- No pressure regulator; output degrades meaningfully in cold or high-altitude conditions
- <cite index="9-4,9-5">
    The handle is a touch short — Jetboil kept it short for packability, but an
    extra inch would give a more secure grip on a hot pot
  </cite>
- <cite index="10-7">
    The lid has a tough time staying on the pot, whether packed in a bag or when
    trying to pour water through its designated spout
  </cite>
- Fuel efficiency collapses if you try to use a non-FluxRing pot
- 0.8L pot capacity is snug for two people

## Who Should Buy This

The Stash is purpose-built for the solo backpacker or thru-hiker whose backcountry cooking starts and ends with boiling water. If your resupply boxes are full of freeze-dried meals and instant coffee, this system will carry less fuel weight over longer stretches than almost anything else on the market. <cite index="4-25">The value of the Stash's fuel efficiency becomes most apparent when you're planning to boil a lot of water between resupplies.</cite> It's less compelling for cold-weather mountaineers or anyone who wants to actually cook — those folks should look at a pressure-regulated system or a separate stove-and-pot setup with better simmer control.

## Verdict

The Jetboil Stash is the rare piece of ultralight gear that delivers on its core promise without significant fine print — as long as you go in eyes-open about what it is. It's a boil-water machine, not a kitchen. <cite index="10-5">The Stash was incredibly fuel-efficient, sometimes burning half as much fuel as the competition</cite>, and that single fact reshapes your fuel carry math for multi-day trips. Dock points for the absent igniter, the fiddly lid, and the cold-weather limitations. On balance: an 8/10 for anyone doing 3-season, boil-only backpacking — and the right answer for most solo thru-hikers who count every gram but still want a hot meal at the end of a long day.

## Overview

The Evac Dry Bag is a roll-top waterproof sack with one unusual feature: its base is made of eVent, a waterproof laminate that lets air out but keeps water from getting in. Roll the top down, press on the bag, and the air leaves through the bottom instead of a clunky purge valve. It suits backpackers who want dry, compact storage for quilts, puffies and spare clothes. The trade-off is that the bag is built for rain and spills, not for being dunked.

## Performance

**Packing and air removal.** The Evac's selling point is that it gets small without extra hardware. Sea to Summit's own advice is to sit on the packed bag to squeeze the air out, then slot it into a tight space in the pack. Owners of the Evac line describe the venting as smooth and effective. One testing site found that air left a jacket-stuffed bag without fuss. Trailspace reviewers note it is a little slower than a non-waterproof compression sack. The 13L is big enough to hold a sleeping bag, an inflatable pad and a foam pillow, according to one REI buyer.

**Weather protection.** The 70D body has a 10,000 mm hydrostatic head. Seams are double-stitched and taped. Reviewers of the Evac family report dry contents through rain, bottle spills and leaking hydration reservoirs. A long-term tester logged more than 200 pack-and-unpack cycles across desert, rainforest and subzero trips. His seams and tape still looked close to new. One Trailspace owner reported more than two seasons and six backpacking trips on 13L and 35L Evac Dry Sacks without a failure.

**The submersion caveat.** The bag shouldn't go underwater. The eVent base that lets air out is also the reason Sea to Summit labels the Evac line as not for submersion. REI buyers have noticed the warning on the label. Owners who tested the compression sibling by holding it under water or hosing it down saw some leakage. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers saw a dry towel after submerging the 70D eVent compression bag. Even so, the safe reading is that the Evac Dry Bag is for rain, wet packs and stream-crossing insurance, not for canoe capsizes or packrafting. For that, a conventional roll-top such as the Ultra-Sil is the better choice.

**Durability.** The 70D nylon is a step up from the 30D fabric in Sea to Summit's ultralight compression sacks. Owners describe it as tough enough to strap on the outside of a bike or snowmobile. The eVent base is the point most exposed to abrasion. Keep sharp objects away from it, and don't dump fuel on it. One Trailspace reviewer's compression bag lost some watertightness after a petrol leak from a fuel bottle.

**Closure.** The Hypalon roll-top is non-wicking and stays stiff enough to fold cleanly. As with any roll-top, leave enough room to fold the top at least three times. Sea to Summit rates its closure and seam performance on that basis. The Field Repair Buckle is user-replaceable with a screwdriver.

## Choosing a Version

The only thing that changes across the range is volume, and with it a modest weight increase from 43 g to 95 g.

- **3L and 5L:** Organizers for electronics, toiletries, a first-aid kit or a small down vest. The 3L fits a phone, charger and headlamp. The 5L swallows a puffy jacket or a pair of insulated gloves and a hat.
- **8L:** A good fit for a spare-clothing kit or a ground-sheet-and-stakes bundle. Many ultralight backpackers fill this size with a base layer, socks and a beanie.
- **13L:** The size that handles a quilt or a 20-degree sleeping bag along with a few small items. It is also the size most commonly cited by owners who compress sleeping gear.
- **20L:** For a bulky winter sleeping bag, a full clothing change or a set of cold-weather layers. Beyond this, a pack liner is usually lighter than a single large dry bag.

Buy the smallest size that closes with three full rolls. A stuffed bag can't get a proper seal.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Air escapes through the base, so there is no purge valve to leak or fiddle with
- 70D body is noticeably tougher than ultralight 30D dry sacks
- Tape-sealed, double-stitched seams hold up after years of use, according to long-term owners
- Weighs 43–95 g across the range
- Non-PFAS DWR and recycled body fabric
- Oval base stands upright and doesn't roll around in a pack

**Cons**

- Not designed for submersion
- Venting is slower than with a standard stuff sack
- Heavier than a plain ultralight dry sack of the same volume
- eVent base needs protection from abrasion and solvents
- Priced above basic roll-tops

## Who Should Buy This

Thru-hikers and weekend backpackers who carry insulation in a pack liner and want a second layer of protection for the items that matter most: quilt, puffy, sleep clothes. Bikepackers who strap bags to a handlebar or frame will also benefit from the compression. The tougher 70D body handles that sort of exposure better than 30D alternatives. Packrafters, kayakers and anyone expecting real immersion should look elsewhere, to a conventional dry bag without an air-permeable base. Those who want the lightest possible weight and don't need waterproofing can skip it too.

## Verdict

The Evac Dry Bag does what it promises: it shrinks soft gear without a valve, and it keeps rain and spills out for years of trail use. Buyers just need to respect its limits. For backpacking, it earns a place in the pack; for paddling, it doesn't.

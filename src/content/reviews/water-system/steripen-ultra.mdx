## Overview

The SteriPEN Ultra is a handheld, USB-rechargeable UV-C purifier that neutralizes bacteria, protozoa, and viruses in a liter of clear water in about a minute and a half. It suits hikers who want to drink from clear streams without pumping, squeezing, or waiting out chemical tablets. The trade-off is the usual one for UV: it does nothing about silt or floaties, and it is a battery-powered device in a category where the alternatives are mostly fabric and plastic.

## Performance

**Speed.** This is the Ultra's main argument. Press the button once for 0.5 L or twice for 1 L, stir the pen through the water, and wait for the countdown on the OLED screen to finish. A half-liter takes 48 seconds, a full liter 90. Compared with a gravity filter hanging from a tree or a squeeze filter that clogs by mile 800, that is hard to beat for a hiker who stops at a stream, drinks, and leaves. Owners who have carried it for years describe the routine as simple enough to run on autopilot, and one reviewer noted that the button is stiff enough that it is unlikely to switch on accidentally in a pack.

**The display.** Earlier SteriPEN models relied on small indicator lights. The Ultra's OLED screen counts down the treatment and ends with a smiley face when the water is done, which owners credit with removing guesswork about whether a cycle was completed or interrupted. It also shows battery status.

**Battery life.** The 2,200 mAh internal cell is rated for up to 50 L per charge. In practice, that covers a long weekend for two people with room to spare. A Backpacking Light member who has used one for close to a decade, including Nepal, roughly 63 days on the Pacific Northwest Trail, and 17 Boundary Waters trips, reports it still gets two people through a long weekend without a top-up. The same user carries a charging cable and a small power block as backup. On a long thru-hike, that means the Ultra joins the phone and headlamp on the charging list. It can charge from any USB source, including a solar panel.

**Clear water only.** UV needs light to reach the pathogens, so turbid or sediment-laden water defeats it. The manufacturer says to use it on clear water only. The Ultra also does nothing to remove debris, a point that reviewers consistently raise. After carrying one on the Pacific Crest Trail and afterward in the Himalayas, Japan, and the Sierra, Halfway Anywhere's reviewer rated it 3.8 out of 5, with floaties as the headline complaint. SteriPEN sells a pre-filter that fits 32 oz wide-mouth bottles, at about $13 and 1.5 oz (43 g). That adds weight and one more thing to drop, but it makes the Ultra workable in cloudy desert sources and silty snowmelt.

**Container fit.** The pen needs a wide mouth. With a 20 mm minimum opening, standard wide-mouth Nalgene-style bottles and collapsible bladders with large openings work. Narrow-mouthed soda bottles, the favored container of many ultralight hikers, do not. Treating water in a pot or a dedicated wide-mouth bottle is the usual workaround. One advantage: water can be scooped in one place and treated in another, which owners find useful in buggy camps.

**Longevity.** The lamp is rated for 8,000 treatments, which no individual hiker is likely to exhaust. The practical failure points are the battery, which wears with age like any lithium cell, and the physical lamp, a quartz bulb that is described as impact-resistant but still a glass part in a pack that gets squashed, dropped, and sat on.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Treats 1 L in 90 seconds with no pumping, waiting, or backflushing
- Handles viruses as well as bacteria and protozoa, which most hollow-fiber filters do not
- 140.7 g is lighter than most pump purifiers and many gravity systems
- OLED countdown and battery readout make treatment status obvious
- USB charging works with power banks and solar panels
- About 50 L per charge covers most trips between town stops

**Cons**

- Useless on cloudy water without a separate pre-filter
- Leaves debris, taste, and chemicals in the water
- Needs a bottle opening of at least 20 mm
- Depends on a battery; a dead pen means a backup treatment method
- At roughly $135–140, costs several times as much as a squeeze filter
- Glass lamp is a potential breakage point

## Who Should Buy This

The Ultra suits hikers in regions with clear water, such as alpine terrain, the Sierra, the Rockies, and Scandinavia, and international travelers who want to treat tap and questionable-source water without carrying a filter. It also suits small groups, since 90 seconds per liter beats pumping for two. Hikers in silt-heavy or cow-trampled country, or anyone who bristles at carrying a pre-filter, a backup treatment, and a charging cable, should look at a squeeze filter or chemical treatment. Hikers who prefer narrow-mouth bottles will need to change their water carry.

## Verdict

The Ultra is the fastest, easiest way to treat clear water for hikers who accept its limits: it needs a wide-mouth bottle, a charge, and a source that is not murky. In the right terrain, it is a justified purchase. In silty terrain it is the wrong tool, and a backup treatment is still wise.

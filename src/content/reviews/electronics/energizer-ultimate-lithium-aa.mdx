## Overview

The Energizer Ultimate Lithium AA is a disposable lithium/iron disulfide cell that drops into any device built for a standard 1.5 V AA. It's the default spare for headlamps, GPS units, avalanche beacons and trail-camera kits, where weight and cold-weather reliability matter more than cost. The trade-off is price: a four-pack typically runs around $15, several times what alkalines cost per cell.

## Performance

**Weight.** At roughly 14.5 g per cell, the Ultimate Lithium is about a third lighter than a typical alkaline AA, which weighs around 23 g. That saves about 8.5 g a cell. A four-cell GPS or a headlamp with a spare set trims perhaps 35 to 70 g. That's not dramatic, but it's free weight, and it comes with better performance.

**Cold weather.** This is where the cell earns its price. Alkaline chemistry sags badly as temperatures drop. Lithium/iron disulfide barely notices. Backcountry hunters and winter hikers on flashlight and outdoor forums describe the gap against alkaline as pronounced. Several owners cite freezer tests down to -40, where the lithiums kept working and alkalines in the same high-drain devices struggled or quit. For avalanche beacons, winter headlamps and GPS units carried on snow, the rating of -40°F to 140°F is the reason to buy.

**High drain versus low drain.** Flashlight reviewer HKJ's bench testing found the cell very impressive at high current. At low loads, though, it was not much better than alkaline, apart from the fact that it doesn't leak. That tracks with how it's used on trail. A headlamp on a bright setting, a camera flash or a GPS with the backlight on gets a clear benefit. A low-draw remote or a clock on a gear-shop shelf gets longevity, not extra runtime. The cell's higher voltage also gives it more total energy than alkaline, and its discharge curve is flatter, so devices hold full output longer. The flip side is that a flat curve gives a battery gauge little warning before the cell is spent. Hikers who rely on a headlamp's battery bars should carry a spare set.

**Storage and leak resistance.** Energizer rates the cell for up to 20 years in storage, and the construction is described as leak-proof under standard use. Owners who stash spares in bear canisters, emergency kits and seasonal gear often point to the lack of leaking as a quiet advantage over alkaline. Fresh cells can read above 1.5 V on a meter, which is normal for the chemistry, and electronics designed for AA cells generally handle it. Some equipment, especially older gear tuned to alkaline's characteristics, doesn't respond well, so check the manual before swapping.

**Safety.** The cell includes a resettable over-current protection element and a vent-release mechanism. It meets ANSI, IEC, UL and UN standards and is certified for intrinsic safety under the ATEX directive.

**Price and availability.** Four-packs usually sell for $14 to $15, and 8-packs and bulk boxes bring the per-cell cost down. At several dollars a cell, they're not what anyone puts in a TV remote. They're easy to find at big-box retailers and outdoor shops, and REI customers rate them 4.8 out of 5 across more than 1,300 reviews.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- About 14.5 g per cell, roughly a third lighter than alkaline
- Operates from -40°F to 140°F, with strong cold-weather performance
- Strong output in high-drain devices
- Up to 20-year shelf life suits caches and emergency kits
- Leak-resistant construction
- Widely sold and easy to replace mid-trip in a town stop

**Cons**

- Costs several times more per cell than alkaline
- Single-use, so it produces waste a rechargeable would not
- Little advantage over alkaline in very low-drain devices
- The flat discharge curve means a battery gauge gives limited warning before the cell quits
- The 1.5 V rating is nominal; fresh cells read higher, which may matter in some equipment tuned for alkaline

## Who Should Buy This

Winter hikers, mountaineers and anyone running a headlamp, GPS, beacon or camera in sub-freezing conditions should carry these. So should thru-hikers on long hauls between resupplies who want a lighter spare set that will still be good in the bottom of a pack months later. Weekend three-season hikers with a rechargeable-friendly headlamp, or anyone powering low-draw household items, can stick with alkaline or rechargeable cells and keep the money.

## Verdict

The Energizer Ultimate Lithium AA is the cell to buy when a device has to work in the cold, draws real current, or has to sit in a pack for a long time. It's lighter than alkaline and tougher in conditions that wreck it. It's overkill for low-draw gadgets, but for backcountry electronics it's the standard to beat.

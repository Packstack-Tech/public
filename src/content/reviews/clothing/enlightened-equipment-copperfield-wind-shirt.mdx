## Overview

The Copperfield is Enlightened Equipment's hooded, full-zip wind shirt, sewn in Winona, Minnesota, from 10D nylon (a 20D option is also offered). It starts at 58.4 g (2.06 oz) in the smallest size, which puts it closer to a candy bar than a jacket. The trade-off is plain: very little weight and very good breathability, in exchange for a thin shell that wants careful handling and a basic finish.

## Performance

**Weight and packability.** The Copperfield is the lightest wind layer Outdoor Gear Lab has tested, with a size medium coming in at 2.2 oz. Backpacking Light, which named it a Staff Pick, lists it at 2 oz. It compresses to something that disappears into a hip-belt pocket or a trail-running vest, and reviewers who have raced or biked in it describe stuffing it into a waist pouch without a second thought.

**Wind resistance.** In Outdoor Gear Lab's fan test, air barely got through the fabric, and testers ranked it among the most wind-resistant shirts in the group. Some of that is the fabric choice. Owners who have dug into the numbers note that EE's 10D sits around 15 CFM of air permeability and the 20D closer to 1 CFM, and one long-term user concluded that only the 20D version behaves like a true wind blocker. The 10D works best as a warmth-extending layer over a fleece or wool sweater, not as a standalone barrier against a hard, cold wind. CleverHiker's testers made a similar point: it handles wind well for its weight but falls behind heavier shells in sustained gales or genuinely cold weather.

**Breathability.** This is where the Copperfield earns its reputation. Backpacking Light credits it with high moisture vapor transmission, about as good as a wind shirt at this weight gets. A reviewer at The Trek found it blocked wind and vented sweat in roughly the same way as the Montbell Tachyon, and that it dried quickly during a rest stop without the clammy chill that wind usually brings. The flip side, per Outdoor Gear Lab, is that it has no ventilation beyond the main zipper, so it suits medium-output activity better than a hot grind uphill.

**Weather resistance.** The C0 DWR sheds light drizzle and mist. Owners treat it as a wind shirt that tolerates a brief shower, not a rain jacket. It wets out eventually, though body heat tends to dry it fast.

**Fit and features.** The cut is regular, with room to layer over a midlayer. The downside, noted by The Trek's reviewer, is that worn over only a base layer, the extra fabric flaps in a breeze. Elastic wrist cuffs keep wind from ballooning the sleeves, a detail one long-term user singled out after owning a cuffless shell that inflated like a sail. The hood adjusts with shock cord, though the cinch exits outside the jacket, and the loose ends tend to whip around in the exact wind the garment is meant to block. There is no chest pocket, which minimalists count as a feature.

**Build and durability.** Reviewers who know Enlightened Equipment's quilts, which are well regarded for refinement, found the Copperfield's finishing basic by comparison. The ultralight fabric is not ripstop and demands care around brush, pack straps and rough rock. CleverHiker flagged durability as the main concession to the weight.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- About 2 oz (58.4 g at the smallest size), among the lightest hooded wind shells available
- High breathability for a fabric this wind-resistant
- Full-length YKK zipper, adjustable hood and hem, and elastic cuffs
- Made in the USA with a PFC-free C0 DWR
- Cut leaves room to layer over fleece or a light insulation piece

**Cons**

- Thin 10D fabric needs gentle handling, and the non-ripstop construction is a durability risk
- No vent options beyond the zipper
- Hood cinch cord ends flap in wind
- Loose fit billows when worn over a single base layer
- Less protective than heavier shells in sustained high wind or cold

## Who Should Buy This

Thru-hikers, fastpackers and gram-counters who want a layer that adds a few degrees at camp and on windy ridgelines, and who will wear it over a fleece, wool top or light insulation, are the target. It also suits runners and cyclists who need something that vanishes into a pocket. Hikers who want a bombproof shell for bushwhacking, or who expect real storm protection, should look at a heavier wind jacket or a proper rain shell. Anyone set on blocking cold, driving wind should specify the 20D fabric.

## Verdict

The Copperfield does one job, keeping wind off a mid layer while letting sweat out, at a weight that makes leaving it home hard to justify. Fussy finishing and delicate fabric hold it back from a higher score, but for ultralight use it remains a sensible, well-reviewed buy.

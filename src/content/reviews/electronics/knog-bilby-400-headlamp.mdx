## Overview

The Bilby 400 is Knog's flagship headlamp built around a genuinely different idea: instead of a plastic housing with a battery compartment and a cable dangling off the back, the entire light "pod" is a self-contained unit that pops out of a one-piece silicone headband and plugs directly into any USB-A port to charge. It's aimed at trail runners, hikers, and anyone who wants a rugged, waterproof headlamp without fussing with charging cables — though the tradeoff is a learning curve on the controls.

## Performance

The headline feature here is the cable-free charging, and it's the thing every reviewer keeps coming back to. The light pod slides out of the silicone strap and <cite index="8-6,8-7">has an exposed male USB that you can plug into a mobile battery pack or USB port, so you don't need wires to connect it to a battery or charger, which simplifies packing.</cite> On a thru-hike or bikepacking trip where you're already juggling a battery bank, one less cable to lose is a real quality-of-life win.

Output-wise, the numbers hold up in practice. <cite index="3-7,3-8">At the maximum 400 lumens you get 5 hours of runtime, and at the lowest setting you get 105 hours.</cite> One long-term user who wore the Bilby for four months of kayak fishing, camping, and hunting trips <cite index="3-5,3-6">put it through warm, muggy mornings, cool foggy mornings, a little rain, and being crammed into backpacks</cite> — and reported no issues with the waterproofing or durability after repeated drops and rough handling.

Beam quality is where opinions diverge a bit. One tester who spent real time on the bike noted that <cite index="11-7,11-8">the spread of light is quite wide, meaning the illuminating power is diluted somewhat, and he couldn't really discern much practical difference between the different modes — he'd have preferred a more focused, concentrated beam on full power</cite> for a dedicated headlamp use case. That tracks with what you'd expect from 5 LEDs optimized for different jobs rather than one big spot emitter — great for general area lighting and camp chores, less ideal if you need to punch light a long way down a dark trail.

The control scheme is the other recurring theme, and not always a positive one. Multiple reviewers mentioned an initial adjustment period: <cite index="3-16">one reviewer was initially a bit flustered about how to turn it on and adjust the settings before getting the hang of it with continued use</cite>, and another compared the learning curve to <cite index="7-9,7-10">figuring out an iPhone for the first time — two buttons control brightness, tilt, and boost across six modes, which is a bit intimidating until it clicks</cite>. The upside is that once you're past that hump, the two-button system lets you cycle through spot, mid, wide, red, and reading modes without ever taking the light off your head — and if the stock button mapping still doesn't suit you, Knog's ModeMaker app lets you reprogram it.

The reading/downlight mode is a sleeper favorite. It's a low, diffuse light aimed down at your hands or map rather than straight ahead, and it came up unprompted in more than one review as genuinely useful for camp chores, reading in a tent, or working under the hood of a car without blinding yourself or your tentmate.

On the strap itself, the medical-grade silicone earns its keep. It's designed to not <cite index="6-14,6-15">lose its elasticity over time, and it won't absorb moisture from rain or sweat, so it stays fresh and can just be wiped clean</cite> — a genuine advantage over the fabric straps that eventually start to smell no matter how often you wash them. The tradeoff, per at least one reviewer, is that <cite index="4-3">the silicone strap works best over a hat or with hair — worn directly against bare skin/hair for some people it's less comfortable than expected</cite>.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Cable-free USB-A charging is a genuinely clever solution — one less thing to pack or lose
- IP67-rated and field-tested through rain, splashes, and being crammed into packs with no issues reported
- 105-hour low-mode runtime is excellent for multi-night trips
- Silicone strap resists odor and doesn't degrade like fabric straps
- Reading/downlight mode is a standout feature for camp use
- ModeMaker app adds real customization if the stock modes don't fit your workflow

**Cons**

- Two-button interface has a real learning curve; expect to consult the manual at least once
- Beam is wide and diffuse rather than focused — not the best choice if you want a long-throw spot beam
- Some users find the silicone strap less comfortable directly against hair/skin without a hat
- At 90 g it's not the lightest option for ounce-counting ultralight backpackers, though it's competitive for its output class

## Who Should Buy This

The Bilby 400 makes the most sense for hikers, trail runners, and campers who want one do-everything headlamp and value the cable-free charging and waterproofing enough to tolerate a slightly fussy button interface. If you mostly need a simple point-and-go trail light and don't care about customization, a simpler single-mode headlamp will frustrate you less. But if you like tinkering with settings, need genuine IP67 durability, and want a reading light that doesn't require digging out a separate light source, this covers a lot of ground in one 90-gram package.

## Verdict

The Bilby 400 delivers on its core promise — a rugged, weatherproof, wire-free headlamp with strong runtime numbers and a genuinely useful reading mode — but the control scheme asks for patience you might not want to give it on night one. I'd rate it a 7.5/10: excellent hardware and charging design held back slightly by an interface that takes real effort to learn.

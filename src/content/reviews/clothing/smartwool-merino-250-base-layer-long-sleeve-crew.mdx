## Overview

The Merino 250 is Smartwool's best-selling base layer and, by the company's own description, its warmest: a 100% merino long-sleeve crew built for cold mornings, shoulder-season hiking, ski days and nights in the sleeping bag. At 276 g it is a cold-weather workhorse rather than a gram-counter's pick, and the trade-off is the usual one for midweight merino: superb comfort and odor control against modest durability and slow drying.

## Performance

**Warmth and breathability.** Outdoor Gear Lab's testers rank the Merino 250 at the top of their base layer lineup, crediting an unusual balance of warmth and breathability. They are clear about the limits of the category: the most breathable shirts in their tests are the thin summer pieces, and a 250 gsm top is a winter-focused garment by design. Greenbelly's reviewer reaches the same conclusion from the other direction, calling it too thick for most summer use and pointing warm-weather wearers toward 100–150 gsm fabrics. Reviewers also note that merino keeps insulating when damp, which is the reason to carry wool on a cold, sweaty climb in the first place.

**Comfort under a pack.** Seams are flatlocked throughout, so there is no raised stitching to rub against skin. Outdoor Gear Lab singled out the shoulder seams, which sit farther apart than on earlier versions. Testers found that made the shirt more comfortable under a heavy pack, particularly for broader frames. Greenbelly's reviewer rated comfort a perfect 10 for the same pack-and-pants scenario. One owner who usually finds wool itchy reported no irritation; another, reviewing it for running and skiing, found it less soft than expected.

**Versatility.** It works as a next-to-skin layer and also as a mid-layer over something thinner. Owners wear it solo under a shell on crisp autumn commutes, and one backcountry skier describes it as a year-round staple that doubles as camp and sleep wear when summer nights dip. The slim, tidy cut means it passes for casual wear in town, which matters on a resupply stop.

**Odor.** This is merino's party trick. Owners report that the shirt stays fresh through several days of sweating in the backcountry, so a multi-day trip needs one top, not three.

**Durability.** This is the weak spot. Outdoor Gear Lab says the thick knit stands up to multi-pitch climbing and hut trips. The retail picture is less rosy. A critical REI review cites three small holes after five machine washes and one hand wash. Other long-time buyers say recent revisions feel thinner and less robust than older ones, and some report inconsistent tailoring from shirt to shirt. Pure merino at this weight is not abrasion-proof, and the shirt will not take well to rough treatment from pack straps or brush.

**Care and drying.** Outdoor Gear Lab flags a recommendation for special laundering and slow air-drying. Wet wool takes longer to dry than a synthetic grid fleece. That's fine for a base layer worn all day, but it is a nuisance for a damp overnight rinse on a short, cool trip.

**Sizing.** Both Outdoor Gear Lab and several buyers say it runs large. Reviewers who wanted a snug, true base-layer fit recommend going down a size, and an owner who is 5'10" and 160 lb found a Small plenty long and fitted without feeling tight.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Strong warmth-to-breathability balance for cold-weather activity
- Flatlock seams and wide-set shoulder seams stay comfortable under a loaded pack
- Excellent odor resistance over multi-day trips
- Works as a base layer, mid-layer or camp and sleep top
- Slim, clean look that wears fine off-trail

**Cons**

- At 276 g, heavy by ultralight base layer standards
- Reported holes after a handful of washes; some long-time buyers say quality has slipped
- Slow to air-dry
- Runs large, so sizing down is common advice
- Expensive, and the care instructions are fussy

## Who Should Buy This

Cold-weather hikers, winter campers, backcountry skiers and shoulder-season thru-hikers who want one reliably warm, low-stink top that also doubles as sleepwear. It suits anyone who tends to run cold and wants a single shirt that can be layered up or down.

Skip it if the trip is warm or if the pack weight target is aggressive. A 100–150 gsm merino or a synthetic grid top is lighter, breathes better and dries faster. Rough-use hikers who bushwhack or wash heavily should also look at a merino-synthetic blend.

## Verdict

The Merino 250 is one of the most comfortable midweight wool crews available, and testers keep rating it near the top for regulating temperature on cold days. Its slow drying and uneven durability keep it from being an ultralight standout, so it is best bought as a cold-weather specialist rather than an all-season shirt. Size down if you want a close fit, and treat it gently in the wash.

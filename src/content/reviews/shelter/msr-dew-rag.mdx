## Overview

The MSR Dew Rag is a small, single-purpose cloth cut from PackTowl fabric, the same chamois-like material MSR sells in its camp towels. Its job is narrow: soak up condensation, spilled water and tracked-in mud inside a tent, then wring out and go again. It's discontinued, so this is now a secondhand-market and old-stock item. For ultralight hikers, the question is whether a dedicated rag earns its place against a pack of kitchen wipes.

## Performance

The Dew Rag's strength is its fabric. PackTowl material is the dense, felt-like cloth that backpackers have long used to dry off after a swim. Thru-hikers on Backpacking Light's towel threads single it out as the type that absorbs the most water and wrings out easily. That matters at 6 a.m., when a double-wall tent's fly is beaded with condensation and every pass of a cloth either clears a panel or just smears it around.

MSR's own condensation guidance points the same way. The company's tent team recommends wiping down the interior of the rainfly with a PackTowl or a similar camp towel. Alternatively, pitch with the fly pulled off the ground and the doors cracked, then lay the fly in the sun. The Dew Rag is the cloth version of that advice, sold in a smaller, tent-specific format.

The mesh sack is the one real design idea here. Stuff the rag inside and it becomes a sponge. That keeps wet fabric off your hands and gives you a firmer surface for pushing water out of a floor seam or a vestibule corner. The same sack handles carry. A wet rag can ride on the outside of the pack and drain, rather than soaking a stuff sack.

At 25 g, it weighs about the same as a few ounces of snacks, which is to say it vanishes in a pack. It also sits in an awkward spot. Hikers on Backpacking Light have long pointed out that supermarket wipes weigh around 0.2 oz each. Owners of those wipes report that they absorb well, dry faster than a PackTowl-style cloth and last a couple of seasons. Ultra-thin disposable towels go lighter still, though users say they can't fully dry a tent and only speed up air-drying. The Dew Rag falls between the two. It absorbs more than the thinnest options and costs several times the weight of a kitchen wipe.

Wet-weather behavior follows from the material. PackTowl-type cloth dries slowly in humid air, so a rag used at dawn can still be damp that night. It will also go stale if left balled up inside a sealed bag. Hikers who use these cloths tend to clip them to the outside of the pack and let the afternoon sun finish the job.

The practical limit is the tent, not the rag. In a single-wall shelter or a trekking-pole tarp with good ventilation, condensation often isn't a daily problem. One Backpacking Light poster said they stopped getting it after switching to a Hexamid. A rag is a fix for a double-wall tent pitched in a damp hollow, a cold-weather camp or a shoulder-season trip.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 25 g, light enough to carry as insurance
- PackTowl fabric absorbs far more than thin disposable wipes and wrings out easily
- Mesh sack turns the cloth into a sponge and gives it a drainable carry pouch
- Works on floors and vestibules, so it also handles muddy spills and wet gear
- Lifetime warranty on a durable cloth, according to retailer listings

**Cons**

- Discontinued, so buying one means hunting for old stock or secondhand
- Slower to dry than kitchen wipes or microfiber alternatives
- A basic PackTowl or a bag of Handi-Wipes does the same job for less
- Single-purpose: it earns no weight back as a camp towel or pot wipe

## Who Should Buy This

The Dew Rag suits a hiker with a double-wall tent who camps in cold, damp or coastal conditions, wants a dedicated mop and likes the sponge-in-a-sack idea. It's a better fit for weekend and shoulder-season trips than for dry-climate thru-hikes. Gram counters who already carry a multi-use towel, or who sleep under a tarp or a well-ventilated single wall, should skip it. So should anyone unwilling to chase a discontinued product. A current MSR PackTowl or a few kitchen wipes cover the same ground.

## Verdict

The Dew Rag works as described: a small, absorbent, quick-wringing cloth with a clever sack for mopping. Its problem is that it's discontinued, and the jobs it does are already covered by towels and wipes that are easier to find and, in the wipes' case, lighter and faster-drying. Buy one if a clean used example turns up cheap and your tent drips. Otherwise, a PackTowl is the sensible replacement.

## Overview

The Fujifilm X100V is a fixed-lens APS-C compact built around a 26.1MP X-Trans CMOS 4 sensor and a 23mm f/2 Fujinon lens, which frames like a 35mm on full frame. It weighs 478 g, so it rides on a shoulder strap or pack hip belt without the lens-swapping baggage of an interchangeable-lens kit. The headline trade-off: it delivers image quality that rivals much larger systems, but its weather resistance is only complete if the buyer pays extra for an adapter ring and filter.

## Performance

**Image quality.** The lens is the story. Earlier X100 models drew complaints about softness at close range; reviewers at Cameralabs and DPReview found the redesigned optics on the V crisp near and far. The 26.1MP back-illuminated sensor and X-Processor 4 pair with Fujifilm's film simulations, which owners cite repeatedly as the reason to buy: JPEGs that look finished straight out of the camera, so there is little to do at a campsite except cull.

**Handling.** The control scheme is analog: a shutter-speed dial with ISO built in, an aperture ring on the lens, and an exposure compensation dial. Reviewers describe it as a camera that makes the shooter think about composition, since there is no zoom to lean on. The tilting 1.62m-dot touchscreen allows hip-level and low-angle shots. Amateur Photographer's testers flagged a few gripes: the body is slippery in cold or wet hands, the Q-menu button is tiny, and the touchscreen can't navigate the main menu.

**Viewfinder and autofocus.** The hybrid finder switches between an optical window and a 0.66x-magnification OLED EVF with 3.69m dots. Autofocus uses 91 points of hybrid contrast and phase detection, and testers praised coverage across the frame and improved low-light performance over the X100F. A built-in four-stop ND filter helps with waterfalls and bright alpine light at f/2. Cameralabs noted it isn't available for video.

**Battery.** CIPA ratings are 420 shots with the optical finder and 350 with the EVF, a clear step up from the X100F's 390 and 270. CIPA numbers run conservative, and Creative Bloq's tester found one battery lasted most of a day of shooting. The Phoblographer still wished for more. Alongside the 200-shot rating DPReview lists for the Ricoh GR III, the V looks sturdy, but a multi-day trip still calls for spares. USB-C charging in the camera means a power bank can top it up on trail. Amateur Photographer reports that wireless image transfer drains the battery quickly.

**Weather resistance.** This is the sore point. The body is sealed, but the extending lens is not, so the camera counts as weather-sealed only with the optional AR-X100 adapter ring and a 49mm filter on the front. Imaging Resource put the official ring at about $45 and called its absence from the box annoying on a camera of this price. Third-party filters such as NiSi's UV can complete the seal for less than Fujifilm's own kit. Owners who shoot in rain, sand or spray report the ring-and-filter setup is the first accessory to buy.

**Video.** Capture goes up to C4K and 4K UHD at 30p, with a 2.5mm mic port and headphone monitoring through a USB-C adapter. The camera has no in-body stabilization, which matters for handheld clips.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Sharp 23mm f/2 lens with APS-C image quality in a 478 g body
- Hybrid optical/electronic viewfinder and a tilting touchscreen
- Built-in four-stop ND filter
- USB-C in-camera charging and power delivery
- Film simulations deliver usable JPEGs with minimal editing

**Cons**

- Not fully weather-sealed without a separately bought adapter ring and filter
- Battery life is middling for multi-day use; spares are needed
- No image stabilization and no zoom or lens options
- Slippery in cold or wet hands; small Q-menu button
- Launched at $1,400, and owners have long reported it hard to find in stock

## Who Should Buy This

The X100V suits hikers who care about photographs and want a camera that stays out on a chest strap or pocket instead of staying in the pack. It rewards photographers comfortable with a single 35mm-equivalent field of view and who will walk closer instead of zooming. Those who want wildlife or distant-peak reach, a rain-ready camera out of the box, or stabilized video should look at an interchangeable-lens Fujifilm body or a stabilized compact instead. Anyone who wants the same format with newer features can also look at the successor, the X100VI.

## Verdict

The X100V remains one of the most satisfying carry-everywhere cameras for hikers who prioritize image quality over versatility. Budget for the adapter ring, a filter and at least one spare battery, and it handles most conditions a trail throws at it. The fixed lens and incomplete weather sealing hold it back from being the default pick for everyone.

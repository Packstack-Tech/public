## Overview

The Zpacks Medium-Plus Dry Bag is an 8.2-liter roll-top sack made from 1.0 oz/sqyd Dyneema Composite Fabric. It weighs 20 g (0.71 oz), about what a few sheets of printer paper weigh. It's built for backpackers who want a sleeping bag or a clothing kit kept dry inside a pack. The trade-off is price: $37.95 for what is, functionally, a very good bag for your quilt.

## Performance

**Weight and packability.** At 20 g, the Medium-Plus sits in the territory where a dry bag stops being something to weigh and starts being something to forget. It is only 3 g heavier than Zpacks' own Medium (17 g), and the extra 2.6 liters buy meaningfully more room. Empty, it folds to roughly the size of a wallet. Hikers on gear forums who have tried several brands regularly name Zpacks' Dyneema bags as contenders for the lightest dry bags available, and they pick Dyneema over nylon options such as Sea to Summit's for exactly that reason.

**Capacity.** The 8.2 liters suit the job Zpacks sells it for: an average ultralight sleeping bag, or a stack of clothing layers. Owners describe it as the right size for a 40-degree bag plus the clothes worn to sleep, and one reports it fits crosswise in a Zpacks Arc Blast without wasting space. Zpacks cuts it to lie horizontally in most packs, which suits a quilt or bag stored at the bottom. Anyone carrying a heavier winter bag or a bulky puffy will want to move up a size.

**Weather protection.** The fabric is waterproof on its own, and the seams are taped, so there's no coating to wear off. The roll-top closure, with a stiffened opening to hold the folds, handles rain, stream splashes and a wet pack bottom. Zpacks is plain about the limits: the bags are meant for typical backpacking conditions where they aren't submerged. That's fine for trail use. It rules the bag out as the only protection on a packrafting trip with real swim risk, though it works well as an inner layer in a boat. A roll-top is also only as watertight as the rolling. Trapped air or a lazy two-turn fold lets water find a way in.

**Durability.** Dyneema Composite Fabric is notably strong for its weight, and Zpacks calls the 1.0 oz material stronger than the fabric in its standard stuff sacks. Roll-top bags see the most wear at the folds and corners, and Dyneema can show creasing and stiffening there over time. That is cosmetic rather than a leak. The bag is stiffer and crinklier than a siliconized nylon sack, and some users find it harder to stuff into tight pack corners. In exchange it doesn't absorb water or stretch out.

**Value.** At $37.95, the bag costs several times what a basic nylon dry sack does. That's the price of the weight and the fabric. The same 8.2-liter body is also available as a Medium-Plus Pillow Dry Bag ($59.95, 46 g) with fleece sewn into half the interior. Anyone who uses a dry bag as a pillow can pay for that version. Those who don't can skip the extra 26 g and $22.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 20 g for 8.2 liters of waterproof storage
- Fully taped seams on fabric that is waterproof without a coating
- Sized for an ultralight sleeping bag or a full clothing kit, and lies flat in most packs
- Stiffened roll-top holds its folds
- Two-year warranty on defects

**Cons**

- $37.95 is a lot for a stuff sack
- Not rated for submersion
- Too small for heavier winter bags or bulky insulation
- Dyneema creases and crinkles with use, and it's stiffer than nylon when packing

## Who Should Buy This

Gram-counting backpackers and thru-hikers who carry a 30- to 40-degree quilt or bag, or a clothing kit of a few layers, and who want one dependable layer of wet-weather insurance inside the pack. It fits especially well in a pack that already lacks a waterproof liner or that is made of non-waterproof fabric. Hikers with a four-season bag, or those who need to protect gear on water, should buy a larger bag or a dedicated submersible dry bag. Hikers who can live with a few extra ounces and a lower budget can find cheaper nylon sacks that handle most of the job.

## Verdict

The Medium-Plus does one job well: it keeps a sleeping bag or a stack of layers dry at a weight that's nearly nothing. It is expensive and it isn't submersible, but within the limits of backpacking use, it is the right size and weight for the typical ultralight kit. Recommended for ultralight hikers who already shop in Dyneema.

## Overview

The Snow Peak LiteMax is a titanium canister stove that strips the concept down to its essentials: a burner, three folding pot supports, and a wire flame-control valve. <cite index="6-4">It's the lightest and smallest canister stove Snow Peak offers.</cite> <cite index="1-8">This stove is suited for spartan solo backpackers on short trips with a favorable weather forecast.</cite> If that description fits you, the LiteMax is worth a close look.

## Performance

### Weight and Packability

This is where the LiteMax earns its reputation. <cite index="21-12,21-14">It tips the scale at just 1.9 oz (53 g), and in a market where "ultralight" gets thrown around loosely, that label is accurate here.</cite> <cite index="21-16,21-17">With folding pot supports and wire handle, this stove folds down to an almost two-dimensional shape when it's time to pack up camp. The included velvety storage sack adds 0.2 oz.</cite> I can drop the whole thing — stove, sack, and a lighter — inside a 700 ml pot without noticing the extra bulk.

<cite index="7-9,7-10">
  The titanium components are made in Tsubame-Sanjo, a town with a rich
  metalworking history located within Niigata prefecture. Utensils, cookware,
  and drinkware are designed with a timeless style and built to last a lifetime.
</cite>
That's not just marketing copy:
<cite index="27-23,27-24">
  one long-distance hiker's LiteMax survived not only the Appalachian and
  Pacific Crest Trails, but made it all the way to Yellowstone on the
  Continental Divide Trail before it was cross-threaded — nearly 7,000 miles of
  use.
</cite>

### Boil Times and Fuel Efficiency

In calm conditions, the numbers are solid. <cite index="21-20,21-21">The LiteMax puts in a decent performance in calm conditions, boiling a liter of water in 5 minutes and 6 seconds.</cite> <cite index="21-6">It used 0.7 oz of fuel to bring one liter of water to boil in still air.</cite>

Wind, however, is the LiteMax's real Achilles' heel. <cite index="21-7,21-8,21-9">In a 2–4 mph fan test, efficiency evaporated. The stove burned through 1.2 oz of fuel over 15 minutes and failed to bring the water to a full boil — it only managed to push water temperature into the 170s°F.</cite> For reference, <cite index="24-7,24-8">the LiteMax performed poorly in wind likely due to its side burning ports, with boil times roughly three times longer than those of the SOTO WindMaster under similar conditions.</cite>

The practical takeaway: always seek shelter before you cook. A foam sit pad propped against your pack, a rock wall, or a boulder in your lee will do the job — don't count on the stove itself.

### Simmer Control

This is where the LiteMax quietly outclasses a lot of the competition. <cite index="27-7,27-8">While the MSR Pocket Rocket boiled water faster, it was difficult to simmer without burning the bottom of the pan. The LiteMax adjusts the flame much more gradually and is more effective at simmering.</cite> <cite index="22-16,22-17">A thoughtful feature is the bit of rubber grip added to the flame control valve. On other stoves, the valve can get quite hot, so the valve handle on the LiteMax stays cool-ish to the touch.</cite> For rehydrating meals or warming up a sauce, the fine control is genuinely useful.

There's a caveat: <cite index="22-20,22-21">the LiteMax leaves a bit to be desired in power efficiency because the burner head is so small, directing heat primarily to the center of the pot, which means it takes a bit longer to heat up.</cite> <cite index="3-13">The flame can also be quite weak at low settings, so keeping a close eye on it is important to make sure it hasn't blown out, wasting precious fuel.</cite>

### Pot Stability and Cookware Compatibility

<cite index="1-7">The pot supports, though sturdy, are small.</cite> A few users
report the supports feel loose when you're handling the stove before lighting,
but that's largely a tactile impression rather than a functional problem.
<cite index="29-16,29-17,29-18">
  One reviewer experienced some initial wobble, but found upon closer inspection
  that the screw holding the supports needed tightening — this completely
  remedied the issue, and it was stable as a tiny stove can be.
</cite>

<cite index="1-23">
  This stove is not well suited to pots much larger than 1 liter, or any but the
  smallest of frying pans.
</cite>
Stick to a 700–900 ml titanium cup or pot and you'll be fine. Try to cook for
two out of a 2L pot and you'll be pushing your luck on both stability and heat
distribution.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- <cite index="21-27,21-28">
    The 2 oz weight and small size are impressive — testers had a hard time
    imagining how a small canister stove could be much lighter or smaller.
  </cite>
- Exceptional simmer control with a cool-touch rubber valve grip
- <cite index="3-16">
    Difficult to find another stove that strikes as good a balance between
    weight and durability.
  </cite>
- <cite index="25-25,25-26">
    Setup is extremely simple and only takes a few seconds: screw it onto the
    canister, flip out the pot stand, turn on the burner and light it.
  </cite>
- <cite index="7-9">
    Titanium components are precision-made in Niigata, Japan
  </cite>
  — the craftsmanship shows

**Cons**

- <cite index="1-3">
    No piezoelectric igniter, something that's becoming standard on many stoves
  </cite>
  — bring a reliable lighter or two
- Wind performance is genuinely poor without a DIY shelter; not viable in exposed camps without intervention
- <cite index="22-23,22-24">
    Boil time in good conditions is around six minutes — longer than average for
    similar canister stoves — and uses about a half ounce of fuel
  </cite>
- Pot arm span limits you to ~1L cookware; not a group or basecamp stove
- <cite index="22-7,22-8">
    At the higher end of the cost spectrum for canister stoves, which isn't a
    surprise given the titanium construction
  </cite>

## Who Should Buy This

The LiteMax is built for the solo backpacker who has already decided that grams matter and wants a canister stove — not an alcohol burner — for the reliability and convenience of on-demand flame. <cite index="3-17">It's a stove CleverHiker's testers often take on solo ultralight trips when keeping size and weight low is the top priority.</cite> It's also a natural fit for thru-hikers who want something that nests inside their cook pot with room to spare, or as a dedicated backup stove that barely registers in your base weight. <cite index="10-7">This is a three-season stove, not designed for snow or winter camping</cite> — if you're heading into cold temps where canister performance degrades, look at remote canister or liquid-fuel options instead.

## Verdict

The Snow Peak LiteMax makes its case with weight and durability. At 56g, it's one of the lightest canister stoves available, and <cite index="22-9">it holds up through hundreds of miles of use.</cite> The tradeoffs — no igniter, narrow cookware compatibility, and poor wind resistance — are real, but they're the predictable costs of chasing that weight number. If you're camping in sheltered terrain or can reliably find a windbreak, none of those drawbacks will ruin a trip. Go in with eyes open, pack a windscreen for exposed nights, and this stove will outlast most of your other gear. **Rating: 8/10.**

## Overview

The SCARPA Helix is a flat, neutral, lace-up climbing shoe with a suede upper, a padded tongue and Vibram XS Edge rubber. It has been a default first shoe for years, and it is the kind of pair that stays in the bag long after beginners have moved on to something sharper. The trade-off is built into the design: comfort, support and longevity in exchange for the sensitivity and bite of a steeper, more downturned shoe.

## Performance

**Comfort.** This is the Helix's headline. Reviewers at Gneiss Climb describe it as comfortable even through all-day wear, crediting the suede upper, which molds to the foot, along with a cushioned heel cup and a padded tongue. One tester said it was already forgiving out of the box, unlike the many shoes that demand a week of suffering. Owners on REI's listing echo the point, calling the suede forgiving and the shoe a gentle way into the sport. That makes it a natural for the multi-pitch crowd, who spend hours on belay ledges and don't want to peel their shoes off after every pitch.

**Edging and support.** The 1.4 mm full-length nylon midsole and 3.5 mm of Vibram XS Edge give the Helix a stiff, supportive platform. Gneiss Climb found good edging support and decent smearing for a shoe this soft on the foot, and noted that the stiffness reduces fatigue and helps beginners build foot strength. Owners on Public Lands call it stiff enough for real support but nowhere near as rigid as an aggressive shoe like the Scarpa Instinct. One long-time owner summed it up as a general climbing shoe that does well enough at everything without wowing at any one thing.

**Where it runs out of road.** The flat last and neutral profile do not suit steep rock. Climbing House rates it for easy-to-moderate routes and notes that a more aggressive shoe gives extra power on tough overhangs. Reviewers consistently place it outside the territory of hard overhangs, aggressive heel hooks and technical bouldering. Longer-term owners say the same thing from the other direction: eventually, steep boulders and micro-edges call for a second pair, though many keep the Helix as a mileage or trad shoe.

**Fit.** Lacing lets climbers fine-tune the fit, and the shoe is cut medium in width. Gneiss Climb's testers flagged it as likely too narrow for wide feet, while climbers with Morton's toe reported a particularly good fit. Women's buyers on REI describe the sizing as close to street size, and one with narrow feet preferred it to the Scarpa Origin. A handful of owners report the opposite problems: one buyer's heel slipped even with a correct toe fit, one who sized down half a size found the break-in very long, and RunRepeat's review notes the suede can stretch more than some climbers like. Sizing down within reason is common practice, but the stretch means leaving room to tighten up.

**Durability.** This is the other strong suit. Owners on REI and Public Lands report three to four-plus years of weekly or twice-weekly gym use. Climbing House scores durability 9.5 out of 10 and calls the construction excellent for a budget shoe, and the Helix can be resoled, which stretches the value further. One buyer reported a hole they felt appeared early, and said the company called it normal wear, a reminder that gym use on abrasive holds is hard on any suede upper.

**Weight.** At roughly 215 g per men's shoe, it is not an ultralight rock shoe, but it is light enough for a multi-pitch rack-and-rope day pack and carries easily on approaches that involve scrambling.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Out-of-the-box comfort that holds up through long days
- Stiff midsole and XS Edge rubber give secure edging and reduce foot fatigue
- Laces allow a precise, adjustable fit
- Resoleable, with owners reporting multi-year lifespans
- Budget-friendly and handmade in Italy
- Handles gym, sport, trad and multi-pitch with one pair

**Cons**

- Flat last and neutral profile limit performance on steep or overhanging terrain
- Medium width runs too narrow for climbers with wide feet
- Suede stretch means the fit can loosen; some report heel slip
- Stiffness is a drawback for climbers who want sensitivity on small holds
- Reports of early wear on a minority of pairs

## Who Should Buy This

Beginners and early intermediates are the obvious audience, especially those who want one shoe that works in the gym on Tuesday and on a weekend multi-pitch. It also earns a spot in experienced climbers' quivers as an all-day trad or mileage shoe. Climbers with wide feet, or anyone whose goals lean toward steep sport routes, hard boulder problems or heel-hook-heavy climbing, should look at a more downturned, softer, more aggressive shoe instead.

## Verdict

The Helix is not exciting, and it does not pretend to be. It is a comfortable, durable, resoleable shoe that does the basics well and lets new climbers progress without an upgrade for a long time. For beginners and for anyone who wants a comfortable long-route shoe, it is an easy recommendation.

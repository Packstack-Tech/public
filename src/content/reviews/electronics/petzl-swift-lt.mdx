## Overview

The Petzl Swift LT is a 43 g rechargeable headlamp built around a thin shock-cord headband and a single wide beam. It is aimed at trail runners, thru-hikers and ski tourers who want a light they can forget they are wearing. The trade-off is plain: the Swift LT gives up beam versatility, pass-through charging and high-mode endurance to get to its weight.

## Performance

**Light output.** The Swift LT throws a wide, even wash of light rather than a focused spot. Wilderness Magazine's tester found the 100-lumen standard mode sufficient for well-formed tracks, with the 380-lumen max mode delivering an even 70 m reach for more technical ground. CleverHiker's testers reached a similar conclusion: standard mode handled most trail and camp tasks, and the top setting was rarely needed. The color skews cool and bluer than Petzl's pricier lamps, a quirk that matters more to tent-side readers than to people running a ridgeline.

**Weight and comfort.** This is where the Swift LT earns its place. Reviewers repeatedly describe it as the kind of lamp that disappears on the head, with one runner reporting that the lamp was forgotten mid-run. The ultra-thin band is adjusted by pulling a cord, and testers who expected a bungee strap to dig in or bounce found it did neither. The pivoting head clicks into position and holds its angle on descents, and it tilts upward so the lamp can hang around the neck and light hands and gear at camp. The band is removable and washable, and it has reflective thread woven through it.

**Battery life.** The numbers are honest but modest. Standard mode runs about 6 hours before a 2-hour reserve, enough for trail runs, evening camp chores and most multi-day trips. The 380-lumen mode lasts only 2 hours. The 7-lumen mode lasts 60 hours, though CleverHiker's testers found it too dim for moving over uneven terrain. Petzl's lamps follow the ANSI/PLATO standard for runtime reporting, and the Swift LT dims itself ahead of a full shutdown rather than cutting out.

**Charging.** A full charge takes around 3 hours over USB-C, and there is no pass-through, so a depleted lamp can't be run off a power bank. On a long night section or a multi-day trip, that means topping up before bed rather than trusting the indicator light the next morning. The indicator flashes the charge level each time the lamp is switched on or off.

**Controls and red light.** Operation is a single button. The lamp starts on its lowest setting and cycles upward, which spares night vision and eyes. A long press switches to red, and a further click gives strobe. A lock mode keeps the lamp from switching on in a pack. CleverHiker's testers called the red light fairly dim, so it works for preserving night vision at camp but not for much else.

**Weather.** IPX4 means splash resistance from any direction. A UK ultrarunner reported several wet runs without trouble. Anyone expecting to submerge it or sit through sustained downpours with a lot of faith should look at lamps with higher ratings.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 43 g, among the lightest rechargeable headlamps with this much output
- Very comfortable, stable band that stays put when running
- Simple one-button interface with a lock mode
- Beam tilts and holds its angle; can be worn around the neck
- Battery indicator and reserve dimming prevent surprise blackouts
- USB-C charging; band is washable and replaceable

**Cons**

- Fixed wide beam with no spot, and a 70 m maximum throw
- Only 2 hours at 380 lumens
- No pass-through charging; about 3 hours to recharge
- Red light is dim
- Integrated battery cannot be swapped for AAAs in the field
- IPX4 rating only
- Cool, bluish light color

## Who Should Buy This

Thru-hikers who walk mostly in daylight and use a headlamp for camp chores, early starts and the occasional night-hiking stretch will get all they need from this light and barely feel the weight. Trail runners and ski tourers will appreciate the stable fit. It also works as a backup lamp for long-distance runners and as a bottom-of-the-pack spare.

Anyone who expects to hike for hours in the dark, navigate by headlamp, or rely on a spot beam for route finding should buy something brighter with a focusable beam and longer high-mode runtime. People who prefer replaceable AAA or AAA-compatible cells for multi-week resupply stretches without power should also look elsewhere.

## Verdict

The Swift LT delivers what it promises: 380 lumens at 43 g, comfortably worn and easy to operate. The compromises are real but predictable, and most hikers who already carry a small power bank will accept them. For ultralight users, it is an easy recommendation at $54.95.

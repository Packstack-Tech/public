## Overview

The GSI Outdoors Essential Travel Spoon is a 9.9-inch nylon spoon with a silicone-coated bowl and edge, built for one job: getting to the bottom of a dehydrated-meal pouch without wearing dinner to the wrist. It costs about $5 to $6 at most retailers and has become a common fixture in thru-hiker food bags. The trade-off is weight. At 28 g it is roughly twice what a titanium long-handled spoon weighs, and that is the price of the reach and the soft edge.

## Performance

**Reach.** The handle is the reason to buy this spoon. A boil-in-bag freeze-dried meal with the top torn off is still about 20 cm deep, and a standard camp spoon forces fingers, or winter gloves, into the sauce. At 9.9 inches, this one clears the pouch with room to spare. Buyers describe it as the right length for eating from a freeze-dried bag without reaching into it. The same length stirs a larger pot without a hand hovering over the steam.

**Scraping.** The tapered silicone edge flexes against the corners of a pouch or pot and collects what a rigid spoon leaves behind. In a seven-spoon test by Far Out Guides, the GSI Essential spoons, small and long, led the field in peanut butter removal. They cleared 93 percent after three scoops. That matters most for the last third of a pouch, and for cleaning a pot at camp when water and fuel are both worth saving.

**Surface safety.** Nylon and silicone won't scratch nonstick coatings, and reviewers note the spoon won't puncture the thin liners of freeze-dried meal bags, a failure that sharp-edged titanium tips are known for. For anyone cooking in a coated pot, that is a practical advantage.

**Durability.** The nylon core is stiff enough for stirring thick rehydrating meals. One long-term owner reports a decade of use before a rodent finally chewed through the first spoon, then bought a replacement. The limit is the material. As Far Out Guides noted, these spoons aren't as strong as titanium, and scraping burned-on food off a pot is better left to a metal tool.

**Bowl and weight.** Critics have two complaints. One is that the bowl is deep for a one-tablespoon spoon, which makes it awkward for some eaters and slow to deliver food. The other is weight. Black Sheep Adventure Sports weighed an early sample at 25 g and put it 5 to 10 g above many alternatives. They called the heft the trade-off for a handle that is both long and sturdy. Retailer reviews are mixed on the details: REI shoppers average 3.8 out of 5 across 96 ratings, while Walmart buyers average 4.8 across 26.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Handle is long enough to eat from freeze-dried pouches without dirtying hands or gloves
- Flexible silicone edge scrapes pots and bag corners cleanly
- Won't scratch coated cookware or puncture meal bags
- Costs about $5 to $6
- Lifetime limited warranty

**Cons**

- 28 g is heavy next to titanium long-handled spoons
- Deep bowl is awkward for some users
- Less rigid than metal, and a poor choice for scraping burned food
- Long handle takes up more space in a pack's cook kit than a short spoon

## Who Should Buy This

This is the spoon for hikers who live on dehydrated dinners and want to eat straight from the bag. It also suits anyone cooking in a nonstick pot, and anyone who cares more about a clean pot and a clean hand than about shaving a few grams. It is cheap enough to carry as a backup. Base-weight-focused hikers who count every gram, and cold-soakers eating out of wide-mouth jars, should look at a titanium long spoon or a lighter plastic one instead.

## Verdict

The Essential Travel Spoon does the unglamorous work of trail dining well, and it does it for the price of a candy bar. Its weight keeps it out of the lightest kits, but for pouch eaters in coated pots it is a sensible, durable buy.

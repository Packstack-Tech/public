## Overview

The NeoAir UberLite is Therm-a-Rest's attempt to take the weight out of a full-length insulated air pad, and it succeeds: 250 g for a 72-inch pad with 2.5 inches of loft. It is built for thru-hikers and gram-counters who sleep in three-season conditions and are willing to treat their pad with some care. The trade is plain. Paper-thin 15-denier fabric and a modest R-value buy the weight and packed size.

## Performance

**Weight and packability.** This is the UberLite's whole argument. Backpacking Light put the original Regular at just over 3 oz lighter than the NeoAir XLite of the time, and Exploring Wild found it more than 5 oz lighter than Sea to Summit's ultralight air pad. Backpacking Light's own scale gave 224 g for the mattress alone and 235 g with stuff sack and repair kit, so the 250 g claim is honest. Rolled, it is about the size of a beer can, and it disappears into the bottom of a 40-liter pack. Reviewers who have used the half-length Small say it drops weight further, to around 170 g, at the cost of a pack or spare clothing under the legs.

**Comfort.** At 2.5 inches, the pad has enough loft for side sleepers. A 5'2" PCT hiker wrote in The Trek that it cushioned all sleeping positions, and a more recent tester reported that the hips did not bottom out. Triangular Core Matrix baffles keep it from feeling like a pool toy. The complaint is width: 20 inches tapering to a mummy foot leaves a restless sleeper's arm hanging off the side. The fabric also has a slick, slightly clammy feel against bare skin on warm nights, and an empty pad will skate around a tent in a breeze.

**Warmth.** The R-value is 2.3 under the current ASTM standard, which makes this a summer pad with a shoulder-season reach. Reviewers at Backpackers.com called it best for hikers who accept being the coldest in camp. Hikers who sleep cold, or who expect frost, usually pair it with a thin foam pad such as a Z Lite. A foam pad adds weight, but it also adds insurance against punctures. For sustained cold-weather use the XLite or XTherm are the right step up.

**Noise.** Opinions split here. CleverHiker calls it the quietest pad in Therm-a-Rest's backpacking line, and The Trek's reviewer found it audible but not disruptive. The UberLite drops the crinkly reflective film that gave earlier NeoAirs their potato-chip-bag sound. Even so, Halfway Anywhere's reviewer was woken by creaking from rolling over, and called it loud. Light sleepers and tent-sharers should not assume it will be silent.

**Durability.** This is the real cost. The 15D fabric is far thinner than the 30D used on the XLite, and every reviewer who tested the pad for long says so. Some hikers have logged 1,500 trail miles without a puncture, using a groundsheet. Others have picked up their first-ever hole on a single bad campsite. Cowboy camping on sand, gravel or pine needles without a ground cloth is how most UberLites die. Careful owners should expect about one long thru-hike from the pad. The included repair kit is worth keeping in the pack, not the bounce box.

**Inflation.** The included pump sack saves lung work and keeps breath moisture out of the baffles, though it adds weight of its own. The WingLock valve inflates and deflates quickly, and its one-way mode keeps air in while the hiker pauses for breath.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- At 250 g, it is among the lightest full-length insulated air pads made
- Packs to roughly 15 x 9 cm
- 2.5 inches of loft is enough for side sleepers
- Quieter than the older reflective-film NeoAirs
- Pump sack, stuff sack and repair kit included
- Small and Large sizes are available for torso-length or wider setups

**Cons**

- 15D fabric is fragile and calls for a groundsheet and careful campsite selection
- R-value of 2.3 limits it to warm three-season use
- 20-inch tapered width is tight for restless sleepers
- Still audible to light sleepers, according to some owners
- Costs about $230, and the lifespan may be a single season or one thru-hike

## Who Should Buy This

The UberLite suits thru-hikers and weekend minimalists who carry a base weight under about 10 pounds, hike mostly in warm months, and are comfortable checking the ground before they pitch. It also makes sense for bikepackers and fastpackers who value packed volume as much as ounces. Hikers who often sleep on rough ground, cowboy camp without a ground cloth, or sleep in frost should look at the NeoAir XLite NXT or a sturdier pad. Cold sleepers and partners who share a tent with a light sleeper should do the same.

## Verdict

The UberLite does what it says: it delivers a real, comfortable, inflatable pad for the weight of a thick sandwich, and nothing else in its class comes close on grams. It is a specialist tool, and owners who treat it that way report good results. Buy it for a summer thru-hike where weight is the priority. Buy something tougher if the pad needs to last for years.

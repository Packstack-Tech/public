## Overview

The Vargo Titanium Nail Peg is a 6-inch titanium nail with a flat head, a straight square-profile shaft and a reflective pull cord. It is built for the ground that destroys ordinary stakes: frozen dirt, compacted trail corridors, gravel and root-laced duff. The trade-off is simple. It will survive nearly anything it can be driven into, but a slim nail has little surface area to hold in loose or sandy soil.

## Performance

The nail peg's reputation rests on one trait: it does not bend. A GearJunkie tester who used an axe as a mallet reported driving a dozen of them into frozen or rocky soil at each campsite with ease. A tester there also described being done with the frustration of bending stakes on hidden boulders. A winter camper on CampSaver reported driving them into frozen ground with no deformation. On Backpacking Light, one forum poster called the standard version the only stake he had found strong enough for truly frozen ground. He drives them with an MSR stake hammer.

That strength has a side effect. The same forum thread notes that a nail will punch straight through a root without the user noticing, and then it is hard to pull out. Sometimes the only fix is to dig around it. Rocks still win, though owners say the shaft will sometimes deflect around one. One long-time user had a stake snap on a bikepacking trip in soft, wet ground, which he blamed on catching a rock at a bad angle. Failures of the standard peg are rare in owner reports.

Holding power is the other side of the ledger. A 5 mm shaft has far less surface area than a V-beam or Y-stake, and Vargo's own marketing leans on strength rather than grip. One Backpacking Light poster found the Vargo nails less effective in very sandy or very hard soil than in rocky ground. Reviewers who like them generally pair them with a toggle: a small stick through the cord loop helps in softer soil. Snow and sand call for something wider.

The pull cord does real work. It makes the stake easier to find in grass, and on a frozen morning it gives a hand-hold for extraction without a pair of pliers. The flat head takes a hammer, rock or axe without mushrooming. Owners also say the stake stays out of the way in the pack, since the stake is only 6 inches long and the heads are welded rather than epoxied.

At 14–15 g, the peg is not the lightest option. It is a few grams heavier than a basic aluminum V-stake, and well above Vargo's own Ultralight Nail Peg, which weighs 8 g and uses a 4 mm shaft. The lighter version has a worse record. Buyer reviews of the UL describe breaks at the cord eyelet or the head weld, including a PCT hiker who lost three of four. The standard peg has no such pattern of complaints, and one owner said it had worked splendidly for years while a UL stake failed on its first trip. A hiker heading for the rocky Larapinta Trail in Australia was told to skip the UL version and buy the heavy ones. Another owner compared them to steel pegs he owns that weigh roughly six times as much.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Does not bend when hammered into frozen, rocky or compacted ground
- 14–15 g is far lighter than steel nails of similar toughness
- Reflective cord helps with finding and pulling the stake
- Flat head accepts a mallet, axe or rock
- Strong owner satisfaction: Vargo's own page shows 93% of 54 reviews at the top rating

**Cons**

- Slim shaft has limited holding power in sand, loose soil and snow
- Heavier than aluminum V-stakes and the UL nail
- Can lodge in roots and be tough to extract
- Needs a hammer or rock for the hardest ground, which adds a small kit item
- Costs more than aluminum stakes, at roughly $4.50–5.35 each from retailers

## Who Should Buy This

Winter campers, alpine and desert hikers, and anyone whose route crosses hard, stony or frozen ground will get the most from the nail peg. It suits people who have bent aluminum stakes and want a stake they can strike without worry. It is a good replacement for the stock stakes that come with ultralight tents, since those are often the lightest option rather than the most durable.

Hikers who mostly pitch on soft forest duff, sand, or snow should look at wider, grippier stakes. Those who need to shave every gram should accept the lighter UL peg only for soft ground. Most owners say six stakes isn't enough for a full tent setup, so budget for a handful.

## Verdict

The Titanium Nail Peg is the dependable choice for ground that defeats lighter stakes, and its few failures come from bad angles, not weak metal. Buy it for frozen, rocky or hard-packed campsites, and carry a few wider stakes for sand and snow.

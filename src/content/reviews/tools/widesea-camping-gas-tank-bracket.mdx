## Overview

The Widesea Camping Gas Tank Bracket is a folding tripod stand that clips onto the base of a standard isobutane/propane canister, widening its footprint so your stove doesn't tip over on uneven ground. It's aimed at backpackers and car campers running screw-on canister stoves (JetBoil-style setups, Soto, MSR PocketRocket, etc.) who've had a near-miss with a wobbly fuel can on a rock or tree root. At 30 grams, it's the kind of accessory you buy once for a few bucks and then forget you own—until it saves your dinner.

## Performance

This is about as low-tech as backpacking gear gets, so there's not much to "test" beyond does it clip on and does it keep the canister upright. It does both, with a caveat or two.

The three plastic legs fold out from a central hub and lock onto the crimped bottom edge of a standard canister. Widesea's own listing touts a <cite index="1-3,1-4,1-5">"double fixed design" that's meant to be more stable than competing brackets, with two size options to fit most gas canisters on the market and a rubber pad on the underside for grip</cite>. In practice, that rubber pad is the feature that matters most—on a picnic table or packed dirt it genuinely helps keep the canister from sliding when you're leaning on the pot to stir something. On loose gravel or a canted rock, don't expect miracles; a wider base helps against tipping, but it won't stop the whole assembly from sliding if the surface itself is unstable.

Similar ABS tripod stabilizers from other brands (Fire-Maple, AOTU, Caudblor) show up constantly in the same searches, and the marketing language is nearly identical across the board—wider base, tripod legs, folds flat into your pot. That's telling: this is a generic, well-proven form factor manufactured by several factories, and Widesea's version doesn't reinvent it. The value proposition is the same one you get from any of them: for a few grams and a few dollars, you trade a small amount of pack space for meaningfully reduced risk of a stove tip-over, which matters more than people think when you're cooking on a picnic table edge, a slanted rock, or snow.

One legitimate limitation worth flagging: like every bracket in this category, it clips onto upright canisters. If you're trying to run a canister inverted for cold-weather liquid-feed performance, this bracket won't help you—that's a different problem requiring a different tool (windscreen-based inverter rigs, as discussed on backpacking forums, rather than a simple stand).

Fit is the other variable. Widesea says it works with "most standard" canisters, and the size range across brands in this category typically covers 100g/butane through 450g canisters, but crimped-bottom shapes vary slightly by manufacturer (MSR vs. Jetboil vs. Coleman fuel, for example). If you've got an odd canister on hand, it's worth test-fitting before you're relying on it in the field.

Durability-wise, ABS plastic is fine for this application—it's not bearing much load, just resisting lateral tipping forces—but I wouldn't expect it to survive being crushed at the bottom of a pack for years. Treat it like the disposable-ish accessory it is.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Genuinely useful safety margin against tipped canisters on uneven ground, picnic tables, or snow
- 30g is negligible weight for the stability gained
- Folds flat and stores inside most cookpots or stove bags
- Rubber grip pad adds real traction on hard, flat surfaces
- Cheap—low-risk purchase

**Cons**

- Won't help on loose gravel, sand, or steeply sloped terrain—wider base doesn't fix an unstable surface
- Doesn't address inverted-canister setups for cold-weather stove use
- Generic design shared across a dozen near-identical competitor products—nothing differentiates Widesea's version specifically
- ABS plastic legs are a wear item; don't expect years of abuse
- Sizing is "most" canisters, not "all"—worth test-fitting your specific fuel can

## Who Should Buy This

This is a sensible add for anyone running a canister stove on a pot stand or heavier cookset—the taller and top-heavier your setup, the more a wobbly canister becomes a real hazard rather than a theoretical one. If you're already ultralight-minimalist with a tiny titanium pot and a feather-light stove, you may never have felt the need. But for car campers, group cooking, or anyone who's had a canister tip mid-boil, 30 grams is a trivial cost for the peace of mind.

## Verdict

The Widesea bracket does exactly what a $5–10 canister stabilizer is supposed to do: widen your fuel can's footprint and add some grip, for next to no weight or cost. It's not a unique product—nearly identical brackets from half a dozen brands crowd the same search results—but that also means the category itself is a proven, low-risk buy. I'd rate it a 7/10: solid, unglamorous, does its job, just don't expect it to save you on truly unstable terrain.

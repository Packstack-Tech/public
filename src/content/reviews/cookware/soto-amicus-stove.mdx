## Overview

The SOTO Amicus is a top-mount canister stove from Japanese manufacturer SOTO Outdoors, targeting backpackers who want reliable, lightweight performance without paying a premium. <cite index="11-9,11-10">It's an impressive addition to a competitive field of canister stoves — affordable, incredibly lightweight, and fairly fuel-efficient.</cite> It sits below the WindMaster in SOTO's lineup and competes directly with the MSR PocketRocket 2 and Snow Peak LiteMax, but carves out its own niche through a combination of build quality, simmer control, and partial wind resistance that most budget stoves simply don't offer.

## Performance

### Boil Time & Heat Output

<cite index="7-1">
  During testing, the stove managed to boil one litre of water in just under
  four minutes during ideal conditions — impressive for a stove at this price
  point.
</cite>
Real-world results will creep up from there with wind, cold temps, or a
near-empty canister.
<cite index="4-9">
  The maximum estimated 2-cup boils per 100g fuel canister is around 13
</cite>
, which is respectable but not class-leading.
<cite index="11-29">
  OGL's lab testing puts average fuel consumption at about 0.28 oz per boil.
</cite>
That's enough to make fuel planning straightforward for most trips — a single
100g canister comfortably covers a solo weekend.

### Wind Resistance

This is where the Amicus earns its reputation, and also where you need to set realistic expectations. <cite index="5-21">The stove has a slightly recessed burner and a raised burner crown that serves to protect the flame from wind.</cite> <cite index="1-2,1-3">In both lab and field testing, the Amicus performed well in friendlier wind conditions with about a 20% reduction in fuel efficiency and a slight increase in boil times. However, it completely blew out with moderate to strong gusts, especially at lower flame settings for simmering.</cite> In practice, cook in a sheltered spot and you'll rarely have a problem. Expect to babysit it in exposed terrain. If you're regularly cooking above treeline in windy conditions, the WindMaster is worth the extra coin and grams.

### Simmer Control

This is genuinely one of the Amicus's stronger suits, and it's what separates it from a lot of stoves in this price range. <cite index="1-1">The stove simmers quite well thanks to the long wire fuel valve, which lets you dial down the burner to sustain a low enough flame to simmer without flickering out.</cite> <cite index="8-1,8-2">Both the Amicus and WindMaster are equipped with long control wires making it easy to reach and adjust the flame without burning your fingers — and the bonus of the control wire is control.</cite> If you're cooking actual food rather than just boiling water for a freeze-dried pouch, this matters.

### Pot Stability

Four spring-loaded pot supports beat the three-pronged designs on most competitors. <cite index="16-19,16-20,16-21">Part of why the Amicus has such good pot stability is that it has four pot supports rather than the typical three, and those supports have more "teeth" that are precisely made, gripping pots better than the rounded-off supports found on many other stoves.</cite> That said, <cite index="8-38,8-39,8-40">when compared to the WindMaster, the Amicus's spring-loaded pot supports look short and appear less stable — they perform adequately on a 2-liter cookpot, but confidence in stability is somewhat lacking; a 1-liter cookpot feels more fitting.</cite>

### Build Quality & Durability

<cite index="14-30">
  A detail worth noting is that the moving parts are riveted and notched as
  opposed to screwed in place, reducing the likelihood of things coming loose.
</cite>
<cite index="7-28,7-29,7-30">
  The Triple O-ring valve system proved reliable in testing, ensuring steady
  fuel flow and reducing the risk of leaks. The stove feels durable despite its
  81g frame and thin arms — it's been taken on several hikes with rough handling
  and continues performing.
</cite>
SOTO's customer support also has a solid reputation:
<cite index="10-25,10-26">
  when one user's igniter got too sandy on the PCT, SOTO sent a free replacement
  igniter kit — easily exchangeable, excellent support.
</cite>

### Igniter Reliability (With-Igniter Version)

The piezo igniter is the Amicus's most consistently cited weak point. <cite index="2-33,2-34">The igniter was unreliable as an ignition source for some users — there was always a visible spark, but it would take multiple attempts and fiddling with fuel flow to ignite.</cite> <cite index="18-16,18-17">One reviewer summarized it neatly: "good wind resistance, adjustable flame, 4 support legs, compact, great value" — but the piezo "doesn't work consistently enough to be worth it."</cite> My honest take: buy the igniter version for the option, but always carry a lighter. The igniter-free version saves 6g and sidesteps the frustration entirely if you're a sparker-skeptic.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Very light at 75g (without igniter) — competitive with most budget ultralight stoves
- Attached four-prong pot supports with no loose parts to lose
- Long wire valve delivers genuinely useful simmer control
- Better wind resistance than most stoves at this price point
- Triple O-ring valve is reliable and leak-resistant
- Compatible with all standard Lindal valve isobutane canisters
- <cite index="8-7">Earned OGL's "Best Buy" award</cite> — strong cross-metric
  value
- <cite index="4-13">
    Outperformed the MSR PocketRocket and Snow Peak LiteMax in both boil times
    and fuel efficiency in comparative testing
  </cite>

**Cons**

- <cite index="6-7">
    The WindMaster is a tiny bit heavier and bulkier, but boils water quicker,
    is more efficient, and is significantly more windproof
  </cite>
  — and the price gap has narrowed
- Blows out in strong, sustained gusts
- Piezo igniter is inconsistent for some users — carry a backup lighter regardless
- Pot supports are less inspiring with larger (1L+) cookware
- No pressure regulation, so output drops as the canister empties in cold temps

## Who Should Buy This

The Amicus hits its sweet spot for three-season backpackers, JMT-style thru-hikers, and weekend warriors who want a stove that does everything competently without a premium price tag. <cite index="8-18">If you're just looking for a reliable, unfussy, and lightweight backpacking stove to get the job done, the SOTO Amicus is your friend.</cite> It's especially well-suited for solo hikers pairing it with a 550–750ml titanium or titanium-alloy pot. If you're cooking in regularly exposed, high-wind environments or want maximum fuel efficiency for a long haul like the PCT, the step up to the WindMaster is worth considering.

## Verdict

The SOTO Amicus punches well above its price class. <cite index="11-13,11-14">Can you find stoves that perform better in one specific metric? Sure — but there aren't many that perform as well across all metrics at such an affordable price.</cite> The wind resistance won't impress anyone coming from a WindMaster, and the piezo igniter on the igniter version is a known gamble, but neither flaw is a dealbreaker. At 75g with solid simmer capability, attached pot supports, and Japanese build quality, this stove earns a **8.5/10** and a strong recommendation for most three-season backpackers.

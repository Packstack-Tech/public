## Overview

The Razor APX is a compact fixed-handle knife with a 2.5-inch replaceable drop point blade, and it stores four spares inside its own grip. Outdoor Edge designed it for backpack hunters who want a fresh edge on demand and a tool that can be stripped down to a skeleton when every gram counts. The trade-off is the usual one for disposable-blade systems: the edge is sharp and easy to renew, but the steel is basic and the knife is not built for heavy leverage.

## Performance

**Blade changes and storage.** The APX's party trick is the handle. Two magnetic retention plates sit under the silicone scales, each holding two spare blades, and the scales cover them against the elements. Owners say the push-button RazorSafe swap is quick and intuitive. One reviewer who tested it in cold, wet conditions reported that it stayed simple to operate even then. For anyone who has hunted for a loose blade in a pack pocket, carrying spares inside the knife is a real convenience.

**Cutting.** The short blade suits detail work: caping, small game, and food prep on a trip. Reviewers describe the edge as consistently sharp, and the practical benefit is that a dulled blade becomes a 10-second swap, not a sharpening session. In a field-butchering test of the APX system, Eastmans' reviewers worked through a deer with it, and the line also offers longer 3-inch blades in skinning, gut hook and bone-saw formats. Those heads are part of the pitch for the platform, though they run on a different blade code (300 and 301) than the 2.5-inch model.

**Steel.** The 420J2 is a soft, corrosion-resistant stainless that sharpens easily but doesn't hold an edge as long as premium steels. One reviewer made the point plainly. With replaceable blades, that matters less than it would on a fixed knife, and users who chose the APX did so deliberately, putting consistent sharpness ahead of steel performance. It's a trade that makes sense so long as spares are in the handle.

**Weight and the skeleton option.** At 79 g with everything installed, the APX is light for a hunting knife and far lighter than a conventional fixed blade with a leather or Kydex sheath. Stripping the scales and storage plates takes it to about 57 g with sheath, a figure that gets attention from weight-conscious hunters. Buyers who carry it for backpack hunting single out the weight and the in-handle blade storage as the main draws. Thru-hikers should note the context, though: this is still several times the weight of a small pocket knife or a scalpel-style blade, and it is a hunting tool first.

**Grip and handling.** The silicone scales are designed to stay secure with wet or gloved hands, and reviewers describe a solid, positive feel for a knife this small. The honeycomb aluminum cutouts in the frame are there to shave weight without leaving it feeling flimsy. Testers who tried small-hand tasks found the short blade controllable. Anyone expecting to baton wood, pry, or process large game with leverage on the 2.5-inch blade should look elsewhere. Reviewers consistently flag that this isn't the tool for it.

**Value.** At $59.99 MSRP (retail listings have run a few dollars higher), the price includes five blades. Replacement packs are cheap relative to sharpening gear, so the long-term cost is modest.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Four spare blades stored in the handle behind magnetic plates
- Push-button blade swap that works with cold or gloved hands
- 79 g as supplied, about 57 g with sheath once stripped to a skeleton
- Replaceable blades remove the need to carry sharpening gear
- Grippy silicone scales stay secure when wet
- Five blades included at $59.99 MSRP

**Cons**

- 420J2 steel dulls faster than premium alloys
- The 2.5-inch blade is limited for large-game work or leverage tasks
- Not a folder, so it needs the sheath for carry and has no locking-blade appeal
- Much heavier than minimalist cutting tools for non-hunting trips
- The 2.5-inch blades use a different blade code from the 3-inch APX heads

## Who Should Buy This

The APX suits backpack hunters and small-game hunters who want a light, razor-sharp knife and don't want to carry a sharpener. It also works for fishermen and trail-camp cooks who value quick blade swaps. Thru-hikers who want the lightest possible cutting tool for cheese and cord will do better with a small folder or blade-only setup. Anyone who needs a robust knife for batoning, prying or heavy game processing should buy a full-size fixed blade.

## Verdict

The Razor APX does one thing well: it keeps a sharp edge within reach in a very light package, and the in-handle blade storage is a smart design. It earns a recommendation for hunters counting grams, but it's a specialist tool, and steel quality and blade length hold it back from broader use.

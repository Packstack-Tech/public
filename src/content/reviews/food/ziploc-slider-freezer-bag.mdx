## Overview

The Ziploc Slider Freezer Bag in the gallon size is the grocery-aisle workhorse of backcountry food organization: a thick polyethylene bag with a plastic slider that zips shut without pinching the seal by hand. It suits section hikers, weekend backpackers and anyone building resupply boxes. The trade-off is closure reliability. The film is tougher than most disposable bags, but the slider is the part trail veterans have distrusted for years.

## Performance

**Weight and bulk.** At 18 g, a gallon slider bag costs less than a fun-size candy bar in pack weight. Retail listings put a 24-count box at about 11.4 oz including the carton, so the bags themselves likely come in lighter than the catalog figure. Either way, the weight is trivial. The pleated bottom is the one feature that adds bulk: it lets the bag open into a flat base and stand upright, which helps when pouring in a dinner or rehydrating food but doesn't compress as flat as a plain bag.

**Durability of the film.** This is where the freezer-grade bag earns its place. Ziploc says its Power Shield film beat competitors in dart-drop and puncture-propagation tests (ASTM D1709 and D2582). Backpackers reach the same conclusion by experience. A long-running Backpacking Light thread has contributors favoring name-brand Ziploc freezer bags after off-brand bags failed on them repeatedly. A touring cyclist on Bike Forums, meanwhile, noted that some competing freezer bags are thicker still, and judged that sliders overall are less sturdy and leak-resistant than double-zipper freezer bags.

**The slider.** Hikers' complaints cluster here. Threads on WhiteBlaze, a long-standing Appalachian Trail forum, are blunt: contributors say sliders tend to break after a few uses, catch and tear, and don't seal as tightly as a double-row zipper, which matters if the goal is keeping food dry. A Backpacking Light poster who otherwise liked Ziploc noted the slide can snag on gear when pulling the bag from a pack. Recent buyers on Ziploc's own product page describe the same family of problems in newer boxes: bags that wouldn't close at all, or whose zipper was separated across part of its width straight out of the carton. A slider that comes off its track is effectively unfixable on trail.

**Odor, moisture and reuse.** For a day's snacks or a dinner repackaged from a boil-in-bag meal, a well-seated slider keeps crumbs in and light rain out. It will not stop a determined bear or a submerged pack, and the bags aren't a substitute for an odor-barrier sack in bear country. They are reusable after washing, and Ziploc advises against reusing them after raw meat, fish or eggs, a non-issue for most trail menus. As a dry-bag substitute, polyethylene bags are noisy at 6 a.m., and one Backpacking Light user who tried them in place of stuff sacks reported leaks and a tendency for contents to expand back to full size.

**Value.** A 10-count box of gallon sliders lists at about 90 cents a bag at one major retailer, and larger boxes bring the figure down. Replacing one mid-trip is easy: they're stocked at virtually every grocery store and gas-station-adjacent resupply.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- About 18 g for a gallon of capacity
- Puncture-resistant freezer-grade film that outlasts thin sandwich-style bags
- Slider is easy to operate with cold or gloved hands
- Expandable bottom stands upright for filling and rehydrating
- Available in nearly every town on a thru-hike
- Reusable and microwave-safe

**Cons**

- Slider closures are the most commonly cited failure point among hikers
- Not as reliably waterproof as a double-zipper freezer bag
- Slider can snag when pulling the bag from a pack
- Pleated base doesn't pack as flat as a plain bag
- Noisy plastic; not a replacement for a proper dry bag

## Who Should Buy This

Weekend and section hikers who want a cheap way to sort food by day, corral cooking gear or protect a resupply box will get their money's worth. The slider is a genuine convenience for people who struggle with pressing a zipper shut in cold weather. Thru-hikers who lean on freezer-bag cooking, or who need a seal they can trust for weeks, should look at Ziploc's double-zipper freezer bags instead, which the hiking forums favor for their durability. Anyone protecting electronics or a quilt from rain needs a roll-top dry bag.

## Verdict

The Ziploc Slider Freezer Bag is a light, tough, nearly free piece of kit that does food organization well for short trips. Its slider keeps it from being the bag long-distance hikers trust, so buy it for convenience, carry a few double-zipper bags for anything that must stay sealed, and replace any bag whose slider starts to wander.

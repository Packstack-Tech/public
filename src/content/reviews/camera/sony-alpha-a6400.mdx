## Overview

The Sony Alpha a6400 is a 24.2MP APS-C mirrorless body with an E-mount, an electronic viewfinder and an autofocus system that still embarrasses some newer cameras. At 403 g, it sits well below any full-frame option and well above a phone, which makes it a natural pick for backpackers who want interchangeable lenses without a DSLR's heft. The trade-off is power: the small NP-FW50 battery turns a multi-day trip into a charging-logistics exercise.

## Performance

**Autofocus.** This is the reason the a6400 has stayed relevant since its 2019 launch. Reviewers at RTINGS call its subject tracking excellent, and DPReview's testers found that once the system is configured, it can be left alone for nearly any kind of shooting, from people to landscapes to sport. For backpackers, that translates into sharp frames of a marmot that won't hold still, or a hiking partner crossing a talus field, with no fuss.

**Image quality.** The sensor is the same 24.2MP unit Sony used in the a6300, and Fstoppers' testers, coming from a Micro Four Thirds video camera, measured roughly 2.5 stops better ISO performance. A DPReview forum thread weighing the a6400 against the full-frame a7 II for a walking trip drew a consistent conclusion: the image-quality gap is small, and the a6400's autofocus, size and weight advantages are not.

**Battery.** The weak point. Sony rates the camera at 410 shots per charge, and Expert Photography's reviewer drained a full battery in about 400 frames. DPReview notes that CIPA numbers tend to understate real use, with twice the rated figure not unusual for a photographer who shoots sparingly and keeps the camera asleep. Video is less forgiving: Videomaker clocked 80–90 minutes of recording per battery, a big step up from the a6300 and a6500 but short of what the site considers good for the industry. One forum owner who walks more than 15 km a day describes carrying four batteries, a charger and a power bank, then admits that routine was the reason a friend chose a camera with a bigger battery. Spares are the answer, and each one adds weight.

**Video.** Footage is detailed. DPReview notes that 4K is downsampled from higher-resolution capture, with no crop at 24p or 25p and a 1.2x crop at 30p. The 30-minute record limit is gone, and the camera runs cooler than the a6300 did, though DPReview stops short of promising it will never overheat. Rolling shutter is the recurring complaint: Expert Photography puts the sensor readout at roughly 30 milliseconds from top to bottom, enough to bend vertical lines in a fast pan. Add the lack of in-body stabilization, and handheld trail footage depends on a stabilized lens or a steady stride. There is no headphone jack, and color depth is limited to 8-bit.

**Handling and build.** RTINGS describes the body as sturdy and portable, with some weather sealing, though not rugged or waterproof. Ergonomics and menus draw criticism. The menu system is dense, and a hotshoe-mounted accessory can block the tilting screen when it is flipped up. The single card slot is UHS-I, so write speeds are modest, although the buffer is reasonably deep. The 180° tilt screen and 2.36m-dot EVF are welcome at this size, and the EVF is a notch above the one in the cheaper a6100.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Autofocus with reliable real-time subject tracking
- 403 g body in a pocketable-for-a-jacket footprint
- Sharp 24.2MP APS-C sensor with strong high-ISO results
- Detailed 4K with no recording time limit
- Some weather sealing
- Wide E-mount lens selection, including compact primes

**Cons**

- Small NP-FW50 battery; about 410 shots per charge
- Pronounced rolling shutter in video and with the electronic shutter
- No in-body stabilization
- Dense menus; screen can be blocked by hotshoe accessories
- Single UHS-I card slot, no headphone jack, 8-bit video only

## Who Should Buy This

Hikers and thru-hikers who care about photography enough to carry a dedicated camera, and who are willing to budget for spare batteries, will find the a6400 a sensible middle ground. It rewards those who want fast, dependable autofocus for wildlife, trail portraits and low-light camp scenes. Anyone whose priority is stabilized handheld video should look at a body with in-body stabilization, such as the a6600, or a dedicated action camera. Those who want to cut grams below all else will be happier with a high-end compact or a phone.

## Verdict

The a6400 delivers an autofocus system and image quality that justify its 403 g and the cost of a few spare batteries. Its rolling shutter and unstabilized body keep it from being a video tool for the trail, but as a stills camera for multi-day trips it remains one of the strongest compact options in the E-mount line.

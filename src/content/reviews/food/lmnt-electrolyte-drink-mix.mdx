## Overview

LMNT is a zero-sugar electrolyte powder sold in single-serve stick packets, built around one blunt idea: most hydration mixes don't contain enough salt. Each stick carries 1,000 mg of sodium, roughly double what mainstream competitors offer, in about 6 g of powder. For hikers who sweat heavily in heat, that density is the appeal. For everyone else, the headline trade-off is a flavor that tastes like what it is.

## Performance

**Electrolyte content.** The formula is three minerals and little else. Sodium chloride leads by a wide margin, which tracks with what sweat actually carries out of the body: sodium and chloride, mostly. A registered dietitian reviewing the mix for BarBend noted that the sodium load is about double that of similar products and said the higher dose may be a plus for people with larger fluid losses. Yahoo's roundup of dietitian-and-tester picks named it the best mix for heavy sweaters, the sort of person who sweats buckets during workouts or hot-weather activities. One dietitian ranking placed it at the top for high-intensity exercise or high heat.

Liquid I.V. is the usual comparison. LMNT carries about twice the sodium of that brand's original and sugar-free formulas, which land around 500 to 530 mg, and it adds 60 mg of magnesium that Liquid I.V. lacks. Liquid I.V. has more potassium, at roughly 370 to 390 mg against LMNT's 200 mg.

**Taste.** Salty, and reviewers say so consistently. Garage Gym Reviews scored the taste 4 out of 5 and called it an acquired flavor. The stevia-sweetened range softens the edge: one dietitian expected a salt bomb and found the tart-sweet balance better than she'd predicted. Not everyone agrees. Some owners object to a stevia aftertaste that they compare to artificial sweeteners, and a family review on Trustpilot reported that nobody at home could stand it. Hiking blogger Alexys Abroad makes the most trail-relevant point: the same drink goes down more easily after miles of sweating than it does at the kitchen counter.

**Mixability.** Testers split here. Garage Gym Reviews gave solubility a perfect 5 out of 5, while another dietitian said it takes a little longer to dissolve but then stays in solution. A scattering of buyers report clumping in cold water. In a trail bottle, a hard shake and a few minutes solve that.

**Weight and packaging.** The box of 18 weighs 110 g. A single 6 g stick delivers a full gram of sodium, which makes it far more weight-efficient than carrying salt tabs plus a flavoring system. The sticks tear open without a scoop and take up almost no room in a hip-belt pocket or food bag. They are mix-in-bottle only, so there's no dry-mouth shortcut as with tablets.

**What it doesn't do.** With zero sugar and about 5 calories, LMNT isn't fuel. Thru-hikers who want carbohydrates in their bottle will need to add them separately. At around $1.50 a stick on a 30-count box listed at $45, it also costs more than most rivals; reviewers regularly list price as a drawback. Sodium-sensitive people, anyone with hypertension, and those eating a high-sodium diet should talk to a doctor first. Garage Gym Reviews also flags electrolyte imbalance as a risk of the high dose.

**Hot drinks.** Alexys Abroad lists hot preparation as a point in LMNT's favor over Liquid I.V., which matters on cold-morning starts when a warm salty cup beats a chilled one.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 1,000 mg sodium in 6 g of powder, the highest dose among mainstream stick packs
- Includes magnesium, which Liquid I.V. lacks
- Zero sugar and a short, simple ingredient list
- Light and compact: 110 g for 18 servings, no scoop required
- Works in hot or cold drinks

**Cons**

- Distinctly salty; many first-timers find it too much
- Stevia aftertaste bothers some drinkers
- Provides no carbohydrates or meaningful calories
- Pricier per serving than most competitors
- Overkill for easy day hikes, and unsuitable for sodium-sensitive users without medical advice

## Who Should Buy This

LMNT suits hikers who sweat heavily, cover big mileage in summer heat, or already eat low-carb and need sodium without sugar. Desert section hikers and 20-mile-a-day thru-hikers are the clearest fit. Anyone doing short, cool hikes, or anyone who dislikes salty drinks, should look at a lower-sodium mix such as Liquid I.V. or simply half a stick per bottle. People managing blood pressure should skip it unless a doctor says otherwise.

## Verdict

LMNT is the most sodium-dense stick pack in its class, and its weight and simplicity make it an easy fit for a food bag. It earns a recommendation for hot-weather, high-sweat use, but the taste and price keep it from being a universal pick. Buy a small variety pack before committing to a box of 18.

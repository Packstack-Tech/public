## Overview

Deuter's Raincover is a plain, rectangular shell of 70D polyamide that stretches over a loaded pack and cinches shut with a drawcord. It comes in two sizes: the II for 30–50L packs and the III for 45–90L expedition-scale loads. It isn't built for ultralight frameless rigs, and at 90–130 g it costs more weight than silnylon alternatives. In return it offers sealed seams and a coating that suit trekkers who use their packs hard.

## Performance

The fabric is a finely woven 70-denier polyamide. Deuter says it packs small and sheds rain well thanks to the PU coating. The construction matters more than the spec line suggests. Taped and welded seams address the usual failure of cheap covers, which is water working its way through the stitch lines in sustained rain. Owners who have used the III in heavy rain report that gear stayed dry. One buyer called it noticeably thicker than the cover that shipped with their pack. Another said it ran for hours of rain on the first day of a trip with everything inside staying dry.

Both sizes carry reflective detailing, a small margin of safety on road walks and in dim light. Deuter makes the fabric without PFAS, and the II is also listed as bluesign, ClimatePartner and Green Button certified.

Packability is a quiet strength. Owners describe the III as small and compact when not in use, and at 90 g the II is easy to forget in a side pocket. Customers on European retail sites rate the II mostly for its low weight and price for the performance, and it averages about 4.5 stars across eight reviews.

Fit is where the complaints cluster, and nearly all of them concern the III. It is cut for packs up to 90L, so it runs large on anything near the bottom of its range. A buyer with a 50+10 trekking pack reported the cover hanging a foot or more below the pack, with the drawcord unable to take up the slack. A second returned it as far too big. Pack size is the deciding variable. A buyer running a 45L pack in a III, meanwhile, said it wrapped around the load nicely. Those with truly large loads like the extra room: one owner praised it as big enough to cover gear lashed to the outside and still cinch snug enough not to flap in wind.

As with any cover that cinches at the hem, access suffers. One owner noted that with the cover pulled tight, side-pocket water bottles were out of reach, and recommended a hydration reservoir for long days. A cover also does nothing for water running down the back panel against the shoulder straps, so it pairs best with a pack liner on multi-day trips.

## Choosing a Version

The two sizes share fabric, coating, seams and reflective trim. Only the cut changes.

**II (90 g, 69 x 30 x 27 cm):** Rated for 30–50L packs. This is the version for weekend and thru-hiking packs in the 40–50L range, including a 50+10 trekking pack, where owners say it fits better than the III even with the extension collar extended. It is also 40 g lighter and $5 cheaper.

**III (130 g, 97 x 37 x 30 cm):** Rated for 45–90L packs. It suits expedition loads, winter kits and packs with bulky lashed-on gear, where the extra fabric is useful rather than flapping. Anyone with a pack under about 55L should expect slack at the bottom.

The overlap at 45–50L is real. In that zone, choose the II unless the pack regularly carries gear strapped outside.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Taped and welded seams plus PU coating give dependable protection in sustained rain
- Reflective detailing for low-light and roadside visibility
- PFAS-free construction, with bluesign certification listed for the II
- Packs small enough to live in a side pocket
- Two sizes cover 30–90L, with the III handling oversized loads

**Cons**

- 90–130 g is heavier than silnylon or Dyneema covers
- The III hangs loosely on packs at the small end of its range
- Cinching the hem can block access to side-pocket bottles
- Offers no protection against moisture on the back panel and straps

## Who Should Buy This

This is for trekkers, mountaineers and multi-day hikers carrying a full-size pack who want a cover that will take brush, gravel and repeated stuffing. Pick the II for 30–50L packs and the III for 55L and up. Those carrying packs under 40L who count every ounce are better served by a lighter cover or a pack liner. Anyone with a 45–50L pack should buy the II.

## Verdict

The Raincover is a durable, properly sealed cover that does its one job well, and it's priced fairly at $30–35. Its weight rules out ultralight setups, and the III's wide size range leaves smaller packs with loose fabric. Buy the size matched to the pack and it earns its pocket space.

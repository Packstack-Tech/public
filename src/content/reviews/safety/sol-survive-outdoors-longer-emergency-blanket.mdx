## Overview

The SOL Emergency Blanket is a single-person emergency shelter made from vacuum-metalized polyethylene — a meaningful step up from the tissue-thin mylar sheets that disintegrate the moment you actually need them. At 70.9 g (2.5 oz) and folded down to roughly 5" × 3" × 4", it's genuinely pocketable. It's designed for day hikers, trail runners, and backpackers who want a credible safety net for unexpected overnights, sudden weather turns, or assisting an injured partner while waiting for rescue.

## Performance

### Material and Durability

This is where the SOL earns its price premium over a $2 mylar sheet. <cite index="1-8,1-9">The metalized polyethylene material is up to 50% thicker than contemporary mylar emergency blankets, and that thicker construction makes it notably quieter while also giving it stretch-before-tear behavior.</cite> <cite index="1-10">Even if a hole forms, it won't shred like traditional space blankets, and it can be repaired with duct tape.</cite> Real-world users have backed this up: <cite index="15-46,15-47,15-48,15-49">one reviewer used the XL version for 7 nights of cowboy camping, with a lot of contact between the blanket and the scratchy ground where it overflowed the sides of a sleeping pad — and found there were still no rips or flaking afterward.</cite> That's a far cry from a standard mylar sheet, which would have been confetti by night two.

### Heat Retention

<cite index="2-6">
  The windproof, waterproof material reflects 90% of body heat and is highly
  resistant to punctures and tears.
</cite>
In practice, that 90% figure holds up reasonably well in mild conditions.
<cite index="14-42,14-43,14-44">
  Standalone use in 50–55°F at Zion kept one user comfortable while wearing
  hiking clothes.
</cite>
Don't expect more than that from any flat emergency blanket, though.
<cite index="14-33,14-34,14-35">
  In the Pacific Northwest at 44°F, a user reported being miserably cold every
  time and needing every piece of clothing available.
</cite>
This is not a substitute for a sleeping bag. It's a bridge between "this is bad"
and "this is fatal" — and that's exactly the role it should play.

### Condensation

Here's the unavoidable trade-off: <cite index="1-27,1-28">the blanket is windproof and waterproof and therefore not breathable, so from time to time it's necessary to open up the front to ventilate — otherwise condensation builds up on the inside, increasing hypothermia risk in an emergency.</cite> <cite index="14-36,14-37">In high-humidity conditions, the inside can become thickly covered in condensation.</cite> This is a physics problem, not a design flaw — every non-breathable emergency blanket behaves this way. The workaround is to leave a small opening at the top and, if possible, keep a base layer between yourself and the blanket rather than using it directly against skin.

### Visibility and Rescue Features

<cite index="1-11,1-12">
  The orange exterior not only makes you more visible to rescuers but also
  reminds you which side is reflective — traditional all-silver space blankets
  can be easily turned inside out in stressful situations, essentially making
  them useless.
</cite>
<cite index="20-7">
  Survival and first-aid instructions are printed directly on the blanket
</cite>
, covering topics like shelter building and water collection — useful in theory,
though
<cite index="14-13,14-14">
  one reviewer noted the printed instructions can come off when the blanket gets
  wet.
</cite>
Worth a look before you head out, just to familiarize yourself.

The orange color has proven its worth in real emergencies. <cite index="8-25,8-26,8-27,8-28">One user whose husband twisted his knee on an icy trail wrapped him in the blanket for 15 minutes while she went for help, noting that the bright orange made him easy to spot.</cite>

### Multi-Use Versatility

<cite index="15-31,15-32">
  The material is lightweight, stretches instead of tearing, and can be used as
  a poncho, survival blanket, or shelter — and the size is large enough to
  provide real coverage from rain, wind, and snow.
</cite>
<cite index="3-4">
  In hot weather, the silver reflective side can be used to build a shade
  structure and reflect the sun's heat away.
</cite>
This kind of multi-role utility matters when you're trying to justify every
ounce in a minimalist kit.

One important caveat that applies to all emergency blanket materials: <cite index="1-16,1-17">while it's possible to make it through a night in reasonable conditions, using it near a fire is recommended for warmth boost — just don't get it too close to the flames, as it will ignite quickly.</cite>

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Genuinely quiet — won't crinkle loudly in wind the way standard mylar does
- Tear-resistant; stretches before it shreds, and tears are duct-tape-repairable
- Repackable and reusable
- Blaze orange exterior for high-visibility rescue signaling
- Two-sided design (orange / reflective silver) removes the guesswork of which side faces in
- Compact enough to live permanently in a hip belt pocket or first-aid kit
- Printed first-aid and survival instructions on the blanket itself

**Cons**

- Non-breathable — condensation buildup is significant during prolonged use
- Slippery material makes it difficult to keep wrapped tightly around yourself without securing it
- Printed instructions can wash off when wet
- Not a standalone sleep system below ~50°F without supplemental insulation
- No grommets or tie-out points — improvising a tarp shelter takes some creativity

## Who Should Buy This

<cite index="12-5,12-6">
  The SOL Emergency Blanket is for minimalist hikers, trail runners, cyclists,
  and anyone who wants the lightest possible emergency blanket that still works
  as intended — great for day hikes, trail kits, and compact get-home bags where
  space and weight are priorities.
</cite>
If you're already carrying a full sleep system, this slots in as a dedicated
emergency layer, a rescue aid for an injured partner, or a windbreak of last
resort. If you want something for multi-night use or alpine conditions, step up
to the SOL Heavy Duty Emergency Blanket (7.9 oz, with grommets for rigging) or
the SOL Escape Bivvy, which adds breathability.

## Verdict

The SOL Emergency Blanket does one thing well: it's a compact, durable, no-excuses safety item that belongs in every pack, every car kit, and every day hike pack top. At 2.5 oz and roughly deck-of-cards packed size, the weight and space cost of not carrying it is harder to justify than carrying it. It's not a sleep system, and you'll still get cold if you try to use it as one — but in a genuine emergency, the gap between this and cheap mylar is the gap between something that holds together and something that doesn't. I'd rate it **8/10**.

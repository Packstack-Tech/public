## Overview

For years, the NeoAir XLite was the sleeping pad that appeared in nearly every Triple Crown shakedown photo: a 2.5-inch air mattress that rolls up to roughly the size of a Nalgene and weighs less than most trail runners. This is the past-season model, since replaced by the NeoAir XLite NXT, but it remains a proven three-season pad for hikers who want warmth and packability above all. The trade-off is a narrow mummy cut and a crinkly surface that announces every shift in the night.

## Performance

**Warmth.** The XLite's insulation comes from two ideas working together. Honeycombed triangular baffles break up convective heat loss inside the pad, and a metallic reflective film bounces radiant body heat back toward the sleeper. The pad is rated R 4.2 under the current ASTM standard. Earlier versions of the same pad were listed at 3.2, and The Trek noted that the jump came from new testing rules, not from any redesign. In practice, that figure covers three-season use, from summer ridgelines to frosty shoulder-season nights, when paired with a sensible ground setup. Cold sleepers heading into true winter conditions will want a second pad underneath.

**Weight and packed size.** Therm-a-Rest lists the Regular at 12.5 oz (354 g), though SectionHiker's test sample came in at 13 oz. The pump sack adds 2.0 oz and the stuff sack another 0.4 oz. With the full kit, including the repair kit, The Trek put the total closer to 15 oz. Hikers who leave the pump sack at home and rely on lung power save a couple of ounces. Packed, the pad measures 9 x 4 in, which fits sideways in the bottom of a frameless pack or vertically in a side pocket.

**Comfort.** At 2.5 inches, the horizontal baffles give a stable platform with enough loft to ignore small roots and pebbles. Backpackers.com's tester, a self-described side sleeper, found it supportive enough to avoid waking with sore hips. The 20-inch width is the trade-off. Hikers who sprawl, or who sleep with elbows out, will find the edges arrive quickly. Side sleepers who bottom out should know the NXT successor added half an inch of loft specifically to address that complaint.

**Noise.** This is the XLite's best-known flaw. The reflective film crinkles, and the pad has a reputation for rustling with every turn, which is a real consideration in a shelter full of light sleepers. Therm-a-Rest's redesign for the NXT claims an 83 percent reduction in crinkle noise, which tells its own story about what the original sounded like on a quiet night.

**Inflation and durability.** The pad was updated in 2020 with the WingLock valve, which allows faster deflation and one-way inflation, plus micro-adjustments for firmness without getting off the pad. SectionHiker noted that this was the only change made to the pad at that time. Pre-2020 pads use the older stick valve. The 30D nylon has built a durable reputation on long trails, and the included repair kit handles field punctures. As with any air pad, a thin groundsheet and a quick check for sharp debris before setting up are the main insurance policy.

## Choosing a Version

The three sizes share the same construction and warmth rating. What changes is the footprint.

- **Women's (340 g):** The lightest of the three and shorter than the Regular at 66 in, which suits hikers up to about average height and saves a few grams. It also adds an extra layer of reflective material aimed at colder sleepers.
- **Regular (354 g):** The default at 20 x 72 in. It is a 14 g step up from the Women's, and it suits most men and anyone taller than the Women's length allows.
- **Large (470.1 g):** Wider and longer than the Regular. It costs about 116 g more than the Regular, and it's the pick for tall hikers, restless sleepers, and anyone who finds a 20-inch mummy claustrophobic.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Roughly 12 oz in the Regular, with packed volume near that of a 1-liter bottle
- R 4.2 covers three-season conditions
- Proven durability across long-distance trail use
- WingLock valve allows quick deflation and fine-tuned firmness
- Pump sack, stuff sack, and repair kit included

**Cons**

- Crinkly and noisy, noticeably more so than the NXT
- 20-inch width is tight for sprawlers
- 2.5 inches of loft can feel firm or bottom out for heavier side sleepers
- Superseded by the NXT, which is thicker, warmer (R 4.5), and quieter at a similar weight
- Full kit with pump sack and repair kit weighs closer to 15 oz

## Who Should Buy This

Thru-hikers and weekend ultralighters who want a proven, packable pad for spring-through-fall trips, and who spot a clearance or secondhand deal on the older model, will be well served. It suits back sleepers and anyone who sleeps alone in a tent. Light sleepers sharing quarters, strong side sleepers, and anyone camping regularly below freezing should look at the XLite NXT instead.

## Verdict

The original XLite earned its reputation by delivering real warmth and respectable cushion at a weight few pads matched. It is now an older design, and the NXT fixes its main shortcomings of noise and thickness for little extra weight. Buy the original only at a meaningful discount. Otherwise, get the NXT.

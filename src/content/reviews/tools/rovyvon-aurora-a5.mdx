## Overview

The RovyVon Aurora A5 is a 17 g, USB-C rechargeable keychain flashlight with a glow-in-the-dark polycarbonate body, a magnetic tail and a reversible pocket clip. It is aimed at backpackers who want a camp-chores light, or a backup to the headlamp, that weighs about as much as a few coins. The trade-off is the small 330 mAh cell: the A5 is bright for its size but won't run hard for long.

## Performance

**Brightness and beam.** The front emitter is a clean flood with enough throw to pick out a trail junction. Medium, at 200 lm, is the setting that does the work. One reviewer at Bikepacking.com called medium and high "quite bright" for something not much bigger than a Mini-Bic lighter. Bench figures are more modest. ZeroAir measured a Nichia-equipped A5 at 258 lm after 30 seconds, about 61 percent of the 420 lm claim. It calculated throw at about 72.6 m, slightly beyond the 70 m claim. Treat the headline lumen number as a ceiling, not a cruising speed.

**Runtime.** This is the A5's main limitation. RovyVon's claimed figures are about 75 hours on Moonlight, 8 hours on Low, roughly 2 hours on Medium and about 90 minutes on High. In practice, tent chores sit on Moonlight or Low, and High is for the occasional trip to the bushes or a look at a noise in the trees. Reviewers at Hiro Equipment list the short high-mode runtime as the main drawback. A full recharge takes about an hour from a USB-C power bank, so topping off in camp is no hardship.

**Emitter choice.** Owners split along predictable lines. The SST-20 is cooler and brighter. The Nichia 219C is warmer and has 90 CRI. One owner on a backpacking forum bought the Nichia and notes that it renders colors more truly than an earlier Cree-based light, which looked washed out by comparison. For reading, cooking and reading a map, the Nichia is the better choice. If maximum output matters more, take the SST-20.

**Side light and GITD body.** The side emitters are what separate the A5 from a plain penlight. White comes in two levels (10 and 50 lm per Hiro Equipment's specs). The other side emitter is UV or red, depending on the version. Clipped to a shirt pocket or hat brim, the side light works as a small lantern. Owners say the reversible clip also lets the A5 work as a hat-brim headlamp. Red preserves night vision in a shared shelter. UV is mostly useful for recharging the glowing body, though it also doubles as a scorpion finder. The green shell glows long enough to locate the light in a dark tent, which is a bigger deal at 2 a.m. than it sounds.

**Interface.** There is one button, and it controls everything. A rapid double press turns on the main beam, and single clicks step through the four levels. Holding the button turns it off. A triple press switches to the side light, where clicks cycle through white levels, red and blinking red. Five rapid clicks engage lockout, so it can ride in a pack lid without being drained. Hiro Equipment's testers found the nine-mode layout takes a little practice. Once learned, it is easy to operate with cold fingers.

**Build.** The body is plastic except for the bezel, which appears to be stainless steel. That keeps weight down, though it will not take abuse as well as RovyVon's all-aluminum A3 and A8 siblings, which keychain owners describe as nearly indestructible. The pocket clip ships separate rather than pre-attached, and it attaches magnetically. Early reviewers of the A5 line also noted visible flicker from low-level PWM dimming when the light is moved. Users who are sensitive to flicker should keep that in mind on Moonlight and Low.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 17 g for a 650 lm-class light with a usable side lamp
- Glowing body makes it easy to find in a dark tent or pack
- USB-C charging, about an hour to full
- Magnetic tail and reversible clip allow hands-free use on a hat brim or tent pole
- Lockout mode prevents pack-lid drain
- Nichia option offers 90 CRI color rendering

**Cons**

- 330 mAh cell gives roughly 90 minutes on High and 2 hours on Medium
- Measured output trails the claimed numbers
- Plastic body is less rugged than RovyVon's metal Aurora models
- Low-level PWM flicker has been noted on earlier units
- Nine modes on one button take some learning

## Who Should Buy This

The A5 suits weight-focused hikers who need a dependable second light for camp and the tent, and for anyone who wants a light that never leaves the keyring. It also fits section hikers who can recharge from a power bank every few days. Thru-hikers who navigate in the dark for hours or hike night sections should carry a proper headlamp. The A5 can back one up, but it won't replace one. Anyone who wants a light that survives being dropped on rock repeatedly should consider the aluminum A3 or A8 models.

## Verdict

The Aurora A5 does a lot in 17 g. The side light, the glowing shell and the hat-brim clip give it the range of a small lantern, a night light and a flashlight in a single package. Runtime on High is brief, but for camp chores and tent duty it is one of the best small lights a hiker can clip to a pack strap.

## Overview

Grivel calls the G12 the "king of crampons," and a few decades of guide-service use in the Alps and the Cascades have made the boast hard to argue with. It is a 12-point steel crampon built for glacier travel, classic mountaineering and moderate technical terrain, offered with three binding systems to match nearly any boot with a welt. The trade-off is weight: at roughly a kilogram a pair, it is a crampon for people who value durability over grams.

## Performance

The G12's reputation rests on range. Reviewers who have used it on bare glacial ice, wet new snow over firm névé and long mixed routes tend to land on the same conclusion: it handles all of it without drama. One Trailspace reviewer called it the crampon 90 percent of climbers need, noting vertical ice, steep snow and even dry tooling in his résumé with it. Climbers on Mountain Project have gone further, saying horizontal front points hold well in chandeliered, awkward waterfall ice, and that the G12 will not be the limiting factor on most of it. Where the G12 gives ground is dense, bullet-hard ice, where a vertical mono-point penetrates with less effort.

Long, forward-pointing secondary points are a large part of the appeal. They add stability on steep ground and let the crampon climb more technical terrain than a pure walking design. The four rear points keep a descending climber from skating on snow. Walking is a different matter: the same long secondary points that help on steep ice make the G12 less pleasant on moderate terrain than a flatter, lighter glacier crampon. Reviewers also note it is secure on rock, which matters on the scrambly approaches that mountaineering routes tend to include.

Snow management is a standout. An Alpinist tester who used the G12 through a season in the Cascades and Alps reported that the antibott plates never balled up, crediting an outward-facing dimple pattern in the plastic that sheds snow, along with a plastic-coated adjustment bar that resists sticking. The same tester's one real complaint was that the crampon does not collapse fully for packing, because the accordion-style plastic stops the two halves sliding together. Trimming a little plastic fixes it.

Build quality is the other consistent theme. The frame is chromoly steel (nickel-chrome-molybdenum, in Grivel's description), a choice that sharpens more easily than stainless. Owners describe crampons that barely show wear after hard use. The steel does rust if stored wet, as one Bergfreunde buyer pointed out, so a dry-out after wet days is part of ownership.

Fit is generally straightforward. Customers on European retail sites rate the G12 highly for secure fit and ease of adjustment, with 43 of 44 reviews on one site at five stars. The Great Outdoors tested a pair at 1,005 g and scored it 4/5, praising the secure fit and solid design but finding the adjustment fiddly and the weight a drawback. Boot compatibility is where the occasional complaint lands: one owner found a large boot did not seat properly between the heel bars even with the extender bar, and a Trailspace reviewer had a crampon pop off on a descent and blamed his own setup rather than the gear. Fitting at home, with the actual boots, is not optional. The design is also older and less curved than newer crampons, which some owners say lags behind the rocker of modern boots, though it still fits.

## Choosing a Version

The frame, points and anti-balling plates are identical across versions. Only the binding changes, and with it the boot compatibility.

- **Cramp-O-Matic EVO (1,012 g):** The automatic step-in version, with a heel lever and a toe bail. It needs a boot with both heel and toe welts, so it suits stiff B3 mountaineering boots and anyone who wants the fastest on and off.
- **New-Matic EVO (1,006 g):** The semi-automatic option, with a heel lever and a flexible toe strap. It works with B2 and B3 boots and with boots that have a heel welt only. This is the version most commonly reviewed, and the most flexible pick for a climber with one pair of boots.
- **New-Classic EVO (964 g):** Flexible bails at both ends, using Grivel's circular pull-tab strap closure. It is the lightest of the three, fits boots with no welts at all, and is the choice for flexible boots or mixed quivers. It is also slower to put on with cold hands.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Proven all-rounder across glacier, steep snow, alpine rock and moderate ice
- Anti-balling plates that owners report stay clear of snow
- Durable chromoly steel frame that resists visible wear
- Three binding options cover most boots from EU 36 to 47
- Long secondary points add stability on steep ground

**Cons**

- About 1 kg per pair, heavier than aluminum or lightweight steel alternatives
- Does not collapse fully without trimming the plastic
- Rusts if stored wet
- Strap and bar adjustment is fiddly for some users
- Older, flatter frame is less tuned to modern boot shapes

## Who Should Buy This

The G12 is for the climber who wants one crampon that covers glaciers, Alpine routes and the occasional pitch of steep ice, and who plans to use it for many seasons. It is a sound first pair for someone building a mountaineering kit. Ultralight alpinists and ski mountaineers counting every gram should look at Grivel's own G12 LT or an aluminum model instead, and technical ice specialists will prefer a mono-point or vertical-front design.

## Verdict

The G12 earns its reputation. It is not light or fashionable, but it bites reliably, sheds snow better than most, and keeps going for years. For a general mountaineering crampon, it remains one of the safest buys.

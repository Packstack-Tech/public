## Overview

The HydraPak Stash is a collapsible bottle with a thin TPU body sandwiched between a rigid top and base. Twist it and it drops to roughly a quarter of its filled height, which makes it a natural spare for bear canisters, summit pushes and carry-on bags. The trade-off is handling: the film walls that make it so packable also make it clumsy to drink from once the water level drops. It comes in 750 ml and 1 L sizes.

## Performance

### Packability and structure

Collapsed, the 750 ml Stash measures 2 by 3.5 inches, about two hockey pucks stacked. Reviewers have slipped it into bear canisters, jacket pockets and sling bags with room to spare, and Pack Hacker's testers once lost the 1L inside a bag because it disappeared so thoroughly. Stackable bases let several bottles snap together, and three collapsed 750s occupy less room than one full-size hard bottle.

The rigid base is the real differentiator from floppy soft flasks. An open Stash stands upright on its own, empty or full, which makes filling from a shallow seep or a muddy stream far less fiddly than propping a bag against a rock. Reviewers also note that the cap stays off the ground while filling.

### Durability and leaks

The seams are RF-welded, and owners report no seam leaks after hikes, kayak trips and general travel abuse. The cap seals cleanly when screwed down, and squeezing a full bottle does not push water past it. Because the 0.4 mm TPU can be wound down between rigid ends, the film is also shielded from scuffs and sharp objects while stowed. HydraPak backs the bottle with its Beyond Lifetime Guarantee.

### Ergonomics

This is the Stash's weak spot, and reviewers across several outlets agree on it. Expanding the bottle means unscrewing the cap, squeezing the midsection to pop four radial tabs and letting the base twist about 180 degrees outward. Several testers fumbled the motion on first use; after a few tries it takes a couple of seconds.

Drinking is trickier. A full bottle feels firm, but as it empties the walls sag, and an unthinking squeeze sends water up the nose or down the shirt. OffGrid's testers found the soft sides hard to grasp without compressing them and ended up holding the bottle by the neck when uncapped. Gripping the rigid top is the standard workaround.

The shape sits in an awkward middle ground for storage. It slides into a side pocket well enough, but it does not cinch down like a hard bottle, and it lacks the flat profile of a true soft flask for hip-belt pockets. Cleaning takes some care: the ridges in the base are difficult to scrub thoroughly, though the wide mouth helps.

### Filters

The 750 ml's 42 mm mouth matches the threading on HydraPak's Flux bottles and Seeker reservoirs and pairs directly with the Katadyn BeFree. The Sawyer Squeeze uses 28 mm threads, so thru-hikers who rely on it need a HydraPak adapter and cap, which means an extra bit of kit to buy and carry. The BeFree pairing is clean. The Sawyer setup works but is not elegant.

### Taste and temperature

New bottles carry a plastic or chemical smell. OffGrid's testers noted it improved with time, and Pack Hacker's reviewer found the plastic taste faded over three weeks of use. The same reviewer reported that the film held a soapy taste for about half a day after washing, so rinse thoroughly. The bottle tolerates freezing and hot fills up to 140°F, but it offers little insulation, and water tracks ambient temperature quickly.

## Choosing a Version

The two sizes share a design, materials and expansion mechanism. What changes is volume, weight and footprint.

**750 ml (68 g):** The lighter and more compact option. It collapses to 2 × 3.5 in, fits more easily into bear canisters and pockets, and suits hikers who carry it as a spare or for side trips. Its 42 mm mouth is the one that mates directly with BeFree-style filter threads.

**1 L (107 g):** Adds 250 ml for 39 g, with a collapsed footprint of 2.3 × 3.9 in. It includes a flexible carry handle and an external capacity gauge, and suits dry stretches where a spare liter matters more than a few grams. The wider, heavier body is slightly harder to hold as it empties. Ultralighters who want a primary bottle will find the 1L's extra capacity more useful than the 750's compactness; those who only need an emergency reservoir should choose the 750.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Collapses to about a quarter of its filled height
- Stands on its own when empty, easing fills from shallow sources
- RF-welded seams with no recurring leak complaints
- Wide mouth for filling and cleaning
- Tolerates freezing and hot fills up to 140°F
- Beyond Lifetime Guarantee

**Cons**

- Expanding the bottle has a learning curve
- Soft walls make drinking awkward as the bottle empties
- Sawyer Squeeze users need an adapter on the 750 ml
- Initial plastic taste, with reports of lingering soap flavor
- Base ridges are hard to clean thoroughly
- No insulation

## Who Should Buy This

The Stash suits backpackers who want a spare liter or three-quarters of a liter that vanishes when empty: a canister-friendly backup, a camp water-hauler, a summit-day extra. It also doubles as an excellent travel bottle. Thru-hikers using a BeFree or a bladder-and-bottle setup will find it a useful complement. Hikers who want a single primary bottle for all-day sipping, or who run a Sawyer Squeeze without adapters, should look at a hard-sided bottle or a purpose-built soft flask instead.

## Verdict

The Stash does the one thing it promises, shrinking to a pocketable puck while standing upright when open, and it is built well enough to survive a season of abuse. The awkward drinking ergonomics and filter-thread constraints keep it from being a primary bottle. Buy the 750 ml for tight packs and the 1L when capacity matters more than size, and treat either as a secondary bottle.

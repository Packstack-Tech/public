## Overview

The TruArc 3 is Brunton's entry-level baseplate compass, built in Riverton, Wyoming. It pairs a global needle with a declination adjustment that needs no screwdriver, two features that usually push a compass well past the $50 mark. The trade-off is a stripped-down body with a short edge, minimal markings and a build that owners say can be hit or miss.

## Performance

**Needle and accuracy.** The global needle is the reason to look at this compass. It is meant to swing freely when the baseplate isn't perfectly level and to hold up in magnetic zones far from the one it was bought in. One REI owner called out that the needle keeps turning when the base is slightly tilted, which suits a compass that lives in a pocket and gets read on the move. Reviewers also note that this is the cheapest global-needle compass in a plain chassis, and that comparable Suunto and Recta models with global needles cost more than $50. A Trailspace reviewer who tested it in a single zone found the 2-degree graduations did what Brunton claims, and called it quick to use.

**Declination.** Squeeze the capsule and spin it. That is the whole procedure, and buyers consistently describe it as easy once they find the trick, though at least one first-time user had to work out the motion first. For anyone who hikes in places with significant declination, setting it once beats doing mental arithmetic at every bearing. Backpacking Light forum posters make the same argument: a real adjustment is one less thing to remember.

**Ergonomics.** The body descends from Brunton's old OSS 10B and Classic designs, which in turn trace back to the Silva Voyager layout. One REI reviewer notes that it carries over the flaws of that older design. The edge is short, which makes it a poor straightedge for drawing bearings on a map. The map orienting lines are hard to see, according to one B&H buyer who found it fiddly to align with a map. Cardinal points aren't printed on the bezel's degree markings. A Backpacking Light poster also warned that the printed rule markings can rub off in a pack, a failure that Brunton's customer service has fixed with replacement parts in at least one reported case.

**Durability and quality control.** This is where reviews split. Satisfied owners report years of trouble-free use, and one Scout leader called it stout. Others describe a capsule that popped out of its shell on the first day, a dial assembly that came apart, or a needle that was slow to settle and sat well off north. One REI buyer's example pointed roughly south-southeast even after being re-magnetized. Brunton's one-year warranty draws criticism from buyers who note that other makers offer longer ones, though reviewers also praise Brunton's customer service and returns. REI's 36 reviews average 4.1 out of 5, which suggests most units work as advertised. Anyone who buys one should check it against a known-good compass before a trip.

**Weight.** At 31 g with the lanyard, the TruArc 3 isn't the lightest compass around. Backpacking Light members have noted that the capsule weighs about 0.8 oz (roughly 23 g) once it is pulled out of the baseplate, which is the kind of modification the ultralight crowd tends to make. For most hikers, 31 g is easy to justify given what the compass can do.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Global needle and tool-free declination at a budget price
- 31 g and pocket-sized at 6.3 x 8.9 cm
- 2-degree bezel graduations, enough for trail and basic map work
- Imperial and metric scales on the baseplate
- Made in the USA, with a lanyard included

**Cons**

- Short baseplate edge makes it a weak ruler for map work
- Map orienting lines are hard to see; no cardinal points on the bezel
- Some owners report capsule or dial failures, and a few report needles that don't settle properly
- Printed markings can wear off in a pack
- No sighting mirror, so bearings are less precise than on a mirrored compass
- One-year warranty

## Who Should Buy This

The TruArc 3 suits hikers who want a backup compass or a first compass that can still handle declination properly: thru-hikers who carry a map and compass alongside a phone, scouts, and anyone traveling across hemispheres. It also works as a spare for the emergency kit or a pack lid pocket.

Hikers who regularly shoot bearings across open country, or who do serious map work, should look at a mirrored model such as Brunton's own TruArc 15 or 20, or a longer-baseplate compass from Suunto or Silva. Anyone who wants a compass for decades of hard use may also prefer something with a longer warranty and a more robust capsule mount.

## Verdict

The TruArc 3 delivers a global needle and easy declination setting at a price that hardly any competitor can match, in a 31 g package that disappears into a pocket. Quality control is uneven and the layout is dated, so test it before relying on it. For a budget backup or a first adjustable compass, it is a sound pick.

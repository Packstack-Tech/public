## Overview

The Nitecore Tiki is a polycarbonate-bodied keychain light that weighs 11 g and is about as long as a AA battery is wide. It pairs a 300-lumen OSRAM P8 main emitter with two side LEDs, a high-CRI white and a 365 nm UV. For thru-hikers, that makes it a backup light, a tent-interior reading light and a gear-check tool in a package lighter than most spare tent stakes. The trade-off is that it's a sealed, single-button gadget with a built-in 130 mAh battery. It isn't a headlamp replacement, and when the cell dies the light is done.

## Performance

**Main beam.** The Tiki's total-internal-reflection optic produces a wide, soft, even flood rather than a searchlight spot. That suits a trail light. It's good for finding the bear-hang cord, sorting a pack at the trailhead or walking to the privy. It won't pick out a trail junction at 70 m. Independent bench testing by ZeroAir on the original micro-USB version measured 282 lumens at 30 seconds, about 94 percent of the claim, with peak intensity just over 1,000 candela. The light steps down on its own to manage heat, which is what a 14.7 mm tube with a 130 mAh cell should do.

**Battery and charging.** The 40-hour figure applies to the lowest mode. On High, a cell this small is good for a short burst, not a night hike. Charging takes just over an hour, and reviewers describe the charge circuit as well regulated. Nitecore's Advanced Power Cut-Off design is meant to keep parasitic drain low between uses. Owner reports are mostly positive, but a minority of buyers on retail sites complain of a dead light after a few days on a keyring. For hikers, the practical habit is to top it off from a power bank before every resupply, the same as a phone.

**Auxiliary emitters.** The high-CRI side LED is the sleeper feature. At close range it renders colors accurately and is gentle enough for reading inside a tent without blinding a tentmate. It is the mode most owners end up using. The 500 mW UV LED is a smaller win. It will check a banknote, find scorpions in some regions and charge the glow-in-the-dark version's shell. Reviewers still call it limited, and buyers who expected serious fluorescence work found it modest. On the trail, it's a novelty.

**Interface.** One rubber button runs everything: short presses cycle four brightness levels, and a triple press reaches the UV and high-CRI modes. Two clicks are needed to turn the light on, so it resists switching on in a pocket, although there's no true electronic lock. Reviewers have flagged one real flaw, visible pulse-width modulation on the lower modes. Anyone sensitive to flicker will see it, particularly when panning the beam.

**Carry and durability.** The Tiki has no pocket clip, so it rides on the tail loop and the included split ring or small clasp. That loop is thick, and reviewers found it doesn't fit some key rings without the supplied hardware. It can't be clipped to a hat brim. The IP66 rating covers rain and splashes, not submersion, and the 1 m drop rating is adequate for a keychain. The stainless bezel doubles as a heat sink. Reviewers describe the build as good for the price, though the design closely resembles the RovyVon Aurora series that came before it.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 11 g, small enough to forget about on a pack zipper or lanyard
- Wide, even 300-lumen beam, with measured output close to the claim
- High-CRI side light is good for reading and close camp work
- USB-C charging in about 75 minutes
- IP66 sealing and a 1 m drop rating
- Typically $20–25, with good value reported by owners

**Cons**

- Sealed battery; the whole light is effectively disposable once the cell fades
- Visible PWM flicker in lower modes
- Weak UV output for its marketing weight
- No pocket or hat clip; keychain carry only
- A 130 mAh cell can't sustain high output for long
- Some buyers report poor charge retention

## Who Should Buy This

The Tiki suits ultralight hikers who want a backup light that costs essentially no weight, and section hikers who keep a keyring on their pack. It also fits anyone who wants a small reading light for the tent. It is a poor choice as a primary night-hiking light: a real headlamp is hands-free, longer-running and brighter on a sustained basis. Flicker-sensitive users should consider a competitor with a cleaner driver, and anyone who wants a replaceable cell should look at AAA-powered lights instead. The Tiki LE, sold at the same price with red and blue emitters in place of UV and high-CRI, is aimed at law enforcement and signaling and isn't the version to buy for camping.

## Verdict

The Nitecore Tiki is a well-priced, sensibly featured backup light whose high-CRI mode earns its place in a hiking kit. Its low-mode PWM, sealed battery and gimmicky UV keep it from being a standout. Buy it as a camp and emergency light, not as the one you'll navigate by.

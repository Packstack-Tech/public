## Overview

The Black Diamond Distance Carbon Z is a fixed-length, three-section folding pole made entirely of carbon fiber, and the company calls it its lightest Z-pole. It is built for trail runners, fastpackers and ultralight hikers whose poles spend as much time stowed as deployed. The trade is simple: give up adjustability, and a pair weighs less than a can of soda.

## Performance

### Weight and swing weight

The headline number is the point. A pair of 110 cm Carbon Zs weighs 264 g, and The Big Outside ranked them among the two lightest trekking poles it has reviewed. Exploring Wild's long-term review put the pair roughly 2 oz under both the adjustable Distance Carbon FLZ and the aluminum Distance Z. It also put them 4–5 oz under fully adjustable carbon poles such as the Zpacks Carbon Fiber Pole and the REI Flash Carbon. Retailer data backs this up: the nearest FLZ pair, at 336 g, outweighs the 110 cm Carbon Z by 72 g.

Grams matter most at the end of the arm. Owners consistently describe the pole as quick to plant and easy to carry over long climbs, with less accumulated forearm and wrist fatigue by the end of a long day than heavier aluminum or adjustable poles produce.

### Deployment and packability

Speed Cone deployment borrows from Black Diamond's avalanche-probe engineering. The three sections snap into line with a flick of the wrist and lock as the cord comes taut. Re-stowing is just as quick, which matters for runners who stash and redeploy poles at every steep pitch. The 120 cm pair folds to 40 cm, and the 110 cm pair to 37 cm. Either one disappears into a running-vest pocket or the side pocket of a small pack instead of poking up over a shoulder. The FLZ folds to a similar size, so the choice between the two comes down to adjustability versus mass.

### Grip and strap

The EVA foam grip is notched to save weight and add traction. The extension below it lets users choke down on climbs without changing pole length, which blunts one of the fixed-length design's weaknesses. The strap is a thin, partly mesh loop that wicks and dries quickly. It is also the pole's one real comfort compromise. It is less padded than the straps on Black Diamond's higher-end poles, and some long-day users report wrist abrasion. Short runs rarely expose this, but a multi-day fastpack leaning hard on the strap might call for light gloves.

### Durability

Carbon poles carry a reputation for snapping, and it is partly earned. The Big Outside's reviewer put them through wet, slick talus and loose scree in Idaho's White Clouds and Sawtooths without damage. Other testers have broken a pole by sitting on it, and carbon will fail under sideways or torsional stress where aluminum would bend. Black Diamond has a record of replacing failed poles. The interchangeable Tech Tips help, since tips are usually the first part to wear out on rock, and a worn pair can be swapped instead of retiring the pole. These are not poles for hauling heavy loads. Andrew Skurka's guidance puts folding Distance poles in the category for ultrarunners and petite backpackers: lighter and more collapsible than the Alpine Carbon Cork, but less steady and durable.

### The fixed-length trade-off

You can't shorten the poles for a steep climb, lengthen them for a descent, or lend them to a friend of a different height. The Big Outside's reviewer called this a minor ding that mostly matters on steep descents, and said ultralight users are the least likely to mind. Shelter owners should check first. Pitching a trekking-pole tent with a fixed length means the pole length must match the shelter's requirement, and some designs won't work at all.

## Choosing a Version

Black Diamond sells the Carbon Z in 5 cm steps. The two versions here sit at opposite ends of the practical range.

**264 g pair (110 cm).** At 132 g per pole, this is the lightest length that fits the five-length range listed here. It folds to 37 cm, the smallest packed size of the set. It suits shorter hikers and runners, and anyone who plans to hold the pole low on the grip extension or keep it stowed most of the time.

**280 g pair (120 cm).** At 140 g per pole, this length costs 16 g more per pair and folds to 40 cm. It fits mid-height users and is the usual choice for hikers who want a taller pole for flat or rolling terrain.

The 115 cm (272 g), 125 cm (288 g) and 130 cm (296 g) lengths follow the same pattern. Each 5 cm adds about 8 g per pair and 1–2 cm of collapsed length. Pick the length that matches your height and stride. Shaving 16 g by buying a pole that is too short is a poor trade.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 264–280 g per pair, among the lightest folding poles available
- Folds to 37–40 cm, small enough for a vest pocket
- Speed Cone deployment is fast enough to use on the move
- EVA grip extension partly offsets the lack of adjustability
- Interchangeable carbide Tech Tips and removable baskets
- Low swing weight reduces arm fatigue on long days

**Cons**

- Fixed length, so a wrong size choice can't be corrected
- Thin strap can chafe on long, strap-heavy days
- Carbon can snap under sideways or torsional stress
- Not suited to heavy loads or rough use
- Incompatible with some trekking-pole shelters
- Expensive for a pole this simple

## Who Should Buy This

Trail runners, fastpackers, ultralight hikers and travelers who care more about grams and packed size than adjustability will find the Carbon Z hard to beat. It works for hikers who already know their pole length and who rarely shelter under a trekking-pole tent. Hikers who pitch a pole-supported shelter, carry loads over about 35 lb, or want one pole for everything should look at an adjustable model such as the Distance Carbon FLZ or Gossamer Gear's LT5. They give up some grams for flexibility.

## Verdict

The Distance Carbon Z does one job, moving fast with almost nothing in your hands, better than nearly anything at its price. Buy it only if you are sure of your length, and be prepared to treat it with a little care. For the ultralight, run-and-hike crowd, it earns its high price.

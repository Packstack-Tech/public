## Overview

The Silva Expedition 4 is a full-size baseplate compass. It has been the default choice of British hillwalkers, Duke of Edinburgh groups and several armed forces for decades. At 36 g it is not the smallest compass a thru-hiker can carry, but the long baseplate and three-scale romer set make it one of the quickest to use with a paper map. Ultralighters who want a bare-minimum button compass should look elsewhere. Anyone who plots grid references or takes bearings off 1:25,000 maps will find it hard to beat.

## Performance

**Map work.** This is where the Expedition 4 earns its reputation. The baseplate is long enough to lay a bearing across a 1:25,000 sheet without guesswork. One UK Hillwalking forum poster put it bluntly: anything with a smaller plate is a poor choice for that scale. Chris Townsend, writing in The Great Outdoors, picked it as his first choice among the compasses he reviewed. He cited the long plate and the romer scales, and noted it was the only model in his group with all three common scales. UKClimbing posters add that the romers sit on both the northing and easting axes, so reading a grid reference takes seconds. Many other compasses only have one axis marked.

**Needle and capsule.** The needle settles quickly and doesn't wobble, which matters when a bearing has to be taken in wind, cold or cloud. Silva's published figures put settling to within ±3° at 2.7 seconds and full rest at 5 seconds. Accuracy is ±1°, with 2° graduations on the bezel. Advnture's testers described the needle as fast to settle and called the layout user-friendly. Their complaints were small: a cheap lanyard and a bezel that is not the easiest to grip.

**Low light and weather.** The luminous markings on the dial, needle and baseplate last about six hours after charging, enough for a night approach or an early alpine start. The IP68 rating and the -40 to +60 °C operating range cover most conditions a three-season hiker will meet, and the compass is rated for use at altitude. Silicone pads on the underside keep the plate from sliding on a wet map. The magnifier helps with contour detail under a headlamp. Townsend notes he rarely uses the luminous points, which is a fair reflection of how most daytime hikers will use them.

**Declination.** The compass has a declination scale, and Townsend points out that it isn't a permanent needle adjustment. Users offset the bearing by hand, as they would with most baseplate compasses. That suits people who navigate in one region, but it means the arithmetic never goes away.

**Durability.** Owners are mostly positive. One owner of a 27-year-old Silva says it still works fine, and a long-term UK reviewer reports no leaks or stiffness after years of mountain use. The complaints are the familiar ones for liquid-filled capsules. A Duke of Edinburgh instructor who went through three of them reported air bubbles, worn markings and, in one case, a bezel that came off the plate. Others report scratching on the acrylic baseplate and a lanyard that chafes. Heavy users on UK forums say Silvas wear out faster than they would like for the price.

**Weight.** The 36 g figure is Silva's own. Retail listings and individual reviewers give figures anywhere from the mid-30s to about 60 g depending on whether the lanyard is counted, so plan on roughly 40 g in the pack. A thumb-sized capsule compass saves around half of that. The cost is the missing ruler, romers and long sighting edge. Few navigators miss those on a day when the map comes out.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Long baseplate makes bearings on 1:25k and 1:50k maps quick and precise
- Romer scales for 1:25k, 1:40k and 1:50k, marked on both axes
- Fast-settling needle with ±1° accuracy
- Magnifier, mm and inch rulers, and stencil holes in a 36 g package
- IP68 and -40 to +60 °C operating range
- Typically sells for roughly £24–38 in the UK

**Cons**

- Larger than a thru-hiker's minimalist compass
- Declination is corrected by hand, not by a permanent needle adjustment
- Luminous markings need light charging and fade after about six hours
- Liquid capsules can develop bubbles, and some owners report scratched plates and worn markings
- Basic lanyard, and a bezel that can be hard to grip

## Who Should Buy This

This compass suits anyone who navigates by paper map: hillwalkers on UK-style terrain, section hikers in featureless country, instructors, and anyone who wants a backup that plots a grid reference accurately. It also suits people who find tiny compasses fiddly with cold or gloved hands.

Hikers on a well-marked long trail, who need only a rough heading to check against a phone, will gain nothing from the long plate. A button or mini-sighting compass will serve them as well and weigh less. Anyone who needs a sighting mirror or an adjustable declination mechanism should look at Silva's mirrored models or a competitor.

## Verdict

The Expedition 4 does the job a baseplate compass is supposed to do, and for 36 g it does it well. It is not a minimalist pick, and some capsules will develop bubbles over the years. For anyone who actually plots bearings on a map, though, it is still the standard to beat.

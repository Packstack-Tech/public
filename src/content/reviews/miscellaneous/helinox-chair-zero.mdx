## Overview

The Helinox Chair Zero is the yardstick for ultralight camp seating: a four-legged sling chair on a shock-corded DAC aluminum frame that packs to roughly the size of a large water bottle. It comes in three versions: the 482 g Standard, the 539 g LT with a mesh seat and ground stabilizer, and the 660 g L with a 320 lb rating. The trade-off is the usual one for this class. Weight and bulk are cut hard, so the seat is low and shallow, and the chair has no pockets, armrests or cup holder.

## Performance

**Comfort.** The Chair Zero's main trick is posture. The seat pocket holds the lower back straight instead of letting it curl, and the backrest rises above the bottom of the shoulder blades on an average-sized adult. Testers repeatedly rate it the most comfortable of the super-light chairs. Reviewers at Better Trail noted no pinch points, a contrast with solid-seat competitors like the REI Flexlite Air. Slits along the seat edges let the fabric flex and breathe instead of cutting into the thighs.

The limits are real, though. The pocket is shallow and the seat is short, so some sitters feel themselves sliding forward over a long evening. Taller or broader users report the narrow back folding its top corners in toward the armpits and shoulders, and the Standard fits best below about six feet. At 8.5 inches, the seat is low enough that getting up is a squat, which is hardest on bad knees. The fabric also runs cool against the back and arms in cold weather. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers called it supportive but not a multi-hour lounger.

**Stability.** The A-frame layout spreads the legs wide and drops the center of gravity, and a side-to-side crossbar resists twist. Lateral stability is the chair's strongest suit, and it handles sloping pine needles and tilted granite better than most rivals. Front-to-back is the weaker axis, so leaning hard in either direction is a bad idea.

Soft ground is the Achilles heel. The rubber-tipped feet are only slightly wider than tent-pole ends and sink into sand and loose dirt. Hard dirt and rock cause no trouble. Helinox sells ball feet and a ground sheet for sandy trips, and the LT version builds the fix in.

**Packability and setup.** At about 17 oz, the Standard is among the lightest elevated chairs on the market, and the packed bundle slides into the side pocket of most packs. Shock-corded poles snap together, and the chair goes from stuff sack to seated in under a minute. The first few times, the fabric is tight enough that getting it onto the pole ends takes effort. The logo sits on the side of the fabric rather than the top, so owners often mount the seat upside down. Look for the "this side up" label.

**Durability.** The DAC TH72M poles are the same family used in high-end backpacking tents. Reinforced corners protect the pole sockets, and the resin hubs hold up. Better Trail's tester reported no fraying or hub squeaks after dirt, sweat and being tossed in a car. A long-term owner has carried a Standard on trips for years with no visible wear. The 100-denier seat is thinner than the 600-denier fabric on the heavier Chair One, so it shouldn't be dragged across rock.

## Choosing a Version

**Standard (482 g).** The baseline and the lightest of the three. It suits hikers under six feet who camp mostly on firm ground and want the least weight.

**LT (539 g).** Helinox swaps the solid seat for GhostGrid, a translucent monofilament grid ripstop that resists the spread of tears and breathes better. It adds an X-strap stabilizer that widens the footprint, so the chair stays up on sand, dirt and grass. Helinox lists the LT at 494 g trail weight and 534 g packed. It carries the same 265 lb rating as the Standard and costs $159.95. Pick it for desert, beach or meadow trips, or for hot-weather hikers who want a mesh back. REI buyers rate it 4.4 out of 5.

**Standard L (660 g).** This version has a wider frame and thicker poles, and it is rated to 320 lb, 55 lb more than the Standard. Helinox lists it at 20.5" wide, 19.5" deep and 26" tall, with a 14" × 4.5" × 4.5" packed size. It is the pick for larger hikers, and for taller ones who find the Standard's back too narrow. The cost is about 180 g over the Standard.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Best comfort-to-weight ratio in the ultralight chair category
- Packs to 14 inches long, small enough for a side pocket
- Strong side-to-side stability, including on uneven ground
- Proven DAC aluminum frame with a 5-year warranty
- Three versions cover mesh, soft-ground and heavy-user needs
- Fast tool-free setup on a single shock cord

**Cons**

- Premium pricing, with the LT at $159.95
- 8.5-inch seat is low and hard on knees
- Shallow seat lets some users slide forward
- Standard feet sink in sand and soft soil without an add-on
- Narrow back is tight on tall or broad users in the Standard
- No pockets, cup holder or armrests, and small items can slip through the seat slits

## Who Should Buy This

The Chair Zero is for backpackers who do multi-day trips, long lunch stops or summit sits and no longer want to perch on a cold rock. Hikers under six feet on firm ground will be best served by the Standard. Those who camp on sand or loose soil should take the LT, and larger or taller hikers should go straight to the L. Anyone who wants to lounge or recline for hours, or who cares more about price than grams, should look at a heavier chair or a cheaper stool. Gram-counters who accept a flimsier build can save a little weight with the REI Flexlite Air, though the Helinox is steadier from side to side.

## Verdict

The Chair Zero delivers on its promise with few caveats: it's light, quick to set up, stable on firm ground, and comfortable enough to justify its weight. The price stings and the seat is low, but few pieces of gear feel as much like a luxury at this weight. Buy the Standard for firm ground, the LT for sand or heat, and the L for bigger frames.

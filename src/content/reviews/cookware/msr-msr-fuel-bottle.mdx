## Overview

The MSR Fuel Bottle is the standard container for anyone running a Whisperlite, Dragonfly, XGK, or other MSR liquid-fuel stove. It's not glamorous — it's a machined aluminum tube with a threaded neck that mates to MSR's fuel pump — but it's the piece of kit that decides whether your stove pressurizes cleanly and doesn't leak white gas into the bottom of your pack. This 11 oz / 325 ml version is the smallest of the three sizes MSR offers and is best suited to solo trips or shorter outings where you don't need a full 20 or 30 oz of fuel capacity.

## Performance

There's not much to "perform" on a fuel bottle beyond not leaking, holding pressure, and threading onto your pump without drama — and on those counts, the MSR bottle does its job. <cite index="19-15,19-16,19-17">MSR fuel bottles are made from a single piece of impact-extruded aluminum, meaning no seams or welds, which helps prevent leaks and cracks, and the aluminum is thicker at the base and shoulders so it resists bulging when pressurized.</cite> That construction detail matters more than it sounds — a fuel bottle that bulges or dents at the seams under pump pressure is a genuine safety concern, and it's the reason MSR's bottles have stayed the de facto standard for decades.

User feedback backs this up. One long-term owner mentioned <cite index="13-19,13-20,13-21,13-22">loving their older bottle, noting it's more than ten years old and still going strong despite multiple dings, with the original rubber gasket on the cap still looking good as new, and calling the bottle "literally indestructible."</cite> Another reviewer flatly said <cite index="13-2">"I have never had any of my MSR bottles leak."</cite> That's the kind of unglamorous reliability you want from something that's going to be pressurized to several PSI and jostled around in your pack next to your food bag.

The recurring complaint isn't about the bottle itself — it's the cap. MSR moved to a child-resistant "push-and-twist" cap, and it's polarizing. One reviewer described it as <cite index="13-7,13-8">"very difficult to remove if the fuel bottle becomes pressurized, (altitude, heats up... in the sun, etc.)," adding concerns about "stripped out teeth inside the cap and not being able to open/close the bottle."</cite> Another simply called it useless for backcountry use, asking <cite index="17-5">"who takes small children backpacking or on expeditions?"</cite> A different buyer reported a more concrete failure: <cite index="13-27">one of two safety caps stopped opening after fewer than a half dozen uses.</cite> If you're buying new, it's worth budgeting a few minutes to practice opening the cap at home under light pressure before you're relying on it at 12,000 feet with cold fingers.

On the plus side, not everyone hates it — several users on Dick's Sporting Goods' listing reported zero issues, including one using it as backup fuel storage on a motorcycle who said <cite index="17-9,17-10">it worked perfectly with no leaks at the cap and poured without dripping over the tank.</cite> So the cap complaints seem to cluster around pressurized use with a hot stove pump attached, not casual storage and pouring.

At 325 ml, this size is genuinely small — enough for maybe 2-4 days of solo cooking depending on how much you're melting snow versus just boiling water for dinner. If you're doing anything beyond a long weekend or you're melting snow regularly, size up to the 22 oz version; the 11 oz will have you refueling more often than you'd like.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros:**

- Single-piece extruded aluminum construction with no seams to fail
- Reinforced base and shoulder resist bulging under pump pressure
- Long track record of durability — multiple owners report 10+ years of use
- Fits the entire MSR liquid-fuel stove lineup (Whisperlite, Dragonfly, XGK, WindPro conversions, etc.)
- Airtight seal keeps stored fuel from degrading between trips

**Cons:**

- Child-resistant cap is fiddly and, per multiple reports, can be hard to open when the bottle is pressurized or hot
- Some caps reportedly fail (stripped threads) after limited use
- 11 oz capacity is small — fine for short solo trips, limiting for longer trips or group cooking
- No fuel included, obviously, but worth noting since it's easy to forget you need white gas or Coleman fuel on top of the bottle itself

## Who Should Buy This

This is the right bottle for solo or weekend backpackers running an MSR liquid-fuel stove who don't need to carry much fuel — think 2-4 night trips without heavy snow melting. If you're doing longer trips, guiding, or winter mountaineering where snow melt eats fuel fast, get the 20 or 30 oz version instead and treat this one as a spare or a lightweight option for short outings.

## Verdict

The MSR Fuel Bottle is exactly what you'd expect from a decades-old industry standard: simple, tough, and functionally identical to the version your dad probably used. The aluminum body has earned its reputation for durability, but the newer child-resistant cap is a legitimate annoyance worth testing before you're relying on it in the field. I'd rate it a 7.5/10 — solid and dependable, docked a bit for a cap design that seems to frustrate as many users as it protects.

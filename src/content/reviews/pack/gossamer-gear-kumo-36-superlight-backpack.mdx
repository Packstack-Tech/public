## Overview

The Kumo 36 Superlight is Gossamer Gear's do-it-all minimalist pack — a frameless 36-liter hauler built for hikers who've already dialed in a sub-15-lb base weight and don't need a suspension system to carry it. It's not trying to be a load hauler or a bombproof thru-hike workhorse; it's aimed at ultralight overnighters, fast-and-light multi-day trips, and long day hikes where every ounce on your back matters more than bells and whistles.

## Performance

The Kumo's whole pitch is weight-to-feature ratio, and it mostly delivers. <cite index="10-1">Weighing just 14.6 ounces (or 16.6 ounces including a SitLight foam pad), it's a fully-featured ultralight backpack that can be used for overnight trips, but is on the small side in terms of capacity at 36 L, with 28 L of covered capacity and roughly 500 cubic inches in pockets</cite>. That's a meaningful amount of organized storage for a pack this light — a stretchy front pocket, side bottle pockets, shoulder-strap pockets, and a rear mesh pocket all pull double duty as quick-access storage without having to break the roll-top seal.

Comfort is where the pack surprises people who assume "frameless" means "your shoulders will hate you." <cite index="7-1,7-2">The Kumo 36 Superlight Backpack is functional and super comfortable, even when fully loaded up, and while its space is limited, Gossamer Gear made exceptional use of the space</cite>. On technical terrain it stays put — <cite index="7-12,7-13">during scrambling maneuvers the pack moved with the tester in a very nice way, never obstructing range of movement, and stayed solidly on the back with no uncomfortable rubbing or sliding</cite>. Long-term testers report similar results even further into a trip: <cite index="3-4,3-5">the backpack carries great even fully loaded with a load of around 22 lbs, and the padded shoulder straps do a great job spreading the load and are still comfortable after an 8-hour day on trail</cite> (that's the vest-style Fast Kumo variant, but the carry principles are shared).

The hip belt deserves a specific callout because it's unusual for a pack this light: it's genuinely removable and comes in two sizes bundled together. <cite index="10-1">Both sizes are bundled with the pack, making it possible to share the Kumo between two people with different waistlines</cite>, and despite minimal padding, <cite index="10-2">the lack of hip belt padding is not an issue — testers report being able to carry about 15 pounds all day with this hip belt without any</cite> real discomfort. Don't expect it to transfer serious load off your shoulders past that 15-20 lb range, though — this is a frameless pack, and physics doesn't care how nice the webbing is.

Where the Kumo gets more mixed reviews is durability and versatility. Outdoor Gear Lab's testing flagged it directly: <cite index="1-3,1-5">it's a pack so light and small that it passes more for a daypack than a multi-day backpacking pack, and while it's one of the lightest packs in its review class, the weight-to-volume ratio isn't overwhelmingly light for the measured volume it has</cite>. Their bottom-line take was that the Kumo shines on long day hikes, and <cite index="1-3">with a super-paired-down kit, it would work for weekend trips into the backcountry</cite> — but it's not a pack you want to lean on for a heavy food carry or a long thru-hike resupply stretch.

Fabric durability is the recurring asterisk across reviews. Gossamer Gear updated the build to 70D/100D recycled Robic nylon, and testers note <cite index="5-8,5-9">the 2024 redesign brought a lightweight and enduring 70D Recycled Robic Nylon build</cite>, which is tougher than the ultra-thin fabrics used on some competing frameless packs. Still, one long-distance tester's experience is a useful cautionary tale: <cite index="8-1">after a month on trail, the side pockets were in pretty bad shape enough that the pack was sent back to Gossamer Gear in exchange for a replacement</cite>, and the fix was simply <cite index="8-2">making sure to only put one water bottle in each side pocket for the rest of the trail</cite>. If you're rough on stretch-mesh pockets, treat them gently or expect to baby them.

A few small design quirks show up repeatedly: <cite index="4-5">side panels can flare out awkwardly under the top lid, and the center ice axe loop placement is awkward, since two loops (one on each side) would work better than a single center loop</cite>. None of these are dealbreakers, but they're the kind of thing you notice on trip three, not trip one.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Genuinely one of the lightest fully-featured packs in the 30-40L class
- Removable hip belt in two sizes, bundled at no extra cost
- Excellent lateral stability and comfortable carry on technical/scrambling terrain
- Smart pocket layout (front stretch pocket, side bottle pockets, shoulder pockets) for on-the-move access
- Foam back panel doubles as a sit pad or sleeping pad supplement
- PFAS-free recycled fabric, a nice sustainability touch without a weight penalty

**Cons**

- 25 lb max load is a real ceiling, not a suggestion — heavier food/water carries will feel it
- Side pockets have shown wear issues under repeated water bottle abuse on long trips
- Frameless design means no meaningful load transfer past ~15-20 lbs
- Small enough capacity that it can feel more like an ambitious daypack than a multi-day hauler
- Minor design nitpicks (side panel flare, single-loop ice axe attachment)

## Who Should Buy This

This pack is built for the hiker who already has a sub-15-lb base weight dialed in and wants a nearly-disappearing pack to carry it — think weekend ultralight overnighters, fast-and-light multi-day trips with minimal food carries, or ambitious day hikes where you want extra capacity in reserve. If your kit still includes a bulky sleeping bag, a framed tent, or you're planning 5+ day food carries between resupplies, you'll outgrow the Kumo's comfort zone fast. It's also a good fit for hikers who want a lightweight loaner setup, thanks to the two-size hip belt included in every purchase.

## Verdict

The Kumo 36 Superlight does what it says on the label: it's a fully-featured, sub-21-oz pack that carries surprisingly well for its minimal frame and padding, as long as you respect its 25-lb ceiling and treat the stretch pockets with some care. It's not the pack for someone still transitioning off a traditional framed backpack, but for hikers who've already lightened their base weight, it's one of the most compelling options in the class. I'd put it at a 7.8/10 — excellent value and comfort-to-weight ratio, docked slightly for the pocket durability concerns and the genuinely tight load ceiling.

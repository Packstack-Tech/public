## Overview

The Voile Strap began life in 1984 as a way to bind skis together. It has since become the default answer to "how do I hold this to that" on bikepacking rigs, ski packs and thru-hiker repair kits. The 12-inch nylon-buckle version is the short, quiet member of the family. It's a semi-elastic polyurethane band with a non-marring buckle, and it earns its place by doing a dozen small jobs well. The trade-off is a modest reach: it only wraps loads between 4.3 and 9.4 inches in circumference.

## Performance

The strap's whole trick is tension. The polyurethane band stretches, so once it's looped back through the buckle and hooked onto the prong like a belt, it keeps squeezing the load. Reviewers at Road.cc described the closure as quick and secure, and noted it releases with a simple pull-down and unhook even after being cinched hard. One bikepacker who swapped it in for webbing on a seat-post tool roll found it easier to use and just as secure. The same reviewer liked that, unlike webbing or Velcro, it shrugs off mud and grit and doesn't snag clothing. Because the strap flexes to fit, a single length handles a tube, a pump and a tire lever one day and a different bundle the next, with no re-threading.

The material is built for neglect. Voile adds a UV inhibitor to the polyurethane, and the company says the strap is unaffected by heat or freezing temperatures. That matches how the strap is used: lashed to a bar bag in July sun, then stuffed in a pocket in a January ski tour. The textured surface grips on its own, which is why it stays put on slick dry bag fabric and bare tubing.

The nylon buckle is the point of this version. It won't scratch frames, carbon bars, camera bodies or painted racks. It's also non-reflective, which suits anyone who dislikes a flash of metal on their pack. And it's non-conductive, a feature electricians prize and most hikers will never notice. Voile positions the nylon buckle for delicate surfaces, bikepacking and electrical gear, while the aluminum-buckle version targets ski touring and heavier lashings.

On the trail, a 12-inch strap suits compact jobs: bundling trekking poles or a tent pole section, hanging a stuff sack off a pack strap, cinching a sagging shoulder strap or a flapping gaiter, holding a splint or a failing ski skin in place, or lashing a cracked pole to a spare. It works as a stand-in for duct tape, which is how Voile pitches it, with the advantage that it's reusable and doesn't leave residue. Straps can also be daisy-chained for longer reach. The buckle design makes that easy, though one tester found it fiddly to keep two straps joined while tightening the second. If a job needs that often, a longer strap is the fix.

The limitation is plain: the 12-inch strap cannot go around anything bigger than about 9.4 inches in circumference, which rules out a sleeping bag stuff sack or a foam pad roll. Road.cc's verdict also flagged the price as a little steep for what is, at heart, a rubber band with a buckle. It's an $7.50 argument, and owners tend to counter it by buying several, since the strap rarely wears out.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Stretch tension holds loads tight and keeps them from working loose over rough ground
- Nylon buckle won't scratch frames, poles or electronics
- UV-resistant, and unfazed by heat, cold and wet
- Easy single-handed operation, even with cold fingers
- Daisy-chains with other straps when more length is needed
- Reusable alternative to duct tape in a repair kit
- Made in Utah, with decades of track record

**Cons**

- Only handles loads 4.3–9.4 in around, so bulky items need a longer strap
- 26 g adds up if a hiker carries a handful
- Pricier than generic hook-and-loop or cord alternatives
- Nylon buckle is less sturdy than Voile's aluminum version for heavy ski or load-bearing use

## Who Should Buy This

Thru-hikers, bikepackers and backcountry skiers who want a couple of small, durable fix-it straps in the repair kit will get the most from it. It's also a sound choice for anyone lashing gear to a painted bike frame, a camera rig or an electronics case, where a metal buckle might mar a surface. Hikers who need to compress a sleeping bag, strap a pad to the outside of a pack or carry a large dry bag should look at the 20-, 25- or 32-inch lengths instead. Gram-counters who rarely need more than a single tie-down can get by with a section of cord.

## Verdict

The 12-inch nylon-buckle Voile Strap is a cheap, tough and reusable fix that outlasts the duct tape it replaces. It costs a little more than it seems it should, and it's too short for big loads. For small bundling and repair work, though, a pair or three in the kit earns its 26 g.

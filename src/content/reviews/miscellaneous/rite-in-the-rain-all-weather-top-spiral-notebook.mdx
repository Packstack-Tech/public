## Overview

The Rite In The Rain No. 146 is a 4" x 6" top-spiral notebook with 50 sheets of all-weather paper, a yellow Polydura cover and a Wire-O binding that resists rust. It was built for surveyors, contractors and field crews, and it has migrated to the trail for the same reason: a regular notebook turns to pulp the first time a pack liner fails. The trade-off is weight and price. At 91 g, it costs more in both grams and dollars than the paper pad most hikers would otherwise carry.

## Performance

**Weather resistance.** The paper is the point. Rite in the Rain's coating sheds water, sweat, grease and mud, and the company's own pitch is that notes survive "through rain, sweat, mud, & over time." Owners across hiking, hunting, lab and construction settings report the same thing: the pages stay legible after a soaking, and a spilled coffee wipes off without bleeding into the next sheet. For a trail journal or a log of water sources and mileages, that is real insurance. A damp pocket or a leaky bottle in the pack no longer means losing a day's notes.

**Writing in the wet.** The paper works with a pencil or an all-weather pen when it is actively raining. A standard ballpoint or permanent marker writes normally once the page is dry, but it skips on a wet sheet. Pencil is the usual backcountry pairing, and a mechanical pencil is a common choice because it needs no sharpening. Ultralight hikers who bring a pen often take Rite in the Rain's own all-weather model. Expect the 20# stock to feel slick and slightly stiff compared with ordinary notebook paper. It takes pencil well but never feels soft.

**Durability.** Backpackers on the Backpacking Light forums have long said the paper's tear resistance matters more to them than its waterproofing. Ordinary notebook pages loosen from the spiral after a few days of pack abuse, leaving loose sheets and a bare wire. This paper doesn't rip, so the notebook can ride loose in a lid pocket or hip belt. The Polydura cover is tough but flexible and protects against scratches and stains. The Wire-O binding holds its shape in a pack or a back pocket, and the rounded corners don't snag or dog-ear.

**Format.** The top spiral lets the book flip back on itself, which suits writing standing up, in a tent vestibule or with gloves on. The 4" x 6" page is large enough for a day's notes on one side and small enough to slide into a pocket. The Universal pattern is a mix of ruled and gridded areas that handles lists, sketches and trail measurements without much fuss. The ruler and map-scale reference on the cover is a small bonus for anyone navigating from a paper map.

**Weight.** Here the notebook gives ground. At 91 g it is not an ounce-counter's item. The usual fix among gram-conscious hikers is to cut the book in half or tear out the pages they won't need, which is easy to do with a top-spiral book. Even so, this is a full-size field notebook, and anyone who only wants to jot a few lines a day will pay for paper they never use.

**Price.** The manufacturer lists a three-pack at $20.85, about $7 per notebook. Retailers sell singles for anywhere from under $6 to nearly $13. That is a lot more than a drugstore pad, though owners who have filled several of these over years of field work generally consider it fair.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Paper sheds water, sweat, mud and grease, and stays legible
- Very high tear resistance; pages don't work loose from the spiral
- Top spiral flips open for one-handed writing and standing use
- Rust-resistant Wire-O keeps its shape in a pack or pocket
- Cover includes rulers, conversion table and map scales
- Made in the USA, widely stocked

**Cons**

- 91 g is heavy for hikers who count every gram
- Ballpoint pens won't write on wet pages; pencil or all-weather pen required
- Slicker, stiffer paper than standard notebooks
- Costs several times more than a regular pocket notepad
- Pages aren't numbered or perforated

## Who Should Buy This

This notebook suits thru-hikers and section hikers who keep a daily journal, log resupply and mileage data, or sketch campsites, and who have been burned by wet paper before. It also suits anglers, hunters, mountaineers and anyone writing in a climate where rain is a regular visitor. Hikers who only need a few lines per day should trim it or take a smaller book, and anyone who prefers to write with a ballpoint in the rain should plan on carrying a pencil as well. Anyone on a strict base-weight diet who is unconcerned about wet notes can skip it and carry a few sheets in a zip-top bag.

## Verdict

The No. 146 does one job well: it keeps paper notes legible in any weather and survives the abuse of a loaded pack. The cost is 91 g and a higher price, both of which are easy to reduce by cutting the book down. For hikers who value written records, it is the safe default.

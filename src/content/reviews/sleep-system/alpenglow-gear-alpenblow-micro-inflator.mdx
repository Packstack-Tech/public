## Overview

The Alpenblow Micro Inflator is a USB-C-powered fan about the weight of two nickels. It borrows power from the phone or battery bank already in a hiker's pack and fills a backcountry sleeping pad in roughly three minutes. The trade-off is built into the design: no battery, no switch and a small motor mean the lowest weight on the market, but also a pad that still needs a breath or two to reach full firmness.

## Performance

The Alpenblow is a small fan with a set of valve adapters. It has no onboard battery and no on/off switch. Plug it into a power source and it runs; unplug it and it stops. Section Hiker's reviewer called leaving out the battery the smartest decision in the design. Most backpackers already carry a power bank, and a second battery would only add weight.

In use, the sequence is simple. Pick the nozzle that matches the valve, seat it, plug in the cable, and let it run while the rest of camp gets set up. Section Hiker's testing covered a NEMO Tensor Elite, an Exped Ultra 5R, a Therm-a-Rest NeoAir XTherm NXT Max and a Sea to Summit Etherlite XT Insulated. The reviewer said it inflated all four easily. Retailer testing with a regular NeoAir XLite landed near the three-minute claim, at a cost of about 0.75% of a phone's battery.

Noise is a strength. At a listed 42 dBA, owners describe it as nearly silent, which matters in a shared shelter or a crowded campsite. One long-term owner who used a NEMO Tensor on a six-day trip praised the quiet and the negligible drain on a battery bank.

The limits are just as consistent. The motor is modest, and the pad that results is full but not firm. The manufacturer says most testers wanted an extra breath or two to reach their preferred pressure. Section Hiker reached the same conclusion: topping off is still required, but the grind of inflating from scratch, or wrestling a pump sack, is gone. Bigger pads take more time and lose more pressure. Backpacker found a Therm-a-Rest NeoLoft took around four minutes and ended up noticeably underinflated. Section Hiker noted that a huge mat like the Exped Megamat is beyond it. The manufacturer says it also won't inflate rafts or oversized mattresses, and it can't deflate anything.

Some owners report results well off the three-minute figure. One reviewer on the manufacturer's site, using a small 6,000 mAh battery, needed about 12 minutes to get a large NeoAir XTherm NXT Max most of the way full. Another found inflation times roughly quadrupled at 9,500 feet and above compared with a test at 5,000 feet. Garage Grown Gear's 4.2-star average over 76 reviews shows the same split: most owners are delighted, while a minority hit pads or conditions that expose the small motor. Two practical notes: it needs a USB-C source, so iPhones on Lightning cables are out, and a nozzle that isn't seated well will leak. A Southwest Hikes tester described a seal that wasn't perfect on a Sea to Summit pad but still locked in well enough.

Against a pump sack, the weight math is lopsided. Pump sacks that ship with pads can weigh several times as much as the Alpenblow, and Backpacker's comparison against the next-lightest competitor, the Flextail Zero, found them in different weight classes. The Alpenblow also won't touch the battery budget on a typical trip.

## Choosing a Version

The two versions differ by 1.6 g: the Standard weighs 8 g, and the NEMO adapter version weighs 9.6 g. Both ship with the full nozzle set listed above, including the NEMO Laylow adapter. Hikers on NEMO pads, and on Zenbivy Ultralight or Flex mattresses (which retailers say pair with the NEMO adapter), should take the NEMO adapter version. Everyone else should take the Standard, since 1.6 g buys nothing for Therm-a-Rest, Sea to Summit, EXPED or Big Agnes valves.

Hikers who want more capability should also look at the Alpenblow Lux, a separate product from the same maker. It weighs 13.7 g and adds a deflation port and reading lamps. The maker claims twice the inflation speed and 80% more pressure than the original, and Treeline Review's testers found it faster too. It costs the same $60 and may be the better buy for anyone with a thick pad.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- At 8 g, about as light as a pad inflator gets
- Quiet at 42 dBA
- Draws about 18 mAh at 5V, under 1% of a phone's battery
- Five nozzle types included
- Easy to use and nearly impossible to break: no switch, no battery
- Takes the dizziness out of inflating a pad after a long day

**Cons**

- Doesn't reach full firmness; most users still add a breath or two
- Struggles with large, thick or high-volume pads
- Can't deflate
- Some owners report much slower inflation than the claimed three minutes, especially at altitude or on a small battery
- Requires USB-C; no on/off switch means unplugging is the only way to stop it
- About $60 for a convenience item

## Who Should Buy This

The Alpenblow suits thru-hikers and gram-counters who already carry a USB-C power bank and sleep on a standard or regular-width ultralight pad. A hiker who hates blowing up a pad after a 25-mile day, or who wants to retire a 70 g pump sack, gets the most from it. Anyone with a thick, wide or long pad, or who wants a full-firmness inflation without any breathing, should look at the Lux or a heavier pump. So should anyone who wants to deflate quickly.

## Verdict

The Alpenblow Micro Inflator does one small job well, at a weight that barely registers on the scale. It earns a place in the kit of ultralight hikers on mainstream backpacking pads. Those who expect a rock-firm pad with no help, or who sleep on a bulky mat, should buy the Lux instead.

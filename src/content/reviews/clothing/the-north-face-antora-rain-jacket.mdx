## Overview

The Antora is The North Face's entry-level waterproof shell. It's a seam-sealed DryVent 2L jacket in recycled nylon, cut to look right on a wet commute and hold up on a day hike. It keeps rain out and costs little. For ultralight backpackers, the trade-off is that it weighs 600 g and has few of the features a long-distance shell needs.

## Performance

**Weather protection.** The Antora's reputation rests on staying dry in ordinary rain. Buyers across retailers say it blocks wind and steady showers, and one camper reported staying dry through wind and light rain. Outdoor Gear Lab tested the women's version and scored its water resistance 7.0 out of 10, a solid middle result. Those testers called it a good fit for light-activity weather. Wearers describe it as a shoulder-season jacket for damp commutes and drizzly days. That's the weather it was designed for, and it isn't a storm shell.

**Breathability.** This is where the Antora loses ground. The jacket has a 2L build with a mesh lining, and Outdoor Gear Lab's testers found it breathed poorly: 3.0 out of 10, the weakest mark in their scorecard. It has no pit zips, so a climb in warm rain means you'll trap heat. The 70D face fabric is also sturdier than the thin ripstop used on dedicated ultralight shells. Hikers who move fast and sweat will notice both the extra fabric and the lack of venting.

**Weight and packability.** At 600 g, the Antora is a heavy rain jacket by ultralight standards. Dedicated trail shells usually weigh well under half that. The 2L construction adds bulk, and Outdoor Gear Lab's testers flagged the lack of a stow pocket and the jacket's bulk when packed. It will fit in a pack, but it takes up real room.

**Features and fit.** The hood has three panels and adjusts, and buyers say it fits over a baseball cap. Testers found its coverage minimal, though, and said it leaves the face exposed in hard rain. Zippered hand pockets are deep and close securely, which keeps a phone dry. The weak spot is the elastic cuffs. Owners on several retail sites say they wish the cuffs had hook-and-loop adjustment, and some also dislike the pocket placement. Reviewers say the cut runs true to size with room for a base layer and light fleece. Slim-build wearers may prefer to size down. Sizes run up to 5X, which is unusual for a technical shell.

**Materials.** The fabric is fully recycled, and the DWR contains no PFCs. Long-term durability is still an open question. A few owners say they've worn the style for years, but one REI reviewer reported that the mesh lining crumbled on an older North Face jacket of similar construction.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Seam-sealed DryVent 2L keeps out wind and steady rain
- Inexpensive for a name-brand waterproof shell
- Deep, zippered hand pockets
- Adjustable 3-piece hood and a storm flap over the zip
- Wide size range, up to 5X
- Recycled face fabric and lining, with a non-PFC DWR

**Cons**

- At 600 g, it's heavy for a backpacking shell
- Poor breathability, with no pit zips
- No stow pocket, and it packs bulky
- Elastic cuffs with no adjustment
- Hood coverage is minimal in heavy rain

## Who Should Buy This

The Antora suits people who need one jacket for the commute, travel and the occasional day hike in light rain, and who care about cost more than grams. It also works for car campers and trail walkers who want a no-fuss shell that looks fine in town. Thru-hikers and ultralight backpackers should look elsewhere. A shell that carries 600 g, vents poorly and packs bulky will cost them comfort and pack space every day, and a lighter, better-ventilated jacket is worth the extra money.

## Verdict

The Antora is a decent everyday rain jacket and a poor ultralight one. It keeps weather out at a fair price, but weight, bulk and poor breathability rule it out for serious backpacking. Buy it for town and easy trails, and choose a purpose-built trail shell for anything longer.

## Overview

The Ultralight Pack Liner is a roll-top waterproof sack cut to the interior shape of a backpack, sold in three sizes that together cover packs from 30 to 100 liters. It's aimed at hikers who want a purpose-built dry layer inside a pack that isn't itself waterproof. The trade-off is simple: it's neater and tougher than the improvised alternative, and it costs several times as much.

## Performance

The liner's main selling point is shape. Where a compactor bag or a loose trash sack bunches up and collapses into whatever space the pack leaves it, Osprey cuts this one as a rectangle sized to the interior of its target pack. Gear goes in as it would into the pack itself, with fewer dead corners and less fighting a slick bag to get the last item in.

A hiker who took the Medium on an eight-day trip inside a 68-liter Osprey Kyte reported that it fit well, rolled and sealed easily, and slid in and out of the pack without snagging. The same reviewer said it held up through the trip. That matters, because the usual failure mode for a liner is a snag on a zipper or a pack's frame sheet, and 40-denier nylon is a good deal more resistant to that than the thin plastic it replaces.

The construction follows the standard liner playbook: waterproof-coated fabric with sealed seams, closed by rolling the top down. A roll-top is only as good as the rolls, so the practical advice is to fold at least three turns and buckle or clip the ends down. Osprey is explicit that the liner is not intended for prolonged submersion. It is a rain-and-splash layer, built for a soaked pack, a stream crossing that goes badly, or a night of rain pooling against the back panel, not a packraft-grade dry bag.

Weight is modest rather than record-setting. The Small comes in at 91 g, the Medium at 116 g and the Large at 152 g. That is meaningful for a pack-protection accessory but not remarkable next to a sheet of plastic, and the owner above said plainly that on a tight budget a black garbage bag would have been the alternative, with the liner's extra convenience being the case for paying more.

The materials are an unusual selling point in this category. The fabric is GRS-certified recycled nylon, bluesign-approved, and the DWR treatments are made without PFAS. That doesn't change how it performs in rain, but it is a real point of difference from the typical coated nylon sack.

## Choosing a Version

The three sizes differ only in dimensions and capacity, and Osprey sizes them by pack volume rather than by torso or any fit measurement.

- **Small (30–50 L packs):** 36 L of capacity and 47 x 31 x 26 cm. The right choice for a ultralight pack of 40 liters or so, where the liner is the whole dry system. At 91 g it's the lightest of the three.
- **Medium (50–70 L packs):** 61 L of capacity. The size most weekend and long-distance hikers using conventional packs will want. Its dimensions run 24 x 14.5 x 12.2 inches, and it fits packs around 65 liters well.
- **Large (70–100 L packs):** 86 L of capacity. This is for expedition-size loads, winter kits and guides' packs. It is also the heaviest at 152 g and the most expensive, so it only makes sense if the pack genuinely falls in that range.

Buy by the pack's actual capacity. A liner that is too small leaves gear stranded outside it. One that's too large leaves excess fabric to bunch and roll down into the top of the pack. Hikers right on a boundary, such as a 50-liter pack, should size up only if they routinely stuff the pack to its limit.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Rectangular cut sits flush inside the pack and packs efficiently
- 40-denier nylon is more snag-resistant than a trash bag
- Easy to roll and seal, and easy to load and unload
- Three sizes cover 30–100 L packs
- Recycled, bluesign-approved fabric and PFAS-free DWR
- Weighs 91–152 g

**Cons**

- Costs $42.50–$52.50, a large premium over a plastic bag
- Not designed for prolonged submersion
- Roll-top closure takes some care to seal, and the pack's contents stay dry only as long as the rolls hold
- Several colors and sizes have repeatedly sold out at retailers

## Who Should Buy This

This suits hikers who carry a non-waterproof pack through wet climates and want a durable, reusable liner that stays tidy over a season. It makes particular sense for someone who stops often, since a pack that keeps its shape inside the liner is easier to dig through. Thru-hikers on the Appalachian Trail or in the Pacific Northwest, where a pack spends days damp, are the obvious audience.

Anyone counting every dollar, or hiking mostly dry routes, will do just as well with a heavy-duty trash compactor bag. Those hauling gear on boats or through serious water should skip it for a true dry bag rated for submersion.

## Verdict

The Osprey Ultralight Pack Liner is a well-shaped, durable and responsibly made dry layer in three useful sizes. It earns its place for hikers who value the neater packing and robust fabric, but it can't justify its price on weight or waterproofing alone, which is why it falls short of a standout score.

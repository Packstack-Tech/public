## Overview

The ENO Atlas Hammock Straps are the standard-issue suspension system that ships with (or gets bought alongside) most ENO hammocks — a pair of daisy-chain webbing straps that let you hang a hammock between two trees without tying a single knot. They're aimed squarely at car campers, backyard hangers, festival-goers, and casual weekend backpackers who want dead-simple setup and don't mind a few extra ounces to get it. If you're chasing base-weight numbers for a thru-hike, these aren't really built with you in mind — but that's not really who ENO designed them for.

## Performance

**Setup and ease of use.** This is where the Atlas straps earn their reputation. Each strap is a daisy chain of 15 sewn loops, so hanging your hammock is just a matter of wrapping the strap around a tree, clipping your hammock's carabiner into whichever loop gets you the height and tension you want, and you're done. <cite index="14-3,14-4">People use these on gear demonstrations because they require little to no explanation, and beginners pick them up quickly.</cite> One long-time hammock camper on Hammock Forums summed it up well: <cite index="17-9,17-10">"they are pretty adjustable, but my only complaint is that they are pretty bulky and heavy... Definately lighter alternatives, but they are very user friendly and very adjustable."</cite> That's basically the whole review of these straps in a nutshell — easy and secure, at the cost of some bulk.

**Material and stretch.** The 1000D polyfilament webbing is the standout feature versus ENO's cheaper Slap Strap line, which uses nylon. Nylon webbing stretches under load overnight, meaning you often wake up closer to the ground than you started. The Atlas straps' no-stretch polyfilament (ENO compares it to seatbelt material) avoids that problem entirely, and it's a big part of why reviewers consistently call this ENO's most reliable strap for a set-and-forget hang.

**Weight capacity and durability.** Rated to 400 lbs combined (200 lbs per strap), and users well over that per-strap threshold report no issues. On Hammock Forums, a user weighing over 300 lbs reported <cite index="17-7,17-8">using the Atlas straps on one of his hammocks with "no issues with stretching or supporting my weight."</cite> That's reassuring if you're a bigger hiker or just want a wide margin of safety.

**Weight and packed size, realistically.** ENO lists these at 9 oz / 255 g for the pair, though independent testing has found actual weight running a bit higher — one reviewer measured <cite index="14-1">318 g (11.2 oz) for the straps alone, and 331 g (11.7 oz) with both straps in the stuff sack</cite>. Either way, that's noticeably heavier than ENO's own Helios Ultralight straps, which weigh in around 4.3 oz per pair — roughly half. If you're backpacking and counting grams, that difference matters over a multi-day trip.

**Tree-friendliness and length.** At 1.5" tapering to 0.75" wide, the webbing is wide enough at the tree-contact point to avoid the bark damage that thin cord or rope causes — an increasingly important consideration as more parks restrict or ban hammocking due to tree damage from careless setups. At 9 feet per strap, though, you're limited on tree spacing; if you regularly hang between trees more than about 12-13 feet apart, you'll want the Atlas XL (13.5 ft, 13 oz) instead, or ENO's separate Atlas EXT extension straps.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros:**

- No-stretch polyfilament webbing means no re-tightening in the middle of the night
- Dead-simple daisy-chain design — no knots, minimal learning curve
- 30 total attachment points give a wide range of height and tension adjustment
- 400 lb capacity with real-world reports of holding up well beyond ENO's own rated limits
- Reflective stitching is a nice touch for finding your straps in the dark
- Wide, tree-friendly webbing

**Cons:**

- At 9-11+ oz for the pair, meaningfully heavier than ultralight-oriented options like ENO's own Helios straps
- Bulkier packed size than minimalist strap systems — several users specifically flagged bulk as their main complaint
- 9 ft length is limiting if your trees are widely spaced; you may need the XL version
- Not the cheapest strap option — the simpler Slap Straps undercut it on price

## Who Should Buy This

The Atlas straps make the most sense for car campers, backyard/park hangers, festival use, and anyone prioritizing foolproof setup and no-stretch reliability over shaving ounces. If you're a beginner hanging your first hammock, this is a genuinely good, low-frustration starting point. Thru-hikers and ounce-counters, on the other hand, will likely be happier with ENO's Helios line or a lighter third-party whoopie-sling-and-tree-strap combo — you'll save several ounces without giving up much in the way of durability.

## Verdict

The Atlas straps do exactly what ENO designed them to do: make hanging a hammock nearly idiot-proof, with webbing that won't stretch out on you overnight. They're not the pick for anyone optimizing pack weight, but for casual and car-camping use, they're a dependable, well-built piece of gear. I'd call this a 7/10 — excellent at its intended job, just not the right tool if grams are your main concern.

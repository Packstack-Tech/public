## Overview

The REI Co-op Rainier is the store's entry-level hiking shell: a 2.5-layer recycled ripstop nylon jacket with pit zips, an adjustable hood, and a left pocket that doubles as its stuff sack. It is built for drizzly day hikes, commutes, and the occasional weekend trip. The trade-off is plain. At 320 g it costs little, but it is neither the lightest shell on the trail nor the most durable.

## Performance

**Weather protection.** The Rainier does its best work in light to moderate rain. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers found the PFC-free DWR beaded water well, but said the jacket performed best in moderate storms and wasn't the pick for massive ones. Better Trail's reviewer rated it as sufficient for a couple of hours of steady rain, with fully taped seams backing up the shell. Wind is where the jacket shines. Reviewers repeatedly single out its wind blocking, and REI rates it windproof to 60 mph. That makes it a useful cold-morning layer even when the sky is dry.

**Breathability.** This is a 2.5-layer jacket, and it behaves like one. Pit zips dump heat quickly, which Outdoor Gear Lab counted among its main reasons to buy. Even so, a reviewer at Worthy called it not the most breathable shell on the market. Some owners on REI's site report a clammy fleece after sustained climbing, even with the vents open. Plan on opening the zips early and often.

**Comfort and fit.** The cut has room for a midlayer and doesn't look bulky over one. Articulated elbows give good range of motion for scrambling and reaching overhead. The hood was updated and now covers the face without eating into peripheral vision, and the visor keeps rain off the eyes. Against bare skin, the coated interior has the plasticky feel common to 2.5-layer jackets at this price. Testers also noted a scratchy patch at the chin. The brushed collar lining softens things when the jacket is zipped to the top.

**Weight and packability.** The jacket packs into its own left pocket: turn the pocket inside out, stuff the jacket in, and zip it shut. It is easy to stash, and owners call it a handy travel layer. Outdoor Gear Lab was blunt on weight, though. It isn't the lightest jacket in the lineup, and the Rainier isn't its recommendation for gram-counting thru-hikers. Better Trail's reviewer noted the face fabric is heavier than that of ultralight shells like the Black Diamond Fineline or Outdoor Research Helium. That extra material buys a little more durability, at a cost in grams.

**Durability.** This is the Rainier's weak point. Better Trail's reviewer said plainly that anyone who prioritizes longevity should look elsewhere. Several long-term owners on REI's site describe the interior coating flaking off at the collar, cuffs, and hood after a few years, leaving flakes on clothing and hair. Others report a few years of trouble-free use. Treat the jacket as a well-priced consumable. Don't expect it to last a decade.

**Features.** The adjustment list is generous for the money: hook-and-loop cuffs, a hem cinch, and two hood adjustments. YKK zippers and mesh-lined pockets round out the package. One owner of an earlier REI shell noted that the interior elastic at the wrists was dropped in this design. REI also describes the current fabric as softer and quieter than previous versions.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Strong wind protection and decent rain performance for light to moderate weather
- Pit zips, a visored hood, and a cuff-and-hem adjustment set that punches above the price
- Fully taped seams
- Stuffs into its own pocket for easy stowing
- Comfortable fit over a midlayer, with good range of motion
- Frequently discounted; it has dropped to about $50

**Cons**

- At 320 g, heavier than purpose-built ultralight shells
- Breathability is only average during hard efforts
- Coated interior feels clammy against bare skin, with some chin scratchiness
- Reports of interior coating flaking after a few years
- Loses out to premium shells in sustained, heavy rain

## Who Should Buy This

The Rainier suits day hikers, weekend backpackers in mild climates, travelers, and anyone who needs a dependable shell for commutes and dog walks without spending $200 or more. It also works as a backup layer for people who rarely see weather. Thru-hikers, gram-counters, and anyone expecting days of sustained rain should look elsewhere. A lighter shell or a more durable, more breathable one will serve them better.

## Verdict

The Rainier is a competent budget shell that does what its price promises: it blocks wind, sheds light to moderate rain, and vents reasonably well. It is too heavy for ultralight loadouts and too short-lived for hard use. Buy it on sale for casual hiking and travel, and pass on it for long-distance trips.

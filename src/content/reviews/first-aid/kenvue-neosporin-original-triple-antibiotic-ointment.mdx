## Overview

Neosporin Original is the triple-antibiotic ointment you probably already have in your medicine cabinet — a petrolatum-based salve combining bacitracin zinc, neomycin sulfate, and polymyxin B sulfate to fight infection in minor cuts, scrapes, and burns. It's not a piece of "gear" in the traditional sense, but it shows up in more backpacking first aid kits than almost anything else, so it's worth evaluating the same way you'd evaluate a stove or a pack: is the standard 1 oz tube actually the right call for the trail, or is there a smarter way to carry this stuff?

## Performance

As a wound-care ointment, Neosporin does what it says on the tube. <cite index="1-2">The formula features HeliDerm Technology, which creates a nourishing environment that helps skin stay hydrated as it heals.</cite> In practice that translates to a soft, easy-to-spread ointment that doesn't sting going on — a real plus if you're treating a raw blister or a gravel-rash scrape on a kid (or a stubborn adult who hates any excuse to stop hiking). The petrolatum base also does double duty as a basic moisture barrier, which matters more on trail than it does at home, since dry, cracked skin around a wound in a dusty, sweaty environment heals slower and re-opens more easily.

Where it gets more interesting is the debate around whether you need the antibiotic properties at all. Wilderness medicine folks and several thru-hiker write-ups point out that thorough wound irrigation and cleaning matters more for infection prevention than the ointment itself — one pharmacist-authored piece for The Trek still recommends it as a first aid kit staple, noting you should <cite index="29-1">"include an antibiotic ointment (such as Neosporin or Bacitracin) for cuts and blister care."</cite> Meanwhile another Trek contributor was blunter about it, admitting <cite index="28-1">"technically you don't 'need' antibiotic ointment for minor cuts"</cite> but still carries it because it helps when he can't easily wash a wound out on trail. That's the honest consensus: it's a nice-to-have layer of protection and comfort, not a substitute for cleaning the wound first.

The bigger practical issue for backpackers isn't efficacy, it's packaging. A full 1 oz tube is way more product than you'll use on even a multi-week trip — most of what I've seen recommended by long-distance hikers is to either buy the small foil single-use packets (the kind that show up in packaged first aid kits) or decant a dime-sized amount into a tiny squeeze bottle or even a bit of foil, and leave the rest of the tube at home. The Hiking Life's minimalist first aid list, compiled by a hiker who's covered more trail than almost anyone, specifically calls for a <cite index="23-3">"Triple Antibiotic Ointment – Sample size tube"</cite> rather than the full-size version. At 28.3 g, the full tube weighs more than plenty of entire minimalist first aid kits combined, so this is one of those items where repackaging is basically mandatory if you actually care about pack weight.

One more thing worth flagging: neomycin, one of the three active ingredients, is a well-documented cause of allergic contact dermatitis in a meaningful chunk of the population. If you've never used Neosporin before, it's worth doing a small test patch at home rather than discovering a rash reaction for the first time on day 4 of a trip with no way to identify what's causing it. It's also explicitly not meant for deep or puncture wounds, animal bites, or anything beyond minor cuts and scrapes — for anything more serious you're looking at wound closure strips, irrigation, and probably evacuation, not ointment.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros:**

- No-sting formula that's genuinely easy to apply to raw skin, blisters, or scraped-up trail falls
- Doctor-recommended and widely available — you can restock in literally any gas station or pharmacy along a trail town resupply
- Petrolatum base doubles as a basic moisture barrier for dry/cracked skin
- Cheap, and a little goes a long way

**Cons:**

- Full 1 oz tube is overkill for backpacking — you're carrying way more product (and weight) than you need
- Neomycin can cause allergic contact dermatitis in some users; not something you want to discover mid-trip
- Not appropriate for deep wounds, punctures, or animal bites — limited to minor cuts, scrapes, and burns
- Some wilderness medicine sources argue the antibiotic component adds less value than proper wound cleaning and irrigation

## Who Should Buy This

If you already keep a first aid kit stocked with the basics, Neosporin Original is a reasonable, no-fuss choice for treating minor cuts, scrapes, and blisters on trail — but buy the small single-use packets, or decant a small amount from the tube, rather than tossing the whole 1 oz tube in your pack. It's a good fit for anyone who wants extra peace of mind on wounds they can't easily wash out with soap and water, especially on multi-day trips where handwashing is inconsistent.

## Verdict

Neosporin Original works as advertised and is genuinely useful in a trail first aid kit, but the full-size tube is the wrong format for anyone counting grams — repackage a small amount and leave the rest at home. I'd rate it a 7/10: solid, trusted, and inexpensive, docked a couple points because the standard packaging works against the exact audience most likely to be reading this review.

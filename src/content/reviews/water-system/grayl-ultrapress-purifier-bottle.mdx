## Overview

The GRAYL UltraPress is a press-style bottle that turns questionable water into drinkable water in one push, with no hoses, bags, batteries or waiting. It is a true purifier, so it handles viruses as well as bacteria and protozoa. The trade-off is a 16.9 oz capacity and a cartridge rated for 150 liters, which is generous for a weekend and thin for a thru-hike.

## Performance

**Speed and effort.** The method resembles a French press. Fill the outer shell to the line, seat the inner container, which is threaded to the cartridge, and push down until it bottoms out. GRAYL claims 10 seconds, and reviewers land close to that. Treeline Review clocked it at under eight seconds on clean tap water. Testers at Outdoor Prolink found it took longer than advertised and needed real hand strength, and a clogged cartridge slows it further. Off the Atlas reported that the wide pressing pad on the UltraPress is a clear improvement over the older Ultralight model, whose lid dug into the palm.

**Protection.** Because the cartridge combines electroadsorption with ultra-powdered activated carbon, the UltraPress goes beyond a typical hollow-fiber squeeze filter. It treats viruses, which matters for international travel and for trails downstream of heavy human or livestock traffic. The carbon also knocks down chemicals, pesticides, heavy metals and the silty, swampy taste that makes some backcountry water hard to swallow. A hunter writing for GearJunkie described a source that had turned out yellow, gritty water even after filtering, and reported that the press cleaned up both the look and the taste.

**Carry and durability.** The bottle slides into most side pockets and bike cages. Treeline Review's testers turned it upside down in a pack without leaks, and an Outdoor Prolink reviewer who tossed it into a work pack daily for a month found no scratches or dents. GRAYL says it survives 10-foot drops onto concrete. A flip-out handle lets it clip to a pack. A pre-installed one-way valve allows electrolyte or drink mix in the bottle, though reviewers note that mixes call for more thorough cleaning.

**Where it falls short.** Capacity is the obvious limit. In a dry stretch, a half-liter bottle means stopping to press and decant several times. Climbers and long-distance hikers who use it commonly pair it with a second bottle and treat water in batches. Cartridge life is the second issue. Section Hiker's Philip Werner rates it as better for day hikes, fishing trips and shorter outings than for extended backpacking trips, citing the 150-liter limit and replacement cost. Replacement cartridges run about $25, which works out to roughly 17 cents per liter, and the bottle itself retails around $90. Weight is a third point. At 309 g, it is heavy beside a squeeze filter and a pair of soft bottles, and reviewers with ultralight leanings call it heavy for what is essentially a water bottle.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- No setup, no priming, no backflushing, and no chemical wait time
- True purification that covers viruses, plus carbon that improves taste and reduces chemicals and heavy metals
- Pack-pocket and bike-cage friendly, and reported leakproof in transit
- Rugged, with a 10-year warranty on the bottle
- Direct drinking from the bottle and a one-way valve for drink mixes

**Cons**

- 16.9 oz per press makes loading up at a dry stretch slow
- 300-press cartridge is short for thru-hiking, and replacements cost about $25
- Heavy compared with squeeze filters
- Takes real effort to press, more so as the cartridge clogs
- Premium price for the bottle

## Who Should Buy This

Weekend backpackers, section hikers, day hikers, trail runners, cyclists and international travelers who want one object that is both water bottle and purifier. It also suits anyone drinking from sketchy sources abroad, where viruses are a concern and a squeeze filter alone won't cover them. Thru-hikers and anyone moving through long dry sections should look at a squeeze filter such as a Sawyer or a BeFree paired with larger soft bottles. Those setups are lighter, carry more water, and are cheaper to run over thousands of miles.

## Verdict

The UltraPress is among the easiest ways to get purified water on a short trip, and it is built to take abuse. It becomes a poor fit once trips run long, because capacity, weight and cartridge cost all work against it. Recommended for weekend and travel use, and a pass for long-distance thru-hikes.

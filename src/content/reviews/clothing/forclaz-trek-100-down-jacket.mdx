## Overview

The Forclaz Trek 100 is Decathlon's budget-friendly, packable down jacket aimed squarely at backpackers and travelers who want real 800-fill-power down without paying Arc'teryx prices. <cite index="14-2">Many hikers have discovered it as an alternative to jackets like the Mountain Hardwear Ghost Whisperer 2, which costs more than four times as much.</cite> It has since been rebranded as the MT100, but the specs are essentially identical — so if you're cross-referencing reviews, know that everything written about the MT100 applies here.

## Performance

### Warmth

The rated 23°F–41°F range is credible in real-world use — but context matters. <cite index="2-5">When temps dropped into the low 40s in Alaska's Caines Head State Park, it was all the warmth one tester needed over a base layer.</cite> Push below freezing and you'll need to layer. <cite index="8-24,8-25">Below 32°F you'll want to start layering both under with a base or mid-layer and a shell over the top — one tester wore it as a mid-layer with a base layer and a ski shell in 27°F temperatures and was very comfortable.</cite>

<cite index="4-17">
  The 800 fill power stands out because most higher-end jackets that easily cost
  2–3 times as much come with about the same fill power.
</cite>
<cite index="10-1">
  Non-hydrophobic 800-fill down performs like down no matter what garment it's
  packaged into, and it works just as well as it would in a jacket four times as
  expensive.
</cite>
The key word there is *non-hydrophobic* — more on that under wet weather.

### Packability

<cite index="2-6">
  The Trek 100 scrunches down into its own pocket to the size of a football.
</cite>
<cite index="13-19">
  It's one of the most packable options in its class, rolling down to the size
  of a Nalgene.
</cite>
That's legitimately impressive for an insulated jacket at this weight.

### Wet-Weather Performance

This is where you need to manage expectations. The down is not hydrophobic-treated, and while the 15-denier outer carries a DWR finish, <cite index="12-1">the DWR-treated shell does a below-average job against precipitation.</cite> In a controlled shower test, <cite index="18-24">the Forclaz soaked through a bit at the top of the zipper, but otherwise stayed dry — no small feat for a jacket that's merely labeled "weather-resistant."</cite> However, <cite index="18-27">after the test, it soaked through at the seams surrounding both pockets within roughly 20 minutes of drying.</cite> Bottom line: trust it for brief light showers, not for sustained rain. Carry a shell.

### Breathability & Active Use

<cite index="12-5,12-6,12-7">
  During testing, the jacket stayed comfortable during moderate movement in cool
  conditions, but started to trap heat quickly when the pace picked up or when
  climbing. There are no features to dump heat — no pit zips, mesh panels, or
  two-way zipper.
</cite>
<cite index="12-8">
  Leaving the hem cinch loose helps a bit with airflow, but once you start
  sweating, you unzip or shed the puffer entirely.
</cite>
This is a belay jacket and a camp jacket, not a hiking jacket.

### Durability & Zipper

The 15-denier polyamide shell punches above its price point in abrasion resistance. <cite index="10-9">Feathers stayed in and seams stayed tight over a year of use.</cite> The weak link is the main zipper. <cite index="10-6,10-7,10-8">Thanks to a burly face fabric this jacket would have scored higher in durability if it weren't for a dinky main zipper — testers found it hard to use especially in the cold and prone to separating, though it was fixed easily with a multitool.</cite> It's a known issue and worth flagging: cold, gloved hands will curse it.

### Fit & Sizing

Sizing runs consistently small. <cite index="3-31,3-32">By all accounts the jacket comes up small — one reviewer who normally wears a large or medium in coats ordered an XL, which was the correct decision.</cite> <cite index="8-21,8-22">Going with a larger size if in doubt is recommended; if you plan to layer with multiple layers, you may even want to size up two sizes.</cite>

<cite index="13-15">
  The jacket features a slim, athletic fit with a longer hem length and deep,
  zippered side pockets that remain accessible even when layering.
</cite>
That longer hem is a genuine win — it won't ride up under a pack hipbelt the way
shorter cuts tend to.

One ergonomic quirk: <cite index="1-9">the neck area goes up high, which sometimes creates rubbing and friction on the lips or chin.</cite> <cite index="2-12">The cuffs can also feel a bit too tight, especially if you're wearing a watch.</cite> Neither is a dealbreaker, but worth knowing before you buy.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 800-fill-power duck down at a price point that routinely undercuts competitors by 3–4x
- RDS-certified fill — ethically sourced and independently verified
- Packs into its own pocket to roughly Nalgene size
- Longer hem stays in place under a pack; doesn't expose your lower back
- Extended sleeve baffles wrap over the wrist for down coverage even under gloves
- Seams and baffles hold up well over extended use with minimal feather blowout
- 15-denier nylon shell is surprisingly durable for this price

**Cons**

- Main zipper is stiff, finicky in the cold, and prone to separating
- Down is non-hydrophobic — wet conditions significantly degrade performance
- No ventilation features; overheating while moving is a real concern
- Runs 1–2 sizes small; ordering the right size requires attention
- High collar can chafe chin and lips, especially for taller-necked or bearded wearers
- DWR treatment is only adequate, not reliable in sustained precipitation

## Who Should Buy This

The Trek 100 is ideal for weight-conscious backpackers and travelers on a real budget who understand layering. If you're using it as a mid-layer or a static-camp layer on shoulder-season or three-season trips, it's hard to argue with the value. <cite index="6-17,6-18,6-19">It might be the perfect backpacking jacket when layers are essential. When used properly as a layering piece it works well — you won't be comfortable going out in 20-degree weather with only this jacket, but if you have other layers, it will.</cite> Skip it if you hike in consistently wet climates, need a standalone piece in sub-freezing conditions, or plan to move fast and need to vent heat.

## Verdict

The Trek 100 delivers a straightforward value proposition: <cite index="12-27,12-28">it's earned a cult following for good reason — a warm, versatile, 800-fill down jacket that delivers incredible performance for the cost.</cite> The zipper is a legitimate frustration and the lack of hydrophobic down means you'll need a shell companion in mixed weather, but neither of those flaws is unusual at this price point. For a budget-conscious backpacker who already owns a rain jacket and understands that this is a layering piece, not an all-weather shell, it's one of the shrewdest $80–100 you can spend on insulation. I give it a **7.5/10**.

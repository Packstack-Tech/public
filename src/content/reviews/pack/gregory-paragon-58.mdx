## Overview

The Gregory Paragon 58 is a 58-liter, frame-supported backpacking pack for hikers who want a lighter carry without giving up padding. It weighs between 1,624 and 1,798 g depending on size, which puts it in the lightweight tier but well short of ultralight. The trade-off is plain: owners get a pack that is comfortable from the first mile and easy to pack, and they pay for it in grams and in a back panel that runs warm.

## Performance

**Comfort and carry.** Comfort is the Paragon's calling card, and reviewers agree on it. One Appalachian Trail tester logged roughly 200 miles in the pack and called it one of the few packs tested with no break-in period. The same tester carried a seven-day load and found it superbly comfortable. Others point to the generous padding in the shoulder straps, hipbelt and back panel. The perimeter frame moves weight to the hips, and the pack's sub-4-lb weight feels lighter on the body than on the scale. A reviewer who has owned Baltoro packs said it carried 40 lb of gear comfortably all day.

**Load limits.** Gregory rates the pack to 50 lb, but most testers stop short of that. Winter overnights at about 40 lb did not overload the suspension. Another reviewer reports that comfort starts to fade past 40 lb. A third found the pack balanced at 35 lb. For most trips the practical ceiling is the low 40s. That is more than enough for a base weight in the teens and a few days of food, but not for a long carry in bear-canister country with winter kit.

**Ventilation.** This is the most consistent complaint. Reviewers who tested the pack in warm conditions found the back panel vents less well than Gregory's marketing suggests, and one called it a step down from earlier Gregory packs. Heavy sweaters should expect a damp back on summer climbs. Testers with trail-running-style cooling needs should look at tensioned-mesh designs instead.

**Access and organization.** The full-length side zipper is the feature owners praise most after comfort. It lets hikers pull a stove or a midlayer from the middle of the pack without unloading it from the top. The bottom sleeping bag compartment has a removable divider. There are six external pockets, including an oversized front stretch mesh pocket, dual side pockets and zippered hipbelt pockets. The sunglasses stow on the shoulder strap is an unusual touch at this weight.

**Fit and stability.** Torso length and hipbelt fit are adjustable. A 5'10", 155-lb tester found the medium/large size right on target. Side compression straps lock down partial loads, and the pack rides close to the body, which helps on off-trail scrambling. The flip side is limited outward expansion. One tester judged the pack better suited to weekend trips than to long hauls when it is stuffed full.

**Durability.** This is where the weight savings show. The body uses 100D and 210D nylon, and reviewers describe the fabric as light and not especially tear-resistant. Several mention thin compression and adjustment webbing as a weak link. The sleeping bag divider uses toggles rather than zippers, which saves weight but weakens the divider, and the hipbelt pocket zippers don't run as smoothly as they should. One long-distance tester raised durability concerns after a winter of use, including a seam separation. It is unclear whether that was a design flaw or a sewing flaw. Thru-hikers planning a 2,000-mile effort should treat the pack as a consumable. Weekend and section hikers are unlikely to wear it out.

## Choosing a Version

The two versions differ in torso range and weight, not in features. The Men's Small/Medium weighs 1,624 g, and the Men's (medium/large) weighs 1,798 g, so the larger pack adds about 175 g for longer back panel and harness components. Both share the same 58-liter volume, frame and pocket layout. Pick by torso length, not by weight. A hiker who falls between sizes should choose the size that puts the hipbelt on the hip bones, because the Paragon's comfort depends on that fit. Testers with torsos around 17–21 inches report that the larger size fits well.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Padded and comfortable from the first trip, with no break-in
- Full-length side zip and bottom compartment make packing and unpacking easy
- Handles loads in the 35–40 lb range without complaint, with a 50-lb rating
- Rain cover included, plus sunglasses stow and hipbelt pockets
- Lighter than most padded packs of this capacity at 1,624–1,798 g

**Cons**

- Back panel runs warm in hot weather
- Thin straps and light fabric raise long-term durability questions
- Comfort drops off past about 40 lb for some users
- Limited outward expansion when fully stuffed
- Not light enough for ultralight base weights; well over 3.5 lb

## Who Should Buy This

The Paragon 58 suits weekend and section hikers, and first-time multi-day backpackers carrying a conventional kit of 25–40 lb. It also suits anyone moving down from a 5-lb pack who wants a big weight cut without giving up cushioning. Hikers who want a true ultralight setup should look elsewhere. Packs like the Osprey Exos 58, listed at 2.94 lb, are lighter and ventilate better. So are frameless and minimally framed packs from cottage makers. Hot-climate hikers and thru-hikers who need a pack to last for thousands of miles should also pass.

## Verdict

The Paragon 58 is a comfortable, well-organized lightweight pack that carries moderate loads very well and runs warm. It is a good choice for shorter trips and conventional kits, and a poor one for ultralight purists and long-distance thru-hikers. Buy it for the carry and the side zip, and expect the lighter fabric to wear faster than that of a heavier pack.

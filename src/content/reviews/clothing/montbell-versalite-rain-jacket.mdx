## Overview

The Montbell Versalite is a 2.5-layer waterproof-breathable shell that weighs 181 g and still carries pit zips, a adjustable hood, hand pockets and adjustable cuffs and hem. It is built for hikers who want a real rain jacket without the weight penalty, and it has been a staple on thru-hiker packing lists for years. The trade-off is a thin 15D face fabric that rewards careful use and punishes bushwhacking.

## Performance

**Weather protection.** The 20,000 mm rating sits well above the 10,000 mm threshold commonly used to call a fabric waterproof, and the fully taped seams and weather-resistant zippers mean leaks are unlikely at the construction level. The weak point of any jacket this light is the outer fabric: once the DWR finish wears or saturates, the face fabric holds water against the membrane and the jacket feels clammy. Reviewers who have tested Versalite jackets across the line, from CleverHiker to Section Hiker, describe the same pattern. Short summer storms and intermittent showers are well within its range. All-day coastal drizzle and sustained downpours are where wrists and neckline start to take on moisture, and where a heavier three-layer shell pulls ahead. DWR needs refreshing after a year or two of regular use, and Section Hiker's Philip Werner recommends a wash-in or spray-on treatment to restore it.

**Breathability and venting.** This is where the Versalite earns its reputation. Owners consistently credit the long underarm zips, rather than the membrane alone, for keeping the jacket tolerable on climbs. Retailer specs list the zips at 12 inches, long enough to dump heat in a hurry, and the zippers are waterproof so they can stay partly open in light rain. Montbell's later Windstopper-based Versalite revision claims 30,000 mm of water resistance and 43,000 g/m² of breathability, a clear step beyond the Super Hydro Breeze fabric. Even so, the pit zips do much of the work on either version, and the jacket vents better than most shells in its weight class.

**Fit and features.** The cut is trim through the body with narrow sleeves, and the hem doesn't ride up when arms are raised. One owner described the size as roomy enough to layer over a down puffy. The two-way adjustable hood cinches down around the face, and the hook-and-loop cuffs seal out wind better than the plain elastic found on many ultralight shells. Eight-inch hand pockets are zippered, and a two-inch longer tail covers the hips and seat. Reflective zipper pulls add some visibility in low light. Bring a hat with a brim if hood coverage matters, since the hood has no stiffened visor.

**Durability.** Testers repeatedly describe durability as better than a roughly 6-ounce jacket has any right to offer, but the face fabric is still thin. The 15D nylon is heavier than the 7D and 10D fabrics found on some competing ultralight shells, which gives it a little extra margin against snags and pack-strap abrasion. Off-trail travel through brush, scrambling on abrasive rock and heavy-pack hauling are still the situations that cause problems, and reviewers recommend a sturdier jacket for that kind of travel.

**Packability.** The jacket stuffs into its included sack to roughly the size of an apple, and it slips into a pants pocket or hip-belt pouch without complaint. At 181 g, it's about the weight of two protein bars, and it won't be noticed in a pack even on a day when it never comes out.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 181 g with a full feature set rather than a stripped-down design
- Long pit zips deliver vent-on-demand breathability that membrane-only shells lack
- Fully taped seams and weather-resistant zippers
- Adjustable hood, hook-and-loop cuffs and an adjustable hem
- Packs to apple size, with a stuff sack included
- 15D face fabric is a little tougher than the thinnest ultralight shells

**Cons**

- Wets out in sustained, heavy rain, particularly at wrists and neckline
- DWR needs periodic restoration
- Not a jacket for brush, scrambling or rough pack use
- Hood lacks a stiffened brim
- Later Windstopper versions of the Versalite carry higher claimed waterproof and breathability numbers

## Who Should Buy This

The Versalite suits thru-hikers and fast-and-light backpackers who treat rain gear as an insurance policy: three-season trips where storms are short, the pack is light and every ounce is counted. It also works as a mountain-travel shell for runners and alpine hikers who want pit zips without carrying a 300 g jacket. Anyone who expects days of unbroken rain, regularly travels off trail, or hikes in a place like the Pacific Northwest, coastal Alaska or Scotland, where wet weather is the default, should look at a more durable three-layer shell and accept the weight.

## Verdict

The Versalite remains one of the most complete rain jackets under 200 g, with ventilation and adjustability that most competitors skip. It's a strong choice for light, fast trips in changeable weather and a poor one for sustained storms or rough terrain. Buy it as a lightweight shell, and treat it as one.

## Overview

The Tarptent Rainbow is the one-person-plus version of the company's long-running Double Rainbow. It uses a single arched pole to hold up a hybrid single-wall shelter: a mesh body with a built-in fly. At 907 g, it's built for hikers who want a cabin-sized interior without carrying a cabin's weight. The trade-off is the usual single-wall one: the pitch has to be tight, and condensation needs managing.

## Performance

**Space.** Room is the Rainbow's selling point. The 88-inch floor is long enough that Tarptent says it suits hikers up to 6 ft 6 in. One 6 ft 5 in reviewer who tested it with a NeoAir XL pad found room to spare, with his pack and shoes fitting in the vestibule. The 42-inch peak gives sitting headroom that most solo shelters can't match. An owner who stepped up from a bivy compared the change to moving into a palace. The "1+" rating is also meaningful. The floor takes two standard-width pads, which is why the tent shows up in recommendations for solo hikers who sometimes bring a dog or a partner.

**Weight.** At 907 g, the Rainbow is light for its size, though it isn't the lightest solo tent on the market. Switchback Travel measured an earlier Rainbow at 2 lb 5.7 oz (about 1,070 g). The current 32 oz figure is a clear step down. A true ultralight freestanding tent can undercut it, but few of those give a 7-foot floor and sit-up headroom.

**Pitching.** The Rainbow pitches fly-first, so the interior stays dry if the tent goes up or comes down in rain. It needs six stakes. A single arch pole and a minimal structure mean the tent relies on stake tension to get its volume. Owners describe it as easy to pitch but say a drum-tight result takes practice. Reviewers of the Double Rainbow, which has the same design, say the layout is less intuitive than it looks. They also say the stake points at the corners and vestibules matter more than they would on a conventional dome.

**Freestanding mode.** Trekking poles aren't needed for normal use. They are only needed to make the tent freestanding. Tarptent's kit uses poles of at least 45 in / 115 cm across the corners. CleverHiker's testers, reviewing the two-person model, called this the standout feature. They said it's handy for waiting out light rain or for pitching where stakes won't hold. Hikers with shorter poles or no poles can't use it.

**Weather and condensation.** The Rainbow is rated for three seasons. The bathtub floor can be raised to close up the tent in cold weather and lowered for airflow in warm weather. One owner reported winter use with better condensation control than expected from a single-wall design. The built-in fly can't be separated from the body, which is a limitation. Tarptent sells an optional condensation liner for hikers who camp in damp, cold conditions. Reviewers of the Double Rainbow note that the tent needs seam sealing before use, and Tarptent lists a seam-sealing kit with the Rainbow.

**Durability.** The 20D polyester fly is thin. The 30D nylon floor is a reasonable middle ground for a tent this light. CleverHiker reports that the Double Rainbow design has long been a favorite on the Appalachian Trail and Pacific Crest Trail and can last a full thru-hike with careful use. Those tests were of the larger model. The Rainbow uses a thinner fly fabric than the 30D fabric reported for the Double Rainbow, so it calls for a little more care around brush and wind.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 88-inch floor and 42-inch peak height give tall hikers real room
- 907 g is light for the interior volume
- Optional freestanding setup with trekking poles
- Fly-first pitch keeps the inside dry in rain
- Costs $269–$284 direct, well below most cottage-industry competitors
- Floor fits two standard pads when a dog or partner joins

**Cons**

- Needs good stake tension and some practice for a taut pitch
- Single-wall design brings condensation risk in humid or cold conditions
- Freestanding mode requires poles of at least 45 in
- Fly is integrated and can't be separated from the body
- Seam sealing is needed before use
- Thinner fly fabric than the two-person model

## Who Should Buy This

The Rainbow suits tall solo hikers, side-sleepers on wide pads, and thru-hikers who want room to sit up, sort gear and wait out a storm without a heavy shelter. It also works for hikers who occasionally share the tent with a dog or friend. Hikers who use no trekking poles, or whose poles are too short, should look at a fully freestanding tent. Anyone who needs a minimum weight at any cost should look at a smaller shelter. So should anyone camping above treeline in high wind or snow, since the Rainbow is a three-season design.

## Verdict

The Rainbow gives hikers more interior room per gram and per dollar than almost any other solo tent. Its quirks are the ones common to single-wall tents, and they're manageable. Tall hikers and comfort-minded thru-hikers should buy it.

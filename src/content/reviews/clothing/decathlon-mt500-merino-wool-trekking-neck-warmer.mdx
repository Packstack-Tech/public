## Overview

The MT500 is Decathlon's merino neck tube, sold under its Simond mountain sub-brand. It is a simple, seamless loop of 89% merino and 11% polyamide that works as a neck warmer, headband, beanie or light balaclava. The trade-off is plain: it is a little less plush than premium merino tubes, and in return it costs about £12.99 and is built to survive being stuffed into a hip-belt pocket for months.

## Performance

**Warmth.** At 155 g/m², the MT500 sits above the gossamer merino buffs that weigh barely more than a pair of socks' worth of fabric. It is a shoulder-season and winter-day tube rather than a summer sun-and-dust cover. Decathlon rates it for cool weather, and owner scores track that: reviewers heading to high altitude, including one who bought it for a month-long Annapurna expedition, report it does what it claims. Others are more measured. One reviewer who wears it under a motorcycle helmet gave warmth three stars out of five, which is a fair reminder that a single layer of knit merino is a wind-baffle, not a down collar. In a stiff breeze on a ridge, expect to pair it with a hood or shell collar.

**Odor and moisture.** This is where merino earns its keep. Owners on Decathlon's UK site describe a tube that doesn't pick up smell after a lot of sweating, wicks well and dries quickly. For a thru-hiker who wears one piece around the neck for days between washes, that matters more than any spec line.

**Durability.** The polyamide sheath around each merino fiber is the main design idea here. Decathlon says the yarn structure protects the wool against friction and cuts, and owners report no noticeable pilling so far. A traveler who compared Decathlon and Icebreaker merino over a long trip found the neck warmer among the Decathlon items that held up well, even though he found other pieces in the range rougher and less refined. The one durability complaint worth noting is mild stretching after a year of near-daily use under a helmet. A UK MTB site named the MT500 its all-round value pick, noting the reinforced yarn suits a layer that gets crammed into pockets and washed regularly.

**Comfort.** The 18.5-micron wool is on the fine side, and Decathlon states the fabric does not itch. Reviews back that up for most users. The polyamide content and denser knit make it feel a notch less luxurious than 100% merino tubes from Icebreaker or Buff, a difference most people will notice only in side-by-side comparison. Sensitive-skinned hikers who already find merino borderline should try it on first.

**Versatility.** Ten wearing styles is a catalog number; in practice, three or four get used. Neck tube, pulled up over the chin and nose in wind, a pulled-down headband, and a pulled-over-the-ears beanie cover nearly every real trail scenario. Diameter is adjustable by scrunching rather than any cord or toggle, so it stays simple and has nothing to snag or break.

**Value.** Decathlon's UK listing shows a 4.8 average across roughly 3,800 ratings. About 3,320 of those are five-star, and only 35 are one-star. Few pieces of merino gear at any price have a satisfaction profile like that.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 54 g, about the weight of a few energy gels
- 155 g/m² fabric is warmer than ultralight merino buffs
- Polyamide-sheathed yarn resists pilling and abrasion
- Merino odor control holds up through multi-day sweaty use
- Ten wearing styles with no cords, toggles or seams to fail
- £12.99 undercuts most merino tubes by a wide margin
- Non-mulesed South African wool, according to Decathlon

**Cons**

- Slightly less soft than 100% merino tubes from premium brands
- Not windproof or waterproof; a single layer of knit
- Some owners report light stretching after a year of heavy use
- Too warm for hot-weather use, and heavier than the thinnest merino buffs
- Decathlon's retail footprint makes it harder to buy in North America

## Who Should Buy This

Backpackers and thru-hikers who want a durable merino tube for cold mornings, camp evenings and shoulder-season trips will get everything they need here. It also suits ski tourers, winter day hikers and anyone who wears a neck warmer under a helmet. Hikers who want the lightest possible option for warm-weather trips should look at a thinner merino or synthetic buff. Anyone planning long exposed stretches in sub-freezing wind should treat this as a base layer for the neck and add a fleece or insulated hood. Those who want the softest possible merino next to skin can pay more for 100% merino.

## Verdict

The MT500 is a small, cheap, hard-wearing piece of kit that does the job of tubes costing two or three times as much. It trades a bit of silkiness for abrasion resistance and warmth, and for most hikers that is the right trade. It earns a place in any cold-weather pack.

## Overview

The Forclaz MT900 is Decathlon's one-person trekking dome: a Y-shaped pole, a silicone-coated polyester fly and a mesh-heavy inner, all for 1,300 g with pegs and poles. It borrows the semi-freestanding layout of far pricier cottage-brand solo tents and sells for a fraction of the cost. The trade-off is a tight floor and a wind rating that suits sheltered camps better than exposed ridgelines.

## Performance

**Weight and pack size.** At 1,300 g, the MT900 is no minimalist shelter. Live for the Outdoors weighed its test sample at 1,302 g and called it neither the lightest nor the heaviest solo tent around. That is about 380 g more than Decathlon's own 920 g MT900 trekking-pole tarp tent. In return, the dome needs no trekking poles and comes with a full mesh-and-fabric inner. The whole kit compresses to a 39 x 11 x 10 cm bundle, roughly the volume of a one-litre bottle plus change, and it slides down the side of a pack without a fight.

**Pitching.** The Y-shaped pole splits into three feet, which gives the tent its semi-freestanding character: the structure holds its shape before staking, though the vestibule and corners still need pegs. Reviewers consistently describe the setup as quick and intuitive, even for first-time users. As with most dome tents, the inner pitches first, so the fabric stays exposed to rain until the fly goes on.

**Weather protection.** The 2,000 mm fly and 3,000 mm floor are sensible numbers for a tent in this weight class. Live for the Outdoors found the waterproofing solid and the tent comfortable in heavy-ish downpours. Active-Traveller's testers pitched it in Scottish rain and wind and found it as robust as the semi-freestanding Y-pole tents it takes after. The 60 km/h rating is the limit, though. Dome tents with light poles are not the first choice on an open col, and the MT900 leaves little margin above a stiff gale. Think valleys, forests and shoulder-season campsites, not summit camps.

**Space and livability.** Length is fine: the 210 cm inner accommodates tall sleepers, and 95 cm of inside height means a hiker can sit upright and change clothes without wrestling the ceiling. Width is the compromise. Floor width narrows from 70 cm at the hips to 50 cm at the feet, and the tent's tapered shape leaves little room beyond a standard-width pad. Live for the Outdoors found the narrow width cramped, and Active-Traveller made the same point about the sleeping area. Wide pads and restless sleepers will push against the walls.

**The vestibule.** This is where the MT900 earns its keep. The side-entry porch swallows a 70 L pack with room to spare, and Active-Traveller called it large and incredibly useful. Wet boots, a stove and a pack all fit under cover without crowding the inner.

**Ventilation.** Adjustable vents in the fly feed air into large mesh panels covering the upper half of the inner. That keeps condensation in check in mild conditions. It also means the tent is draughty when temperatures drop, a typical downside of mesh-heavy inners.

**Durability.** Aluminum poles keep the tent sturdy for its weight. Active-Traveller noted that it will not match tents costing around three times as much for finish and fabric quality, but saw no sign it would fall apart. Decathlon backs the tent with a five-year warranty.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 1,300 g with poles, pegs and guylines, competitive for a freestanding-style double-wall solo tent
- Packs to 4.2 L
- Quick, simple pitch
- Vestibule takes a 70 L pack
- 210 cm inner length and 95 cm height suit tall hikers
- Sells for around £140–160 in the UK in published reviews, far below name-brand rivals
- Five-year warranty

**Cons**

- Narrow floor, tapering to 50 cm at the feet
- 60 km/h wind rating limits it to sheltered sites
- Mesh-heavy inner runs cold in cold or windy weather
- Inner-first pitch exposes the inner in the rain
- 380 g heavier than Decathlon's own trekking-pole tarp tent
- 2,000 mm fly is adequate, not generous

## Who Should Buy This

Budget-minded backpackers, bikepackers and first-time thru-hikers who want a conventional, fast-pitching solo shelter with real headroom and a roomy porch for gear. It also makes a sound backup or shoulder-season tent. Hikers who sleep on wide pads, camp above treeline in exposed terrain, or count every gram should look at a trekking-pole shelter, including Decathlon's own tarp tent, or spend more for a stronger pole structure.

## Verdict

The MT900 delivers most of what a three-season solo hiker needs at about a third of the cost of the big-name competition. The narrow floor and modest wind tolerance keep it from being an all-rounder, but for sheltered camps and tight budgets it is hard to beat. A solid recommendation for valley and woodland hikers.

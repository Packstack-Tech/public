## Overview

The Evernew FP Mat 125 is a five-panel, 5 mm closed-cell polyethylene mat made in Japan. It stretches 125 cm, enough for head to hips, and folds down to a 25 cm-wide slab. It is built for fastpackers and minimalists who care more about durability and pack versatility than comfort, and the trade-off is blunt: almost no cushioning and little insulation.

## Performance

**Weight.** The 200 g claim is conservative. Owners who put the mat on a scale report 181 g, 188 g, 189 g and 193 g. A thru-hiker who carried one on the Pacific Crest Trail measured 181 g. That is not the lightest foam on the market. Japanese cottage-industry pads such as Yama to Michi's Minimalist Pad weigh a fraction of it. But the FP Mat offers more length and a stiffer, tougher foam than those ultra-thin options.

**Comfort.** Reviewers reach for the same comparison: a tatami mat. The foam is dense, with only a slight spring, and the surface is rough rather than plush. At 5 mm it takes the edge off gravel and small stones, which is more than its looks suggest, but it does nothing for a flat floor or for hip bones. Side sleepers will notice. One PCT hiker who used it for five months said the lack of cushioning made it hard to recover fully after 30 to 40 km days, and he added a second pad under his hips. Another reviewer noted that the mat does not feel like a conventional sleeping pad at all.

**Warmth.** Insulation is modest. Evernew's own distributor describes it as a booster under an air mat, and owners agree it works best as a summer or three-season supplement. It is not a standalone winter pad. Because the folds are not fully crushed in manufacturing, the mat is designed to avoid cold spots, and it holds no water, so it can go straight onto damp ground.

**Durability.** This is the mat's real strength. The foam resists scuffing, tearing and punctures, and one thru-hiker described using it with no care at all for the whole PCT, including as a trail-side seat on rough ground. An owner with 20 to 30 nights logged reports only minor scratches. Cleaning is a wipe-down, since there is no fabric to hold mud.

**Versatility.** The five panels pack to roughly 2.5 cm thick, which slides into the back of a frameless pack as a stiff back panel. Owners use it as a sit pad, a camp-ground floor for gear, and a puncture shield under an inflatable. That last use is popular: if the air mat fails, the FP Mat still gives a usable sleeping surface. The fold lines fall every 25 cm, so some owners trim a panel with scissors to save weight. One cut a 125 cm mat to 100 cm and weighed it at 142 g. Evernew also sells a four-panel 100 cm version at 160 g.

**Quirks.** The foam builds static electricity, which a reviewer in Japan described as startling. At 125 cm the mat leaves legs and feet on the ground, which is workable with a pack under the calves but less so for tall hikers. A 5'5" owner found the length acceptable for its job as a puncture barrier.

The mat sells for about ¥3,000 to ¥3,600 in Japan, which is cheap for a pad this durable.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Measured weights run 181–193 g, under the 200 g claim
- Very durable foam that tolerates rough ground, rocks and neglect
- Folds to about 2.5 cm thick, so it fits inside a pack and works as a frameless-pack back panel
- Works as a seat, a gear mat, or a puncture shield and backup under an air mat
- Absorbs no water and wipes clean
- Can be trimmed along its fold lines to save weight
- Inexpensive

**Cons**

- Little cushioning; hips and shoulders will feel the ground
- Limited insulation; cold ground comes through
- 125 cm leaves legs and feet uncovered
- Heavier than the thinnest ultralight foam pads
- Builds static electricity
- Not a comfortable all-night, multi-month sleep surface on its own

## Who Should Buy This

The FP Mat suits fastpackers and short-trip minimalists who sleep warm in summer, hikers who want a back panel and a sleeping pad in one piece of kit, and anyone who runs an inflatable and wants a lightweight insurance layer under it. It also pairs well with a short torso-length pad for hikers who want a firmer surface under their legs. Side sleepers, cold sleepers and thru-hikers who rely on a full night's recovery should look at a thicker foam pad or an insulated air mat instead.

## Verdict

The FP Mat 125 does a narrow job well: it is a tough, packable, cheap piece of foam that serves as a seat, a back panel, a ground cloth and a backup pad for around 190 g. It is a poor choice as a primary bed for anyone who values comfort. Buy it as a multi-use component, not as a mattress.

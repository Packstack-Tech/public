## Overview

The AMG™ 105 is Mountain Hardwear's flagship expedition hauler — a 105-liter beast built for guides and climbers who need to move tents, ropes, stoves, and multiple days of food up big mountains. This isn't an ultralight backpacking pack; it's a load hauler for Denali, Rainier, and similar high-altitude objectives where you need serious capacity and a suspension that won't fold under 60-80+ pound loads. If your trips involve a rope team rather than a resupply box, this is the category the AMG 105 was built for.

## Performance

The headline feature here is the pivoting hip belt, and it's not just marketing fluff. Multiple guide-reported tests back this up — one <cite index="1-6">tester carried 80-pound loads comfortably, thanks to a memory-foam-like lining on the shoulder straps and pivoting hipbelt, and a strong U-shaped stay made of aircraft-grade aluminum</cite> during a Rainier guiding trip in rough weather. That's the kind of load figure that separates a true expedition pack from a beefed-up backpacking pack, and it tracks with what you'd expect from the 7000-series aluminum frame doing the heavy lifting while the pivot lets the belt track your hips through scrambling and postholing.

Durability is where this pack seems to earn its keep. The same Rainier field test found the pack <cite index="1-7">withstood plenty of abrasion, carrying 100 pounds of rocks up and down a slope, and emerged without a single scuff</cite>. That lines up with the fabric choice — the 840D carbonate-coated nylon base is genuinely burly, and the pack is popular enough on Denali that guides note <cite index="4-2">it's probably the most popular backpack on Denali</cite>. When a pack becomes the default choice among people who guide the same mountain dozens of times a season, that's a stronger endorsement than any spec sheet.

Organization is intentionally minimal. RMI guides who use it professionally describe it as combining <cite index="3-2">user-friendly simplicity with the massive capacity needed for extended expeditions</cite>, with <cite index="3-4">intuitive exterior pockets that make it incredibly easy to pack "anything and everything," ensuring immediate access to critical gear when conditions change</cite>. Translation: don't expect a zippered organizer suite. You get big, glove-friendly external storage and lash points, not a laptop sleeve. That's exactly right for its intended use — fumbling with small zippers at altitude in gloves is a real problem, and this pack is designed around avoiding that.

Fit is the recurring sore spot in owner feedback. Retailer-aggregated reviews note that <cite index="4-4">some smaller-framed individuals reported issues with the hip belt not tightening enough, which was a significant drawback</cite>, even though the overall consensus was that <cite index="4-5">the backpack is considered one of the best large-capacity options for mountaineering and backpacking, though the sizing may need to be addressed to improve inclusivity</cite>. If you're on the smaller end of the S/M size range, try this on with a loaded pack before committing — the pivoting mechanism needs a reasonably built hip to actually pivot around.

One buyer's experience stands out as an outlier worth flagging: a Backcountry reviewer who'd been excited about the pack ultimately found it <cite index="4-9">clearly was made with cheap suboptimal materials making it unreliable</cite>. That's a single data point against a broader chorus of durability praise, but it's a reminder that quality control on any gear-intensive pack can vary, and it's worth inspecting stitching and buckles closely on arrival.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Pivoting hip belt genuinely improves comfort and stability under heavy loads (tested to 80+ lbs)
- 840D nylon base and burly ripstop body shrug off abrasion from rocks, crampons, and rough handling
- Guide-vetted: a go-to pack for RMI and other outfits running Denali and Rainier trips
- Simple, glove-friendly access beats fussy organization when you're at altitude
- 7000-series aluminum frame keeps weight reasonable for a pack this size (~5.1–5.2 lbs)

**Cons**

- Hip belt fit runs into trouble for smaller-framed climbers — try before you buy if you're at the edge of the size range
- Minimal internal organization means no dedicated pockets for smaller items
- At $425 and over 5 lbs, it's a big investment and a big pack for anyone not actually hauling expedition loads
- One reported instance of inconsistent build quality — worth inspecting on arrival
- Overkill (and then some) for anything shorter than a multi-day, gear-heavy trip

## Who Should Buy This

This pack is for climbers and guides tackling multi-day, gear-intensive objectives — think Denali expeditions, extended Rainier routes, or any trip where you're hauling group gear like ropes, stoves, and shared shelter alongside your own kit. If you're a thru-hiker or ultralight backpacker, look elsewhere; the AMG 105's weight and capacity are wasted on a trip where you're counting grams of food, not pounds of rope.

## Verdict

The AMG 105 does what expedition packs are supposed to do: carry brutal loads without falling apart, and do it with a suspension that actually earns the "dynamic" label on the pivoting hip belt. It's not for everyone, and smaller-framed climbers should test the fit carefully, but for big-mountain hauling it's held up as a guide favorite for good reason. I'd put it at a 7.8/10 — excellent at its actual job, with the caveat that its actual job is a narrow one.

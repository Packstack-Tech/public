## Overview

The Salewa Litetrek II is a 2-person, 3-season double-wall tent built specifically for alpine trekking — the kind of environment where the weather can turn mean faster than you can finish a hot drink. <cite index="6-3">It sits in a niche between tents with breezy mesh interiors and full alpine tents</cite>, which really does describe its personality well. This is not a gram-counting shelter for fair-weather desert routes; it's a capable, freestanding workhorse for the mountains that trades some weight against durability and wind stability. It's also the more affordable sibling to Salewa's own Litetrek Pro II — a relationship worth understanding before you hand over your money.

## Performance

### Weather Protection

<cite index="5-7,5-8">
  Wind-tunnel tested for stability at 90 km/h by the Technical University of
  Munich, the semi-geodesic, double-wall construction puts two layers of fabric
  between you and the elements, enabling full use of space while ensuring an
  optimal balance of weather protection and ventilation.
</cite>
That TU Munich test isn't just marketing copy — users who've taken this shelter
into serious mountain environments have confirmed it.
<cite index="7-15,7-16,7-17">
  The tent proved itself in the Caucasus — lightweight, compact, and wind
  resistant
</cite>
— and one reviewer took it all the way to Mount Kazbek, reporting it
<cite index="7-21">"worked great in strong winds."</cite>

The fly uses <cite index="5-11">50D ripstop polyester PU-coated to a 3,000 mm water column, while the floor is 70D ripstop nylon rated to 5,000 mm.</cite> The 3,000 mm fly rating is solid for 3-season use — not bombproof, but adequate for sustained rain. The 50D fabric on the fly is notably heavier and more abrasion-resistant than the 20D nylon used on the Litetrek Pro II, which is part of why the II carries more grams. <cite index="6-9,6-10">The fabrics have higher-than-average waterproof ratings and are more durable than those found on many three-season tents, and the zips are stronger than those on the lightest tents.</cite>

### Setup

<cite index="5-13">
  Salewa's 3S Speed Set Up Solution uses short, flat sleeves and hooks to allow
  a fast, effective one-pitch setup.
</cite>
In practice, users consistently note how quick the tent goes up —
<cite index="7-18">
  the very quick installation is a genuine advantage in the rain.
</cite>
<cite index="5-9,5-10">
  The 100% self-supporting design means you can pitch on rocky terrain where
  there's not enough space to use guy lines and pegs, though anchor points can
  be extended for more flexibility on snowy terrain.
</cite>
That freestanding capability is genuinely useful in the alpine world, where
you're often threading a needle between boulders for a campsite.

<cite index="5-14">
  On good weather nights, you can also pitch the flysheet alone
</cite>
for a lighter, more ventilated setup — a welcome option that many competitors in
this category don't offer.

### Interior Space & Comfort

<cite index="6-6,6-7,6-8">
  The 2.52 m² floor area is small but typical for an alpine tent. Headroom is
  most generous near the door and narrows towards the foot — it's a little
  cramped for two people changing clothes, and large people might find it a
  touch narrow, but for short trips or smaller people it's sufficient.
</cite>
At 1.0 m peak height, sitting up is fine; anything resembling a leisurely
stretch requires negotiation. Solo use is comfortable; two people with full kit
is workable but snug.

The vestibule at 0.56 m² is on the tight side — don't expect to cook a three-course meal under it. <cite index="5-15">The vestibule is integrated into the inner tent but can be extended as required,</cite> which adds a little versatility. The tent also has only a single entrance, which means one sleeper has to climb over the other to get out at 2 a.m. — a minor but real annoyance on multi-night trips with two people.

### Ventilation & Condensation

<cite index="5-12">
  Ventilation is regulated via a rear panel with zippers that can be operated
  from both inside and outside, allowing you to adjust airflow and manage
  humidity.
</cite>
That inside/outside operation is a nice touch. That said, condensation with two
occupants is a known reality — a French forum user with the non-Pro version
noted
<cite index="11-2">
  some condensation with two inside, but considered it normal.
</cite>
Double-wall tents in this category all face that trade-off; the Litetrek II
manages it reasonably well without sacrificing weather protection.

### Durability

<cite index="6-9">
  The fabrics have higher-than-average waterproof ratings and are more durable
  than those found on many three-season tents.
</cite>
One concern raised in user forums is pole breakage —
<cite index="12-1">
  there are reports of snapped poles, which put at least some prospective buyers
  off.
</cite>
The 8.5 mm 7001 T6 aluminium poles are a reasonable spec, but this is worth
monitoring if you plan to push the tent into sustained high-wind situations
repeatedly. A spare pole sleeve and some tent repair tape in your kit is
sensible insurance.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Fully self-supporting — pitches on rock, snow, or anywhere you can find a flat spot
- Wind-tunnel validated at 90 km/h; genuinely reassuring in alpine conditions
- Heavier-duty 50D fly and 70D floor are more abrasion-resistant than most 3-season competitors
- Salewa's 3S system makes solo setup fast, even in bad weather
- Fly-only pitch option for fair-weather nights
- <cite index="6-12">
    Priced at the low end of its class, making it excellent value
  </cite>
  relative to its weather protection

**Cons**

- At 2,100 g minimum weight, it's noticeably heavier than top ultralight alternatives (e.g., MSR Hubba Hubba NX at ~1,720 g)
- Single entrance only — inconvenient for two-person use at night
- Vestibule at 0.56 m² is tight for gear storage and cooking
- Some forum reports of pole breakage under extreme conditions
- <cite index="4-10">The Litetrek Pro II is 320 g lighter</cite> for those
  willing to spend more

## Who Should Buy This

The Litetrek II is built for the mountain trekker who wants a freestanding, genuinely weather-capable 3-season shelter without spending Pro-tier money. <cite index="6-3,6-4">It sits in the niche between breezy mesh tents and full alpine tents, offering warmth and reassurance in strong winds at an affordable price.</cite> It's a strong pick for alpine routes in the European Alps, Scottish Highlands, or similar exposed terrain during the shoulder seasons. If you're a gram-counter chasing desert thru-hiking records, look elsewhere. If you're heading above the treeline where the weather is genuinely unpredictable, this tent earns its place on your back.

## Verdict

The Salewa Litetrek II makes a clear, honest bargain: more weight, more durability, more weather confidence, less money. <cite index="7-37">When considering durability and price, the cost-performance is strong compared to the Pro.</cite> The cramped vestibule and single door are real compromises for two-person use, and the weight puts it out of reach for strict ultralight kits — but for a solo alpine trekker or a pair willing to embrace cozy quarters, it's a dependable, well-built shelter that punches above its price point. I'd rate it **7/10**.

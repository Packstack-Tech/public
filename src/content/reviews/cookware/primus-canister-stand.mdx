## Overview

A canister-top stove puts a hot burner, a full pot and a few hundred grams of pressurized fuel on a very small footprint. The Primus Canister Stand widens that footprint with a foldable plastic tripod that fits on the canister's base. At 13 g it costs about as much weight as a spare lighter, and it suits anyone who cooks on rough ground or with pots larger than a mug. Its trade-off is that it's plastic, and a few owners have been unhappy with how well the base grips.

## Performance

**Stability.** The stand does one job: it turns a narrow canister into a wider-based tripod. That matters most with a stove that threads directly onto the canister, where the center of gravity sits high and a pot of water adds to it. Primus pitches the stand for large pots and uneven ground, and this is where it helps most. Tent vestibules, gravel bars and sloped campsites are all places where a bare canister stove can tip. Reviewers of canister stands in general make the same point: a tall, tippy stove on off-level ground is the norm at backcountry camps, not the exception.

**Weight and packing.** At 13 g, the stand adds almost nothing. Retailer listings for the Primus consistently single out its light weight as the top selling point, and its 76 x 66 mm footprint is small enough to slip into a pot beside a canister without taking up meaningful room. A Trailspace reviewer who uses MSR's stainless steel Universal Canister Stand notes it runs 10 to 15 g heavier than the plastic stands from Primus and Optimus. That reviewer also found the MSR too long to stow in some pot setups and a bit fiddly to stretch onto the canister. The Primus stand's folding design and small size avoid the packing complaint.

**Durability and grip.** The fiberglass-reinforced bio-plastic should hold up to normal use, though the same Trailspace reviewer guessed that stainless steel would be tougher and worth the extra cost and weight. Owner feedback on the Primus is mixed. Two buyers on a European outdoor retailer's site rated it 5/5 and praised the sturdy base. Two others rated it 1/5, with lightweight as the sole pro and the base as the complaint. Scores elsewhere run higher, in the 4 to 5 star range, but those pools are small, and many of the comments are about delivery rather than trail use. The takeaway is to check the fit on your own canister before you rely on it, particularly with off-brand canisters, since Primus promises compatibility with "most" other brands rather than all.

**Limits.** The stand attaches to the canister, so it doesn't help with remote-canister stoves that already have their own legs. Primus has also never offered a version for inverted canisters, a trick some winter stove users rely on. For cold-weather inverted use, this isn't the accessory.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 13 g, about half an ounce
- Folds small enough to pack inside a pot
- Fits Primus 100, 230 and 450 g canisters and most other brands
- Cheaper and lighter than metal stands
- Made in Sweden from 94% bio-based plastic and fiberglass

**Cons**

- Plastic is less durable than stainless steel alternatives
- Some owners report a poor base and have rated it 1/5
- Only helps stoves that attach directly to the canister
- Fit with non-Primus canisters is promised for "most" brands, not all
- Canister sold separately

## Who Should Buy This

This stand is for the ultralight hiker who cooks with a PocketRocket-style stove and wants insurance against a spilled dinner for the weight of a few sheets of paper. It particularly suits people who use 750 ml or larger pots, cook for two, or camp where flat ground is rare. Hikers who tend to boil water in a small mug on stable ground can skip it. Winter campers using inverted canisters, or anyone who wants a metal stand that will last for years, should look at a stainless option like MSR's, accepting the extra 10 to 15 g.

## Verdict

The Primus Canister Stand is a cheap, light fix for a real problem, and it gets the weight and packability right. The mixed reports on base performance keep it from an easy recommendation, so test the fit on your canister before a trip. For cooking with larger pots on uneven ground, it earns its 13 g.

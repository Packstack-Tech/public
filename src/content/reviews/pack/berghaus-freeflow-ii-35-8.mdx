## Overview

The Freeflow II 35+8 is Berghaus's take on a ventilated day-and-a-half pack, built around a rigid plastic framesheet and a mesh back panel that holds the bag off your spine. It's a long-running UK hillwalking staple rather than a thru-hiking pack, and it's aimed at day hikers, Munro-baggers, and casual scramblers who'd rather carry an extra half-pound of frame than deal with a sweaty back. If you're chasing a sub-2-lb base weight pack, this isn't it — but it's worth understanding what it does well before writing it off.

## Performance

The whole point of the Freeflow system is the gap it creates between the mesh panel and your back, and by most accounts it actually delivers on that. <cite index="14-6,14-7">Its firm frame keeps it off of your back, avoiding that cold sweaty area where most rucksacks rest on you.</cite> That's the pack's one unambiguous win — if a soaked back is your main complaint with backpacks, this addresses it directly.

The trade-off is stability. <cite index="1-8">Having little material in contact with your back helps with ventilation and cooling, but with heavier loads it can make the pack more unstable than others.</cite> That instability shows up specifically on technical terrain: multiple UKClimbing forum users reported that <cite index="7-1,7-2">the Freeflow helps stop your back getting drenched in sweat, but it really throws off your centre of gravity for scrambling and climbing, taking a while to get used to.</cite> Another climber put it more bluntly — the weight sits too far back and pulls you off balance compared to traditionally seated packs (per UKClimbing forum posts). If your hikes involve any scrambling, helmet use, or off-trail balance work, factor that in; one climber even noted the loaded hood was bulky enough behind their head that it interfered with looking up while wearing a climbing helmet, per UKHillwalking forum discussion.

Packing is the other recurring gripe. <cite index="11-9,11-10">The Freeflow features an incredibly sturdy frame in the form of a plastic framesheet — super strong and rigid so it'll support heavy weights, but its inability to bend means you'll struggle to pack this into a larger travel bag.</cite> Because the frame has a built-in curve to create the air gap, you're loading gear around a bend rather than straight down, which several owners flagged as fiddly — one forum poster went as far as comparing it to <cite index="7-15">"packing round a corner essentially."</cite> The framesheet also means the +8L expansion doesn't come from vertical compression — <cite index="8-8,8-9,8-10">the freeflow frame won't compress vertically since there's a coated metal hourglass skeleton attached to the backboard, though there's a little springy play; most of the +8 expansion comes from the full-length zipped side pockets instead.</cite>

On the organization side, it's a genuinely thoughtful daypack layout. <cite index="6-1,6-2,6-3">The main pocket capacity of 35 litres puts it in large-daypack territory rather than backpacking-pack territory — long and thin due to the Freeflow system, but big enough for a day's clothing, lunch, and emergency snacks, with an extra base pocket for waterproofs and quick-access items linked to the main compartment by a zipper.</cite> <cite index="6-4,6-5">The hood is large enough to take an SLR camera and reachable on the move, with an internal zippered pocket for valuables.</cite> Build quality on the strap retainers, D-rings, and compression webbing gets consistent praise — <cite index="6-6,6-7">closure is via a drawstring and toggle plus sturdy webbing compression straps, and it all feels well built with nice finishing touches.</cite>

Durability is where reports get genuinely mixed. Several long-term owners (via forums like The Windy Munro and UKHillwalking) reported years of trouble-free daily use, but there's a persistent thread of zip failures — one UKHillwalking poster said their <cite index="12-15">35+8 only lasted a year before they had to replace the zips after six months</cite>, and another mentioned a returned unit where <cite index="12-16">all the zips had gone and a strap broke after only one trip</cite>. That's not universal, but it's common enough across independent threads to flag as a real risk, especially on packs bought secondhand or well-used.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Genuinely effective ventilation — keeps your back noticeably drier than a standard panel-loader
- Sturdy, well-finished build with a full-length side zip, base access pocket, and camera-friendly hood
- Includes a proper rain cover with its own storage pocket
- +8L expansion via side pockets adds real flexibility for overnight or long-day loads

**Cons**

- At 1,450 g for 43L, it's roughly double the weight of comparable ultralight packs
- Rigid, curved framesheet makes packing more awkward than a straight-loading pack
- Doesn't compress vertically, so a half-empty pack stays bulky
- Shifts center of gravity backward — a real liability for scrambling, climbing, or uneven terrain
- Multiple independent reports of zip and strap failures within the first year of use

## Who Should Buy This

This is a pack for UK hillwalkers and day-trippers who run hot and prioritize a dry back over gram-counting — think Munro-baggers, mountain bikers, or casual multi-day walkers doing straightforward trail miles. It's a poor match for anyone doing technical scrambling, climbing approaches, or serious thru-hiking, where the rearward weight bias and lack of packability become real problems rather than minor quirks.

## Verdict

The Freeflow II 35+8 does exactly one thing well — keeping your back dry — and does it at the cost of weight, packability, and balance. For ultralight or thru-hiking use it's a hard pass, but as a budget-friendly ventilated daypack for hot-weather UK hillwalking, it's earned its long production run for a reason. I'd rate it a 5.5/10 for this audience specifically; bump that up a couple points if ventilation is your single biggest pack complaint and weight isn't.

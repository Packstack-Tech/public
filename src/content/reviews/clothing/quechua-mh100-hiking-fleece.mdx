## Overview
The Quechua MH100 is Decathlon's entry-level hiking fleece: a hoodless, pocketless, quarter-zip pullover in brushed 100% polyester. It has no wind panels, no stretch inserts and no marketing copy about proprietary fibers. In exchange it costs roughly the same as a bag of trail mix and a fuel canister, and it does the basic mid-layer job competently.

## Performance

**Warmth.** The 200 g/m² brushed knit is a classic lightweight fleece. SectionHiker's Philip Werner puts its useful range at roughly 50°F down to freezing when worn alone, and considerably lower once it sits under a wind shirt or rain shell. That matches how most owners use it: as an insulating layer for cool mornings, camp evenings and shoulder-season ridgelines. It is not a substitute for a puffy on a cold night. An Indiahikes trek leader who used one through peak-winter Himalayan trips still praised its warmth for the weight, though in that setting it was one layer in a stack.

**Breathability and moisture.** This is where reviewers agree the MH100 is weakest. Great British Adventure Club calls breathability its soft spot, and the Indiahikes review notes that it can feel sweaty on hard uphill sections. For moderate hiking it moves vapor well enough, and a trail tester wearing it as a base, mid and outer layer over a winter and spring reported that it kept them from feeling clammy on warmer days. The practical advice is the usual fleece advice: unzip early, or take it off before the climb rather than after.

**Wind and weather.** There is no wind resistance. The open knit passes breeze straight through, so it works best paired with a wind shirt or shell, which is how it earns its place in a layering system. Fleece does hold warmth when damp and dries quickly on the body, which makes it forgiving in drizzle.

**Fit and features.** The cut is slim and athletic. Werner found it runs about a half size small and suggested sizing up. Decathlon's free-returns policy, in his view, makes ordering two sizes a low-risk option. A tight-fitting stand-up collar seals out drafts at the neck, and the YKK zipper is quarter-length even though some spec sheets call it a half-zip. It has no pockets, no thumb loops and no drop tail, which keeps the pack weight down and the garment simple, but it also means cold hands have nowhere to go.

**Durability.** Werner described the sewing as impeccable, and the garment needs no special care to wash or dry. Longer-term owners are less uniformly glowing. The Indiahikes review reports pilling after several washes and some wear at the zipper and stitching with frequent use, and a UK review notes minor stitching issues after a few washes. At this price, that is a reasonable trade, though anyone expecting a decade of service from a $15 fleece will be disappointed.

**Weight.** At 270 g, the MH100 is not an ultralight garment. Werner weighed a men's XL at 10.2 oz (about 289 g). Premium grid fleeces and thin hoodies from cottage and mainstream brands run lighter and pack smaller, and a 100-weight fleece is lighter still. What the MH100 offers is dollars per gram of warmth, not grams per dollar of anything.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**
- Costs roughly $15–19 in the US and under a tenner in the UK, which makes it nearly disposable
- Soft, double-brushed fleece that retains warmth when damp
- Close-fitting collar and a reliable quarter-zip with venting
- Machine washable and quick drying, with no special care
- Layers cleanly under a wind shirt or rain jacket
- Clean construction, with sewing that testers praised on new garments

**Cons**
- Breathability falls short on sustained, sweaty climbs
- No wind resistance
- No pockets or thumb loops
- Runs small, with a slim cut that can feel restrictive on broader frames
- Pilling and zipper or stitch wear reported with heavy use
- At 270 g, heavier than many purpose-built lightweight fleeces

## Who Should Buy This
Budget-minded hikers, first-time backpackers, scout and Duke of Edinburgh groups, and anyone who wants a spare camp or sleep layer they will not mind losing or ruining. It also suits thru-hikers who want a cheap, replaceable insulating layer for cool-weather sections. Hikers who run hot, who want pockets, or who are counting every ounce of their base weight should look at lighter grid fleeces or a synthetic or down insulated layer. Anyone who wants a single garment that blocks wind should buy a hybrid piece instead.

## Verdict
The MH100 does not try to be anything more than a simple, warm, cheap fleece, and it largely succeeds. It is too heavy for the gram-counting crowd and too breathable-in-name-only for hard efforts, but as a $15 mid-layer or camp top it is hard to argue with. Buy it, size up if between sizes, and wear it under a shell.

## Overview

The Outdoor Research Helium Bivy is a minimalist, single-pole bivy sack built for solo hikers, bikepackers, and alpine climbers who want a backup shelter (or primary shelter on fair-weather trips) that disappears into the bottom of a pack. It's not trying to be a one-person tent — there's no vestibule, no real sitting-up room — but it does add a curved pole across the head end and a clamshell hood opening that make it noticeably more livable than a plain sack-style bivy.

## Performance

The headline number is the weight — right around a pound, depending on which source you check (Outdoor Research lists it slightly differently across product cycles, and independent testers have <cite index="10-9">clocked it at an impressively low weight of just 494g</cite> while another tester weighed a slightly earlier version at <cite index="13-1,13-9">very compact and light at ~476g</cite>). Either way, it's in the same weight class as a bivy-only shelter should be, and it packs down small enough to disappear into a hip belt pocket on a bikepacking rig.

The single-pole design is the biggest usability upgrade over older OR bivies. One reviewer who'd used the older two-pole Alpine Bivy noted <cite index="16-15">the Helium uses a single pole with no snaps and a minimum of bits</cite> — fewer parts to lose, and a faster pitch. That pole only spans the head end, though; <cite index="10-14">there is no pole for the foot end of the Helium Bivy, but Outdoor Research has added extra height to the volume in the toe end</cite>, and testers found that extra toe-box volume isn't hugely noticeable in practice. What the head pole does deliver is real headroom — instead of fabric draping across your face, <cite index="10-15">the end result of all of this pole-based structure is that you get considerably more headroom with the Helium Bivy</cite>.

Weather protection is where the Helium earns its price tag. The Pertex Shield Diamond Fuse fabric and taped seams held up well in testing: one reviewer found <cite index="11-1">fully taped seams and solid waterproof credentials are in place to stop a sudden shower ruining your night</cite>, and a Trailspace user who slept in it through 20 minutes of sprinkler runoff on a back lawn reported staying dry all night. Breathability is also better than you'd expect from a fully sealed shelter — multiple testers independently praised this, with one noting <cite index="11-2">the bag is also very breathable, even when the bivvy is completely sealed up</cite>.

That said, condensation is the recurring complaint across nearly every review I found, and it's worth taking seriously if you're considering this as a primary shelter. An REI reviewer who'd used it for two years summed it up bluntly: <cite index="14-2,14-3,14-4">I have been using this bivy on and off for two years now, primarily in the south-west. The weight and packability are outstanding. The issue I often have with it is condensation.</cite> Even reviewers who liked the bivy overall echoed this — <cite index="11-13">you will still see some moisture build up on the inside, as is always going to be the case with a bivy bag</cite>. It's a fundamental limitation of the category, not a flaw unique to this product, but it means the Helium is best suited to drier climates or shorter trips rather than damp, humid environments where you're sealed up every night.

Interior space is tight, which is the trade-off you're signing up for with any true bivy. One reviewer who tested it against other bivies for a group review was direct about it: <cite index="12-13">for a small weight penalty of 8 ounces, I am strongly considering purchasing the Miles Gear Uber Bivy for future trips because it is larger and from what I've read has next to zero condensation issues</cite>. If you're claustrophobic or plan to spend rainy days waiting out weather inside, this isn't the shelter for that — as one reviewer put it, <cite index="12-18">if forced to spend an entire day sheltering inside from rain, I would prefer a tent</cite>.

Durability seems solid for the weight class, though the 30D/40D fabrics are clearly built for weight savings over abuse resistance. One long-term user comparing it to older, heavier alpine bivies noted <cite index="13-9,13-10">the lighter weight comes at the expense of a much less robust construction, but that's just part of the trade off as you move to lighter and lighter</cite> gear — a fair characterization of where this bivy sits on the spectrum.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Genuinely sub-pound weight (roughly 448–494g depending on the source), competitive with the lightest bivies on the market
- Single-pole head hoop adds real headroom without much weight penalty
- Fully taped seams and Pertex Shield fabric provide legitimate rain protection
- Surprisingly breathable for a fully sealed shelter
- Fast to pitch — reviewers consistently cite setup in under a couple minutes
- Compact packed size, good for bikepacking frame bags or minimalist thru-hiking kits

**Cons**

- Condensation is a near-universal complaint, especially on multi-night trips or in humid climates
- No foot-end pole means the toe box, while roomier than a flat bivy, still isn't spacious
- Interior is tight — not a shelter you want to be stuck in during an all-day storm
- 30D/40D fabrics trade durability for weight; not built for rough alpine abuse
- At $200+ retail, it's a premium price for a shelter with inherently limited livability

## Who Should Buy This

This bivy makes sense for gram-counting thru-hikers, bikepackers who need something that fits in a small frame bag, and alpine climbers who want a minimalist emergency shelter for fast-and-light missions. It's also a smart pairing for hammock campers or as a backup/supplement to a tarp. It's a poor fit if you camp in consistently humid or wet climates, need to shelter in place for extended periods, or simply want more livable space — for those users, an ultralight solo tent will serve better despite the weight penalty.

## Verdict

The Helium Bivy delivers on its core promise: legitimate sub-pound weight, real weather protection, and better headroom than most bivies thanks to its single-pole design. Condensation and cramped interior space are the price of admission for going this light, and they're worth weighing seriously before you buy. I'd rate it a 7.2/10 — excellent at what it's designed to do, but a specialist tool rather than an all-around shelter.

## Overview

The Classic Thermal Merino Base Layer Crew, long sold as the Merino 250, is Smartwool's thickest base layer and a perennial fixture on shop walls from October to March. It is a 250 g/m² long-sleeve in 100% merino, cut slim and aimed at skiing, winter hiking and cold camps. The trade-off is familiar to anyone who has worn heavyweight wool: exceptional warmth and comfort in exchange for weight, slow drying and a fabric that does not love abuse.

## Performance

**Warmth.** At 250 g/m², this is the heaviest fabric Smartwool puts in a base layer, and Outdoor Gear Lab's testers ranked it among the warmest tops they had assessed, scoring it 9 out of 10 for warmth. Owners say the same thing in plainer terms. One reviewer reports wearing it at 18°F and staying warm throughout, and a backpacker who used the bottoms of the same fabric in the teens and twenties says it controlled moisture well. Several testers found themselves wearing it as a mid-layer, since it adds warmth without much bulk.

**Breathability and temperature range.** The interlock knit gives the fabric a double-faced, plush hand, and it breathes better than its thickness suggests. Outdoor Gear Lab scored breathability 7 out of 10, noting that warmth and breathability pull in opposite directions. That is the real limit here. A backpacker who tested the crew in Colorado and Utah with daytime highs in the 70s judged it overkill for most summer hiking and recommended a 100–150 g/m² layer for warm conditions. One Appalachian Trail thru-hiker reported wearing it for the whole trail, not just the cold sections, because it stayed comfortable, but that is the exception.

**Odor.** Merino's best trick holds up. Owners report wearing it two or three days in a row with hardly any smell, and the AT hiker found it wicked moisture without the funk synthetic shirts pick up. For multi-day winter trips where laundry is not an option, this is the main reason to choose wool.

**Drying speed.** Thick wool holds moisture. Outdoor Gear Lab gave it just 5 out of 10 for drying, the weakest mark in its scorecard. Wool does keep insulating when damp, which softens the problem in winter, but a sweat-soaked top will take a long time to dry overnight in a tent.

**Comfort and fit.** Comfort is where the crew earns its reputation. Reviewers on Zappos and REI describe it as soft and warm, including a few who normally find wool itchy. The cut is slim and follows the body, so layering over it is easy. One REI buyer, 5'10" and 160 lb, suggested sizing down for a snug base-layer feel and found a Small plenty long. Flatlock seams and the shoulder panels keep chafing under a pack strap to a minimum.

**Weight.** The catalog weight is 236 g (8.3 oz). Retailer listings for the current cut cite a 276 g (9.74 oz) garment weight, and a backcountry tester described it as nearly 10 oz, at the heavy end for a merino top. Either way, it is not a gram-counter's piece, but the weight buys real insulation, and heavier wool means a thicker fabric that insulates better.

**Durability.** Opinions split. Outdoor Gear Lab rated durability 7 out of 10 and argued the thick cut holds up on multi-pitch climbs and multi-day hut trips. The most critical REI review repeats a common complaint that the 150 and 250 base layers are not especially durable, and a long-time Zappos owner says a pair from more than a decade ago frayed. They still preferred the old neckline, which had thinner edging and less stitching than the current one. Treat it as a well-made wool garment, not a bombproof one: wash it gently, skip the dryer, and keep abrasive pack straps and hip-belt rubbing in mind.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Among the warmest base layers tested, with a wide comfort range for winter
- Soft interlock merino that wool-averse skin tolerates
- Excellent odor resistance for multi-day trips
- Flatlock seams and offset shoulder seams reduce chafing under a pack
- Slim cut layers cleanly and doubles as a light mid-layer

**Cons**

- Heavy for a merino top, at roughly 8–10 oz depending on size and version
- Slow to dry once soaked
- Too warm for most three-season hiking
- Durability complaints from some long-term owners
- $115–$125 list price, though discounts are common

## Who Should Buy This

This is a cold-weather top for winter hikers, skiers, climbers and shoulder-season campers who want a single warm layer that tolerates several days between washes. It suits people who run cold, or who will wear it to sleep in camp. Summer and three-season backpackers should look at 100–150 g/m² merino or a synthetic grid fleece instead, and so should anyone who sweats heavily and needs a shirt to dry in a couple of hours. Hikers rough on their gear should expect to replace it sooner than a synthetic.

## Verdict

The Classic Thermal Merino Crew remains one of the best-liked winter base layers because it does the basics well: it is warm, soft and stays clean. It is heavy, slow to dry and not the toughest wool shirt around. For cold-weather trips, it is an easy recommendation. For anything above freezing, buy something lighter.

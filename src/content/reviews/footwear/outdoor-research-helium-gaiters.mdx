## Overview

The Helium Gaiters are Outdoor Research's answer to a question ultralight hikers keep asking: how much waterproof leg coverage can fit in a pocket? The answer is a high gaiter, reaching to just below the knee, that weighs 139 g and packs to roughly the size of a pair of light gloves. The trade-off is the usual one for fabric this thin. It's excellent for mud, wet brush and shoulder-season slush, and it isn't built for sharp rock, crampon points or years of abuse.

## Performance

**Weather protection.** The fabric does what the name promises. Owners describe dry feet through driving rain, snow and tundra bog. One hiker on a 10-day fall trip in the Arctic reported the gaiters held up through sand, boulder scrambles and muddy quicksand without losing their seal. Another used them on a six-day trip in wet mountain weather and came away satisfied. The two-fabric construction is the reason. The 30D ripstop on the upper leg keeps weight and bulk down, while the heavier 420D PU-coated panel around the foot and ankle takes the abuse where debris and boot contact are worst.

**Breathability.** Testers at UK retailer Trek Scotland found breathability good, with no major clamminess whether the gaiters were worn over or under legwear. They also called the thin upper panel almost unnoticeable against the skin, more comfortable than most gaiters of this length they had worn. Owners consistently describe the gaiters as light enough to forget they're on. Wet gear also dries fast. The Arctic hiker simply hung them or laid them under the tent vestibule, and they were dry by morning.

**Fit and stay-put.** Fit is a strength. A ProView tester wore them with light waterproof boots in late-season snow and mud and found they stayed in place even when post-holing to the knee. The shock-cord top flexes on climbs without biting into the calf and stays up as the miles pile on. They work over boots and trail runners, and they clear Kahtoola Microspikes and similar traction aids without interference. SectionHiker notes they suit any footwear with an arched sole, which rules out flat-soled shoes. The hook-and-loop front closure and offset closures make left and right easy to tell apart and the gaiters easy to get on and off.

**Sizing.** Calf fit runs snug. One women's-model reviewer on REI couldn't close a small around an average calf and recommended sizing up, and the sizing chart deserves attention. At the other end, a 65-to-74-year-old owner in an XXL reported a snug fit over size 13 high-top boots.

**Durability.** This is the catch. SectionHiker's tester saw the interior coating abrade away after several months of use and wouldn't recommend the gaiters for hard-core use. The ProView tester said durability was good but warned it would be easy to overwork them on rocky ground or with crampons, a point Trek Scotland echoed: a nick from a crampon spike or an ice-axe adze will end the fabric's life quickly. One buyer reported a lace-hook clasp falling off after a single outing, though that appears to be an outlier among otherwise positive reports.

The Hypalon instep strap, which takes the most wear on any gaiter, is replaceable. Owners cite that as a reason to buy, since the strap is the usual failure point on long hikes. A review at one outlet singles out the lightweight strap as potentially vulnerable under the boot, so a spare strap is a sensible thing to carry on a long trip.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 139 g and pocket-sized, a fraction of a mountaineering gaiter
- Waterproof fabric that still breathes
- Stays put on boots and trail runners, even in deep snow
- Replaceable Hypalon instep strap
- Compatible with microspikes
- Quick to dry under a vestibule

**Cons**

- Thin fabric abrades, and the inner coating can wear through within months
- Not suited to crampons, ice axes or rough scrambling
- Calf fit runs snug, particularly in smaller sizes
- Requires footwear with an arched sole
- Instep strap is a wear point on long routes

## Who Should Buy This

Three-season backpackers and thru-hikers who want waterproof lower-leg protection for mud, wet brush, spring slush and stream-side trail, without the weight of a Gore-Tex gaiter. It suits hikers in boots or trail runners who want something to pull on when the weather turns and stash when it clears. Those who plan to wear crampons, bushwhack through granite or use one pair for years of daily abuse should look at Outdoor Research's heavier Crocodile or Rocky Mountain High gaiters instead. Hikers who only want to keep grit out of low trail runners can get by with a far lighter low gaiter.

## Verdict

The Helium Gaiters deliver real waterproof coverage at 139 g, and they stay put, breathe well and disappear in a pack. They are a light-use tool whose fabric won't take much punishment. For three-season hiking and non-technical winter walking, they're a smart buy. For anything involving metal points or sharp rock, they're the wrong gaiter.

## Performance

**As floss.** The 20-inch length is the standard pre-cut size for a full-mouth session: enough to use a fresh section between each tooth with some left over to wind around the fingers. That is the point of the format. There is no dispenser to crush in a hip belt pocket, no cutter blade, and no half-used spool collecting lint in a toiletry bag. Tear the packet, floss, and the packet and strand go into the trash bag. After a week of bars, jerky and freeze-dried dinners, the mint wax gives the mouth a modest reset, and the wax helps the strand slide between tight teeth without shredding.

The sealed packet also keeps each strand clean in a pack that spends its days in dirt, sweat and the occasional spilled electrolyte mix. Packaging is slightly bulkier than a rolled-up loose strand, but at 1.5 by 3.75 inches it tucks into a ditty bag or the corner of a first-aid kit without much notice.

**As repair thread.** This is where floss earns its reputation among thru-hikers. Gear-repair guides from The Trek, Outside and others recommend floss as a stand-in for thread, since nylon is stronger than standard sewing thread and shrugs off moisture. Pack straps, webbing, shoe stitching, mesh pockets and tent tie-outs are the usual candidates. The practical method is to thread a heavy needle, or a safety pin in a pinch, and use a backstitch or box stitch, then reinforce with a wrap of tape. Waxed floss is the preferred type, as the coating helps it hold knots and resist fraying.

A single 20-inch strand is a short length of thread, which is the main limitation. It suits a popped strap seam or a torn mesh pocket, not a rebuilt jacket. Hikers who want floss as a serious part of the repair kit usually carry a few packets, or wind a longer length from a roll around a needle card.

Floss also has a known weak spot. Thin, hard nylon can saw through lightweight shelter and pack fabrics under load, so it works best stitched through webbing or backed by a patch of repair tape rather than pulled directly through delicate silnylon or Dyneema.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- About 1 g per packet, effectively weightless
- Pre-cut 20-inch strands remove the need for a dispenser or cutter
- Sealed packets stay clean in a dirty pack
- Strong nylon doubles as emergency thread for webbing, straps and shoes
- Priced at $0.99 per packet, so a trip's worth costs a few dollars

**Cons**

- Single-use format means carrying a packet per flossing session, which adds up on long carries
- One strand is a short length of repair thread
- Can cut through ultralight fabrics if used without reinforcement
- Foil packets add a small amount of trash
- A roll is cheaper per use for hikers who floss daily

## Who Should Buy This

Section hikers and thru-hikers who floss and want a clean, no-fuss solution will get the most from these. They are also a good fit for anyone building a minimalist repair kit who wants a dual-purpose item that stays in the pack all season. Weekend backpackers can simply toss a couple in the first-aid kit.

Hikers on stretches of 10 days or more between towns, or those who floss every night, may prefer a short length of roll floss wound onto a card, which has a lower weight and cost per use and supplies more thread for repairs. Hikers who skip flossing entirely should still consider a packet or two for the repair kit.

## Verdict

For $0.99 and 1 g, a packet of floss is among the cheapest dual-use items a backpacker can carry. It handles oral hygiene without the clutter of a dispenser and offers real stitching strength when a strap or seam gives out. Pre-cut packets are the best choice for short and mid-length trips, while long-haul hikers should move to a roll.

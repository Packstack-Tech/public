## Overview

MSR's Groundhog line is the default Y-beam stake of the backpacking world: 7000-series aluminum, three-sided cross-section, a notch for the guyline and a pull loop for extraction. The full-size Groundhog runs 7.5 inches; the Mini-Groundhog trims that to 6 inches and 9.4 g per stake. Both are built for backpackers replacing the thin shepherd-hook stakes that ship with most tents. The trade-off is the usual one for short stakes: less weight, less margin in soft ground.

## Performance

### Holding power

The Y-beam is the reason these stakes have outlasted a decade of lighter-weight fashion. Three faces put a lot of surface area into the soil for very little metal, and the shape is non-directional, so there is no orienting a stake with cold fingers at dusk. MSR rates the Mini for medium to firm soil. In hard-packed trail dirt, compacted forest floor and gravelly alpine ground, owners report the stakes bite and stay put, including hiker reports from the Colorado Rockies of a full trip in hard soil with no failures.

Pull-out comparisons put the Mini on par with other stakes in its weight class. It does not match heavier options such as the full Groundhog or Easton's 8-inch stakes, and no one claims otherwise.

### Length and angle

Six inches is the real constraint. Set at roughly 45 degrees from vertical and leaned away from the load, the Mini holds in firm ground. In deep duff, boggy forest floor or coastal sand, where the soil is soft for several inches, the shorter stake simply does not reach anything solid. The 7.5-inch Groundhog gives more purchase in those conditions, though neither size is a snow or loose-sand stake. A common community compromise: full-size Groundhogs at the high-tension corners, Minis on lower-tension guylines.

### Durability

The 7000-series alloy resists bending and denting noticeably better than the 6000-series aluminum in budget stakes, and well-treated sets last for years. The weak point is the narrowed neck at the guyline notch. One JMT hiker reported breaking two of four Minis at that narrowing, and others describe a Mini snapping while being driven into frozen soil. Aluminum is also more prone to bending than titanium. The practical rule among ultralight hikers is to push stakes in with foot pressure or tap them with a rock, rather than hammering, and to treat ground that refuses a stake as a cue to find a rock anchor instead.

### Pull loop and visibility

The pull loop makes retrieval easy in firm soil, though in very hard ground even the loop may not be enough. Owners report that the loops can work loose after heavy use; re-tying them with a more secure knot fixes it. The red anodized finish stands out against leaf litter and dry grass, an advantage over bare titanium stakes, which tend to be left behind at camp.

## Choosing a Version

Two listings, Mini-Groundhog Tent and Mini-Groundhog, are the same 6-inch kit at 56.4 g for six. The Groundhog Tent 6-Pack is the 7.5-inch stake at 78 g, about 21.6 g more than the Mini kit, or roughly 3.6 g more per stake. The Groundhog Tent listing at 83.7 g is the same full-size design.

- **Mini-Groundhog (56.4 g):** The choice for trekking-pole shelters and tarps on firm trail soil, and for lower-tension guylines on any setup. Saving 21.6 g across six stakes is a real gram-counter's gain.
- **Groundhog (78 g and up):** The pick for anyone camping in mixed or softer ground, for the main corner stakes in windy conditions, and for hikers who prefer a wider safety margin over the lowest possible weight.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 9.4 g per Mini stake is light for a Y-beam
- Non-directional cross-section sets easily in the dark
- 7000-series aluminum resists bending better than cheaper alloys
- Red finish makes stakes easy to find and harder to lose
- Pull loop speeds removal in firm soil
- Sold as a kit of six, enough for most tarps and single-wall tents
- Two lengths allow a mixed set

**Cons**

- Mini is rated only for medium to firm soil
- Six-inch length makes placement angle critical
- Thin neck at the notch can snap if the stake is hammered into frozen or rocky ground
- Pull loops can loosen and need re-tying
- Stakes can be hard to extract by hand from hard soil
- Per-stake cost runs above generic Y-stake clones

## Who Should Buy This

The Mini is for the thru-hiker with a trekking-pole shelter or tarp on PCT, JMT or Rockies-type terrain, where firm ground is the norm and every ounce is accounted for. The full Groundhog suits hikers whose routes include softer forest soil or exposed, windy camps. Anyone who regularly pitches on sand, deep duff or snow should look at dedicated sand stakes or snow anchors, and anyone who intends to hammer stakes into frozen ground should choose a steel or heavier option.

## Verdict

The Groundhog family is the baseline most other ultralight stakes are measured against, and the Mini keeps most of the original's holding power at 9.4 g per stake. Match the length to the ground: Minis for firm trail soil and low-tension lines, full-size Groundhogs where the dirt is less cooperative. For most three-season hikers, that pairing is the sensible default.

## Overview

The Sea to Summit Camp is a self-inflating foam-and-air pad aimed at campers and casual backpackers who would rather open a valve than spend five minutes huffing into a mat. It pairs a body-mapped Air Zone construction with a 75D recycled polyester shell at an approachable price. The trade-off is plain: at 944.9 g, it is a pad for people who value convenience and durability over shaving grams.

## Performance

**Warmth.** An R-value of 4.2 puts the Camp comfortably in three-season territory. It handles spring through fall and can stretch into chilly shoulder-season nights. Sea to Summit's own literature pitches the pad as a four-season option for backpacking, camping and scouting trips. That is generous. On frozen or snow-covered ground, a 4.2 is a solid starting point rather than a finishing one, and a closed-cell foam pad underneath is the usual fix once temperatures fall well below freezing.

**Comfort.** At 4 cm, the Camp is not plush by inflatable standards. The polyurethane foam core, though, avoids the pebble-under-the-hip sensation that thin air-only pads can produce. Retailers selling the pad point to the thickness as enough to keep bony hips off the ground for back sleepers. Side sleepers and anyone sensitive at the shoulder or hip will find it adequate rather than cushy, and may want something thicker.

**Inflation and valve.** This is where the Camp earns its keep. Open the valve, let the foam expand for a few minutes, then add a few breaths to firm it up. There is no pump sack to carry and no lung-searing full inflation at the end of a long day. The XPEL multifunction valve also allows fast deflation, which helps at pack-up time. Rolling a self-inflating pad that is still fighting back is a reliable way to annoy a shoulder, and a valve that dumps air quickly takes some of the pain out of it.

**Durability.** The 75D recycled polyester shell with TPU lamination is on the tougher end of what sleeping pads use. It should shrug off the abrasion of rocky tent floors, picnic-table dragging and the general abuse of scout trips and family camping. The foam core adds a margin of safety too: a pad that holds some structure even when the air side is compromised is more forgiving than a thin-walled, air-only model. Sea to Summit's self-inflating line has a reputation among owners for long service life, and the Camp's heavier fabric fits that pattern.

**Weight and packability.** This is the pad's weak point for anyone counting grams. At 944.9 g, it weighs roughly double what the lightest dedicated backpacking air pads do, and self-inflating foam does not compress as small as an air-only design. The name is honest about the intent. It is a camp pad that can double as a backpacking pad for short trips, not a contender for a sub-10-pound base weight.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Self-inflates with only a few top-off breaths, with no pump sack needed
- Tough 75D recycled polyester shell with TPU lamination
- R-4.2 covers most three-season conditions
- Foam core keeps some support even if air is lost
- XPEL valve speeds up deflation and packing

**Cons**

- Heavy, at 944.9 g, for anything beyond casual backpacking
- 4 cm of thickness is adequate but not plush for side sleepers
- Not a true four-season pad despite the marketing framing
- Foam core packs larger than an air-only pad of similar warmth

## Who Should Buy This

Car campers, scout troops, families and newer backpackers will get the most from the Camp. It suits anyone who wants a pad that mostly sets itself up, tolerates rough ground and doesn't cost much. Weekend trips with short mileage are its natural habitat. Thru-hikers and ultralight-leaning backpackers should look at lighter air pads, since carrying nearly a kilogram of pad over hundreds of miles is hard to justify. Winter campers should also look elsewhere, or plan to stack a foam pad beneath it.

## Verdict

The Camp does what its name promises. It inflates with little effort, sleeps warmly enough for three seasons and is built to survive years of casual abuse. It is too heavy for gram-counters and too thin on insulation for winter, but as a dependable, low-fuss pad for camping and short backpacking trips, it earns a 7 out of 10.

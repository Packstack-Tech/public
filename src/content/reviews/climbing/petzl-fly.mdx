## Overview

The Petzl Fly is a certified sit harness that weighs 130 g in size M, about what a full-size smartphone weighs. It has no metal buckles, and the foam comes out. It was built for ski mountaineers, glacier travelers and alpinists who rope up occasionally and walk, skin or boot the rest of the day. The headline trade-off is plain: it is easy to forget while moving and less forgiving once weighted for a long time.

## Performance

**Weight and packability.** The Fly is Petzl's lightest harness. Its closest sibling, the Altitude, weighs 160 g in size M, so the Fly saves 30 g. GearJunkie's reviewer, a ski mountaineer and splitboarder, found that difference hardly noticeable in use. The harness packs down to roughly the volume of a few Clif Bars, and the included minimalist pouch compresses it further for stowing without foam. An REI reviewer described it as rolling into a tiny ball for the hike out.

**On the move.** This is where the Fly earns its price. Owners describe a harness that vanishes under a shell while skinning, booting and riding, with no restriction of movement. One Mountain Project user called it the most comfortable harness for walking he had ever used. The drop-seat design and girth-hitched leg loops mean it goes on with both feet on the ground, over skis or crampons. That is a real convenience on a glacier, where nobody enjoys balancing on one leg in a crampon.

**Hanging and rappelling.** The foam is thin, and the harness is not built for lounging at a hanging belay. Reviewers still report it holds up for practical use. Outdoors Magic's tester took fully weighted 30 m rappels with skis strapped to the pack and had no complaints. A Mountain Project user rates it comfortable enough for regular rappelling without a very heavy pack. Outdoors Magic suggests winter users may find the foam redundant under layers of clothing. Alpine climbers with more fall potential may prefer to leave it in.

**Organization.** Two thin but reinforced gear loops carry the rack. Four small vertical loops handle the odds and ends. Each leg loop has a silicone retainer that holds an ice screw. GearJunkie's reviewer found that enough for organized glacier travel, though he noted that mountaineers wanting more carrying capacity may want something bulkier. One Mountain Project owner clips ice clippers directly over the fabric and skips dedicated slots.

**Tying in.** Petzl's minimalist construction requires tying in through both the waist belt and leg loops. GearJunkie warns that tying into only one will fail catastrophically. That is standard for a harness of this type, but it is not forgiving of mistakes. Anyone who normally clips a single belay loop should read the manual before the first outing.

**Fit.** Sizing is given in centimeters, and one REI buyer who ordered a large based on his waist in inches said it did not fit as expected. Check the chart against a tape measure. The girth-hitched leg loops allow some adjustment, but they are not as quick to adjust as a buckle.

## Choosing a Version

The three sizes differ only in belt and leg-loop length and the weight that comes with them. Size S (66–76 cm waist, 120 g with foam, 90 g without) suits smaller frames. Size M (76–86 cm, 130 g with foam, 100 g without) is the reference size for Petzl's published weight and for most reviews. Size L (86–96 cm, 140 g with foam, 110 g without) covers larger waists and winter layering. Layering matters. The waist belt has to close over a shell and insulation, so those between sizes or planning bulky winter use should measure over clothing before choosing.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 130 g in M, 100 g with foam removed
- Packs to roughly the size of a few energy bars
- Goes on over skis or crampons with feet on the ground
- Barely noticeable while walking, skinning or riding
- Removable foam lets users trade comfort for weight
- CE EN 12277 type C and UIAA certified

**Cons**

- Thin foam makes long hangs less comfortable than a padded harness
- Gear loops are thin and limited for rack-heavy routes
- Must be tied in through both the waist belt and leg loops
- Sized in centimeters; at least one buyer found sizing off
- Pricey for a harness this simple, with retail listings spanning roughly $105–$160

## Who Should Buy This

Ski mountaineers, glacier travelers and alpinists who count grams and use a harness for crevasse rescue, short rappels and occasional roped pitches will get the most from the Fly. It also suits climbers who want a spare, ultra-compact harness for approach days. Climbers who spend long periods hanging at belays, haul heavy racks or want a harness for gym sessions and sport routes should look at a conventional padded model instead.

## Verdict

The Fly delivers what it promises: a certified harness so small and light that it stops being a decision. It gives up hanging comfort and gear capacity to get there, and that is the right trade for ski mountaineering and glacier travel. Buy it for the approach and the descent, not for the project.

## Overview

The Naturehike Mongar 2 is a freestanding, double-wall, 3-season tent for two people who want a Hubba Hubba-style layout without the Hubba Hubba invoice. It has a Y-frame pole set, a centre spreader for near-vertical walls, two doors and two vestibules, and a footprint in the box. The trade-off is weight: at 1,900 to 2,100 g depending on version and what goes on the scale, it's light for the space but not ultralight by thru-hiker standards.

## Performance

**Setup.** The Mongar is built to be pitched without thought. Stake out the footprint corners, seat the double-Y pole ends in its eyelets, clip the inner to the frame, then throw the fly over and buckle it down. Both ends are identical, so there is no wrong way round. One reviewer had it up alone in a little over four minutes on a first attempt. The catch is the order: the inner goes up first. Pitching in a downpour means leaving a fly door open, and the inner can get damp.

**Weather.** The 3,000 mm fly and floor ratings hold up in practice. Owners report multi-day rainy trips without leaks, and testers describe the pitch staying planted through high wind and cold rain. The same mesh-heavy inner that keeps the tent airy in July also gives it little heat retention. On nights below freezing, the air inside is not much warmer than the air outside. This is a spring-through-autumn shelter, and Reddit users who rate it highly consistently add that it is not a four-season tent.

**Space.** This is the Mongar's strongest argument. The spreader pole squares off the ceiling, so the interior reads as a box, not a dome. About 80 percent of the ceiling clears 80 cm, which makes sitting up and changing layers easy. The floor is a true rectangle, about 210 x 135 cm. Two people fit with two pads, and gear goes in the vestibules, not inside. Hikers over roughly 183 cm (6 ft) will find the length tight. The vestibules are large enough that a 75-liter pack fits under one half, leaving the other half free for getting in and out or cooking under cover.

**Ventilation.** Results are mixed. The mesh inner breathes well, and in cold weather with two bodies inside, condensation has been manageable for many owners. The two fly vents are another matter. Long-term owners say they do little on a muggy night, so don't count on them in humid, hot camps. Rolling back the vestibules does more.

**Build.** Testers rate the finish well for a budget brand, with consistent stitching, sealed seams, and sturdy fly clips. The 7001 aluminum poles and their clips feel solid, and the interior has gear pockets and a loft. Reflective guylines and tabs help in finding the tent at night. The one repeat complaint is the stock pegs, which are light but bend easily. Most owners swap in V-stakes or shepherd's-hook pegs before the first trip.

**Weight in context.** Owners who weighed their tents report about 2.16 kg with footprint and pegs, and about 2.03 kg once the pegs come out. That is slightly lighter than one hiker's go-to Wild Country Zephyros 2 once that tent's footprint is added. Split between two people, the tent adds about 1 kg to each pack. For a solo hiker, 4.6 lb is a lot to carry.

## Choosing a Version

The 1,900 g and 2,100 g listings are the same tent: the same 20D fly, Y-frame poles, 210 x 135 cm floor, dual doors and vestibules. What changes is the weight you carry. The 2,100 g figure covers the full kit with footprint, pegs and stuff sack. The 1,900 g figure is the lighter listed weight, 200 g less, or about 100 g per person when two hikers share the load.

- **1,900 g:** For hikers who count grams and are willing to trim the kit, for example by leaving the footprint at home or swapping the stock pegs for lighter ones. It is the weight to plan around for a trimmed-down carry.
- **2,100 g:** For anyone pitching the tent with everything it ships with. It is the realistic figure for a stock tent on the trail, and the better choice if the footprint will protect the floor on rocky ground.

Neither is a gram-counter's tent. At either weight, a Mongar is a comfort pick that happens to be reasonably light.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Fast, symmetrical pitch with no wrong end
- Headroom and a rectangular floor that feel larger than most 2-person tents
- 3,000 mm fly and floor that hold up in sustained rain
- Footprint included, which many competitors sell separately
- Freestanding, so it works on platforms, sand and rock
- Fly-only or inner-only pitches for emergency or hot-night use
- Two doors and two roomy vestibules, so neither sleeper has to climb over the other

**Cons**

- Not ultralight: 1.9–2.1 kg is heavy for gram-counters
- Inner pitches first, so it can get wet in a downpour
- Mesh-heavy inner offers almost no warmth below freezing
- Fly vents do little in humid or hot weather
- Stock pegs bend and should be replaced
- Tight in length for anyone over about 183 cm

## Who Should Buy This

Two hikers who want a freestanding tent that goes up in minutes, with real sitting-up room and vestibule space, and who don't want to spend $400 or more, will get the most from it. It also suits solo hikers who value comfort over grams, and anyone who regularly pitches on platforms or rocky ground. Naturehike has said this model won't be restocked once it sells out, and points buyers to the upgraded Mongar BASE, so availability may vary. Thru-hikers chasing a sub-1 kg shelter should look at a trekking-pole tent or a cuben-fiber tarp setup, and cold-weather campers should look at a tent with solid inner walls.

## Verdict

The Mongar 2 is a well-executed budget shelter that does the basics right: it keeps rain out, sets up in minutes, and gives two people more room than its weight suggests. The weak pegs and poor vents are real flaws, and the weight rules it out for serious ultralight use. For a duo that values comfort and price, it is an easy recommendation.

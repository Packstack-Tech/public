## Overview

The Anker PowerPort Solar Lite is a two-panel, bi-fold solar charger that clips to a pack and feeds phones, small batteries and camera gear through a pair of USB ports. It has no internal battery, which keeps weight down and the electronics simple. The trade-off is that it only produces useful power when the sun is out and the panel is pointed at it.

## Performance

**Charging speed.** In clear midday sun, the Solar Lite behaves like a modest wall charger. Diglloyd's tests put an iPhone 5s from 20% to 100% in just over two hours, and the panel refilled two drained 700 mAh batteries in about 3.5 hours of strong sunlight. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers plugged it into a dead battery pack and found it had reached 49% after three hours of full sun. That was a hair behind the 50% managed by the Instapark Mercury 10W, which is a smaller panel. The takeaway is that 15W on the label means about 10W at the port, and real-world output depends heavily on angle and haze.

**Clouds and shade.** Owners are consistent here. Bikepackers have reported it charging a phone directly through cloud cover. Others describe overcast conditions as slow, and forest canopy as a poor match. One long-term reviewer described a gray Seattle morning as barely a trickle. Owners who get the best results park the panel stationary in full sun and charge a battery bank, then top up the phone from the bank overnight. A strapped-to-the-pack panel on a moving hiker works, but it yields less. One owner noted the built-in regulator handles changing light well, and that charging goes better with the phone switched off.

**Durability.** The cells are laminated in PET polymer and sewn into a polyester canvas shell. Outdoor Gear Lab called it incredibly durable, though not fully waterproof. It will shrug off a sprinkle but not a soaking. A long-term reviewer reported six months of being stuffed into packs, dropped on rocks and rained on with the panel still looking nearly new. The USB converter sits in a canvas pocket, which gives the ports some protection from weather. Sand is another matter, and a beach user warned about grit getting into them.

**Weight and packing.** At 355 g, the panel was an outlier when it launched, since Outdoor Gear Lab noted that a 15W panel under 14 oz was unheard of. It folds to roughly the size of a paperback, thicker than the 4 mm opened profile suggests. Elastic loops at the corners let it hang from a tent, tree or backpack. One owner stripped 100 g by replacing the cover. The 21W PowerPort Solar sibling adds a third panel for about 60 g more (417 g, 3A output), and diglloyd suggested it as the better pick for anyone willing to carry the difference.

**Quirks.** The canvas is black, and so the panels and pocket run hot. One reviewer found everything very warm after four hours of sun, and Anker's own guidance is to keep phones and tablets out of direct sunlight while charging. The accessory pocket is tight; Outdoor Gear Lab found it barely fit an iPhone 6, and modern phones will not fit. The Velcro closure also picks up trail debris.

**Availability.** Outdoor Gear Lab reports that Anker discontinued the Lite 15W in April 2018. Stock tends to be spotty and secondhand prices on resale sites can run well above the original $40–60 range.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 355 g is light for a 15W panel with two USB ports
- No internal battery to fail, so any power bank can be paired with it
- Tough PET-and-canvas construction that handles drops and light rain
- Regulator copes well with passing clouds and shifting light
- Two ports can run a phone and a headlamp or camera battery together
- Historically priced at a fraction of comparable Goal Zero panels

**Cons**

- Real-world output is capped at 5V/2.1A, or about 10.5W
- Slow under overcast skies and poor under forest canopy
- Black fabric gets hot; phones should not sit on it
- Small pocket and debris-prone Velcro closure
- Not fully waterproof
- Discontinued, so sourcing a new one is hit-or-miss

## Who Should Buy This

This panel suits backpackers on desert, alpine or ridgeline routes who want to keep a phone, GPS and a small battery bank topped up on multi-day trips. It rewards people who stop for lunch, lay it out in the sun and charge a power bank. It's also a fair pick for bikepackers who want something light and simple to strap to a bag.

Hikers in dense, wet forest, such as the Pacific Northwest or Appalachian summers, will get little out of it. So will anyone on a short trip, where a bigger power bank is lighter and more reliable than a panel.

## Verdict

The PowerPort Solar Lite is a well-built, light, no-nonsense panel that delivers what it promises in clear sun and little in gloom. Buy it if the route is open and sunny and a used or leftover unit turns up at a sensible price. Skip it for forested trails, and consider the 21W sibling if an extra 60 g is no obstacle.

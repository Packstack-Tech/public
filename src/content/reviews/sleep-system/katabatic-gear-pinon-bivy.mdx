## Overview

The Piñon Bivy is a bug-proof, splash-proof sleeping pod from Colorado quilt maker Katabatic Gear, built to sit under a tarp or inside a floorless pyramid. It has a waterproof bathtub floor and a breathable, water-resistant upper, so it handles ground moisture, wind-driven spray and mosquitoes without the condensation of a fully waterproof bivy. The trade-off is that it is not a standalone shelter. Without a roof over it, the upper will not keep out a real storm.

## Performance

**Weather protection.** The floor does most of the work. Katabatic uses a ROBIC high-tenacity nylon with a UTS coating, which it says adds tear and abrasion resistance and is less slick than a straight silicone finish. The floor has fewer seams, and it wraps up the sides in a bathtub shape. Owners who pair the bivy with a tarp describe it as the part that keeps a quilt dry when rain blows in sideways or the ground goes soggy. One long-term owner reports more than 20 trips over five years, including some wet, rough weather under a tarp, with no failures. A reviewer who logged 40-plus nights across the Sierra, desert and snow reached a similar conclusion. He also added shock-cord loops to the corners to pull the tub walls up higher in desert thunderstorms.

**Breathability.** The Pertex Quantum Air upper is the reason to pick this over a fully waterproof bivy. It is more air-permeable than typical shell fabrics, but still wind-resistant and water-resistant enough to shed mist and spray. Backpacking Light's description notes that this means less condensation than waterproof-topped bivies. The mesh panel over the head and torso adds ventilation, and owners call the setup extremely breathable. The upper is water-resistant, not waterproof, which is why Katabatic points buyers toward a tarp or pyramid overhead.

**Bugs and entry.** The mesh and zipper give full bug protection. The long U-shaped zipper runs from waist level across the top, so getting in and out takes no contortions, and owners cite the zipper length and ample mesh as reasons they bought it. Tie loops at the head and foot hold the fabric off your face and quilt. One buyer's tip is that a ball cap keeps the netting off your nose without tying anything up.

**Space.** The cut is generous for a 7-ounce bivy. Owners report room for a phone, book and headlamp at the head, and for a pair of shoes or a rolled layer along the side. One 6'3" buyer found the 6' wide plenty roomy. Claustrophobic sleepers should still be warned. One five-year owner admits to being teased for sleeping in a coffin, and says the weight savings made it worth it.

**Setup and fit with a pad.** Internal attachment points keep the pad from sliding out from under you in the night. Both versions take a 25" wide pad. With a wider pad, the extra shoulder and foot girth of the wide version becomes more useful. The bivy comes with no stakes or guylines, a point at least one buyer noticed on unboxing. Plan on six stakes, plus two trekking poles if you want to pitch it as a standalone bug shelter.

**Weight.** The bivy's weight is the one thing its fans flag as a downside. At roughly 7.3 to 7.8 oz, it is a real addition to a base weight that already has a tarp and a quilt. It does replace an inner tent and a ground sheet, though, and it packs to about the size of a soda can.

## Choosing a Version

Two versions are covered here. They share the same fabrics, zipper, pad attachment and layout. The differences are girth, height and a bit of weight.

- **Standard (207 g):** Katabatic lists this one with a 77.5" shoulder girth, a 63.5" foot girth and a 17" peak height. The mesh window measures 31.5" x 36". It suits average-build sleepers on a regular-width pad, back sleepers and anyone counting every gram.
- **Wide (221 g):** This adds 14 g. Katabatic lists it at 82" shoulder girth, 68" foot girth, an 18" peak and a 33.5" x 36" mesh window. It suits broad-shouldered sleepers, side sleepers who shift around, and anyone who wants to keep more gear inside. It also gives a quilt more room to loft without pressing against the walls.

Both versions fit 25" pads, so the pad is not what separates them. For 14 g, the wide version is the better choice for anyone between sizes or anyone who sleeps restlessly.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Waterproof ROBIC floor with a bathtub cut and few seams
- Breathable Pertex Quantum Air upper keeps condensation down
- Extra-large mesh panel and a long, easy zipper
- Internal pad attachment and tie loops for head and foot
- Packs to about 3.5" x 7.5"
- Long-term owners report good durability, with 20-plus trips and 40-plus nights logged

**Cons**

- Upper is water-resistant only, so it needs a tarp or pyramid overhead
- 7.3–7.8 oz is a real weight cost on top of a tarp
- Stakes and guylines are not included
- Tight, coffin-like feel for those who dislike enclosed sleeping
- $159 is a lot for a bug-and-splash layer

## Who Should Buy This

This is for tarp and pyramid campers who want bug protection and a dry floor without the weight of a full tent. It suits thru-hikers on mosquito-heavy or wet routes, cyclists, and anyone using a floorless shelter. Hikers who want a standalone shelter should look elsewhere, since the upper will not handle a storm on its own. The same goes for gram-counters who sleep under a tarp in a bug-free place and need only a ground sheet.

## Verdict

The Piñon Bivy does one job well. It adds a dry floor, bug netting and a breathable top to a tarp setup for about the weight of a light down vest. Owners report that it holds up through years of use and wet weather. For tarp campers who need it, it is an easy recommendation.

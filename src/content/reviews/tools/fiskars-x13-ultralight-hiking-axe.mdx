## Overview

The Fiskars X13 is the lightest axe in the Finnish company's X-Series: a 13-inch, orange-handled hatchet built for backpackers who want to process kindling and small firewood without carrying a full-size camp axe. It replaces the old X5 hand axe, with a longer handle and a head Fiskars says is 15% lighter than its predecessor's. The trade-off is plain. It is far lighter than the X7 and X14, and it gives up some of their chopping authority to get there.

## Performance

**Weight and carry.** Most of the X13's weight savings come from the head. A cutout through the head, which also works as a bottle opener, takes metal out while keeping the balance point where Fiskars wants it. One owner on Bushcraft USA put the X13 at roughly 60% of an X7's weight, noting that a Bahco Laplander folding saw and the X13 together weigh less than the X7 alone. At 480 g, the axe lands in the same range as a heavy folding saw, which is the comparison ultralight hikers will actually make.

**Chopping and splitting.** The blade is thin and ground wide, and the low-friction coating helps it release from wood instead of binding. Reviewers describe clean, controlled cuts on kindling and small rounds. The limits come from physics. Owners who have used the X7 say the X13 splits less aggressively because the head is thinner and lighter, though they still call it capable for a roughly one-pound hatchet. Reviewers also note that the 13-inch handle gives less leverage than the 14-inch X7 and X14. Fiskars pitches this as a kindling-and-camp-chores tool, and testers' experience agrees. Rounds much beyond about 6 inches are better left to a saw or a bigger axe.

**Handle and durability.** The FiberComp handle damps shock well enough that a swing doesn't sting the palm on cold mornings. The head is insert-molded, which Fiskars says reduces loosening and overstrike breakage. A European buyer noted the handle is hollow inside but still feels solid. That is how the weight comes out, not a flaw. The bright orange color earns its keep: set this axe down in duff or snow and it stays visible.

**The sheath.** This is the accessory owners mention most. It locks onto the head positively, comes off easily when needed, and has a belt loop. It also exposes the poll while the axe is sheathed, so the back of the head can drive tent stakes without dulling the edge. One Bushcraft USA member cut off the hanging loop and has used it ever since, carrying the axe in a car, on backpacking trips and in a canoe. The same member said it protects the head without trapping moisture. Owners rate it above many aftermarket edge guards.

**Edge and upkeep.** The double-hardened blade and proprietary grind are meant to hold an edge longer, and users report that it stays sharp through ordinary camp use. The coating also guards against rust. As with any coated head, a field file or small stone will restore the edge once the coating wears off at the bevel.

**Price.** Street prices run from about $30 on Amazon, where one reviewer reported paying just under that, to about €45 at European retailers. That is cheap for a forged head with a 25-year warranty and a sheath that works.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 480 g with sheath, about 60% of the X7's weight
- Forged, double-hardened head with a low-friction coating
- Secure locking sheath with belt loop; poll can be used as a stake hammer while sheathed
- Shock-absorbing FiberComp handle in high-visibility orange
- 25-year warranty and Finnish manufacture at a modest price
- Red Dot "Best of the Best" design award (2023)

**Cons**

- The 13-inch handle gives less leverage than 14-inch X-Series hatchets
- Thin head splits less well than the X7 and struggles with larger rounds
- Still heavier than a saw-only or knife-only fire kit for most trips
- Hollow handle and plastic composite construction won't suit bushcrafters who want a hickory-and-steel tool

## Who Should Buy This

Backpackers who build fires regularly, such as winter campers, canoe trippers, hunters, and bushcraft-leaning hikers on long routes. They want a real axe in the pack without the 700 g-plus penalty of a standard camp hatchet. It also suits anyone who wants a stake hammer and a kindling splitter in one tool. Gram-counters who cook on a stove and rarely light a fire should skip it, since a saw or a good knife batons small wood for less weight. Campers who regularly process thick logs should step up to the X7 or X14, which have more mass behind the swing.

## Verdict

The X13 is a well-made, sensibly priced axe that delivers what its name promises: a usable chopping tool at a weight ultralight hikers can justify on fire-dependent trips. Its limits are the short handle and thin head, which make it a kindling and small-wood tool rather than a log-splitter. For its intended job, it is the best-sorted lightweight camp axe on the market.

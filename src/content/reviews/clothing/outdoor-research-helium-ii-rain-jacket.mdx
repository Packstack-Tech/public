## Overview

The Outdoor Research Helium II was, for the better part of a decade, one of the go-to minimalist rain shells for thru-hikers and fastpackers who wanted real waterproof protection without the weight penalty of a "backpacking" rain jacket. It's built around 2.5-layer Pertex Shield fabric, skips almost every non-essential feature, and packs down into its own chest pocket to about the size of a fist. Outdoor Research has since discontinued the Helium II in favor of updated Helium lineup jackets, so this one is now mostly a secondhand or closeout find — but it's worth understanding what made it a category staple in the first place.

## Performance

**Waterproofing and breathability.** The Pertex Shield DS 2.5-layer laminate does what it's supposed to for the conditions it's designed around: <cite index="16-1,16-2">the shell is made of 2.5-layer 30D Pertex Shield DS ripstop fabric with clear laminate, and while very lightweight, it still has an impressive array of features</cite>. Real-world feedback lines up with that — one long-term user noted <cite index="11-11">this jacket provides good protection from moderate rain for up to a few hours before wetting out</cite>. That's the honest ceiling for any 2.5-layer fabric this thin: fine for showers, squalls, and "I need to get to camp" situations, less fine for all-day monsoon-season slogging where a heavier 3-layer shell will hold up longer before the face fabric saturates and breathability drops off.

**Fit and feel.** Sizing runs true. One backpacking reviewer running a men's medium said <cite index="18-20,18-21">he liked the fit a lot, and the medium ran true-to-size for someone who normally wears mediums</cite>, describing the cut as <cite index="18-22">close but not slim — what Outdoor Research calls "Standard"</cite>. There's enough room to layer a light fleece or puffy underneath without feeling like a sausage casing, but it's not roomy. On skin contact, one Trailspace reviewer noted <cite index="16-3">the fabric does feel a little clammy, though it isn't as bad as other plastic/wax rain jackets</cite> they'd owned — a fair way to describe most non-eVent, non-Gore-Tex Pro shells in this weight class.

**Features (or lack thereof).** OR pared this jacket down hard to hit 6.4 oz, and it shows. There are no hand pockets, no pit zips, and minimal adjustability. A Trailspace reviewer summed up the compromise well: <cite index="16-4,16-5,16-6">this is one area where a few extra ounces wouldn't hurt — the Helium II was designed to keep weight as low as possible, so OR cut the wrist adjustment straps in favor of elastic wrist openings</cite>. That same reviewer found <cite index="16-9">the wrist openings a little larger than their wrists, letting water run in when arms were angled upward</cite> — a minor but real annoyance in a downpour. Backpackers.com's reviewer had a similar gripe about the missing hand pockets, saying <cite index="18-1,18-2">he'd dock the Helium for not having two standard side pockets, since it made it difficult to warm his hands</cite>, though he was quick to add <cite index="18-3,18-4">he refused to blame a purposely simple jacket for his personal preferences — it does the job well for which it's intended</cite>.

**Packability.** This is where the Helium II earns its reputation. <cite index="16-11,16-12">Weighing in at 6.4 oz, it's easy to find space for in your pack, and you can also stuff it into its own pocket</cite> for a self-contained stuff sack. Pack Hacker's testers called it <cite index="13-9">one of the lightest rain jackets available, packing down into the size of your fist</cite>, and noted it's <cite index="13-16">quite breathable, even when tested in a very hot and humid climate</cite>.

**Durability.** The thin 30D face fabric is the trade-off for the low weight. One reviewer put it bluntly: <cite index="11-16,11-17,11-18">the fabric is very thin, so you may need to be careful wearing it — good for trail running, but not very durable for backpacking</cite>. That tracks with what I'd expect from a fabric this light: fine if you're not bushwhacking or scrambling over granite in it, but not something to treat carelessly around pack straps, buckles, or brush.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Genuinely ultralight at 181 g — barely noticeable in the pack
- Reliable waterproofing for showers and moderate rain
- Packs into its own pocket, no separate stuff sack needed
- True-to-size, standard fit that layers reasonably well
- Historically inexpensive relative to other ultralight shells (when available)

**Cons**

- Discontinued — only findable secondhand or in dwindling retailer stock
- No hand pockets, no pit zips, minimal venting
- Wrist elastic isn't fully adjustable and can let water in at certain arm angles
- Thin 30D fabric wants babying, not rough treatment
- Will wet through on multi-hour, all-day rain rather than a quick squall

## Who Should Buy This

This jacket made sense for ultralight backpackers, thru-hikers, and trail runners who wanted emergency-grade waterproof protection at the lowest possible weight and didn't mind giving up pockets and adjustability to get there. If you can still find one secondhand or on a closeout rack at a reasonable price, it's a solid buy for that use case — just go in knowing it's a "get you through the storm" jacket, not an "live in it all day" jacket.

## Verdict

The Helium II earned its reputation as an ultralight classic for good reason: at 181 g it delivers real waterproof protection with almost nothing wasted on frills. It's not the jacket for someone who wants hand pockets, pit zips, or all-day foul-weather durability — for that, look at something like the Helium HD or a heavier 3-layer shell. But as a minimalist, pack-it-and-forget-it rain layer, it's still worth hunting down on the secondhand market. I'd put it at a 7.5/10, docked mainly for the lack of features and the fact that you can no longer buy it new.

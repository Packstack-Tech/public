## Overview

The Borealis Hooded Jacket is Rab's long-running, stripped-down softshell: a single layer of thin stretch nylon, a helmet-friendly hood, two hand pockets and not much else. It is aimed at people who move fast and run hot, such as fastpackers, scramblers and climbers. It also suits three-season hikers who want something between a flimsy windshirt and a full rain shell. The trade-off is plain. It breathes and moves far better than a hardshell, but it is not waterproof and it adds little warmth.

## Performance

**Breathability and wind resistance.** The Borealis earns its reputation here. Rab builds it from a light, tightly woven single-layer nylon that blocks gusts on exposed ridgelines while letting heat and moisture escape during sustained climbing. Live For The Outdoors' testers singled out its breathability, calling it a "put it on and leave it on" layer. They also found it more comfortable than a typical windproof and more weather-resistant than a lightweight fleece. A REI reviewer who wears it over a base layer in mild winter conditions says it stays warm enough while remaining very breathable on high-intensity climbs.

**Weather protection.** The DWR finish handles drizzle and light showers, and the fabric dries quickly. Owners and testers agree that is where it ends. REI buyers describe it as a bridge for the moments when a hardshell feels like overkill, and also say it is no replacement for one. Live For The Outdoors rates it for fair-weather and shoulder-season use, and notes UPF 50+ sun protection, a useful extra on exposed summer terrain. In sustained rain, a hiker will want a real rain jacket in the pack.

**Stretch and fit.** Two-way stretch is the other selling point. The cut is slim and climbing-oriented, and REI owners note that it runs closer to the body but moves easily. Slim means a base layer and perhaps a thin fleece underneath, not a puffy. Anyone between sizes or planning to layer heavily should consider sizing up.

**Durability.** Despite feeling nearly insubstantial in the hand, the fabric has held up. Switchback Travel's testers reported that it stayed resilient through weeks of use on rocky alpine terrain. Matrix is a tightly woven nylon, and sharp granite or brush is still the likeliest source of damage, but it is not a delicate fabric.

**Features and packability.** The feature list is short on purpose. The hood is thin and low-volume, so it sits under a helmet without bunching, though it won't offer the shelter of a cinched storm hood. The hem adjustment is low-profile enough to sit comfortably under a pack hip belt or harness. The cuffs seal against wind without adding bulk. Switchback's testers liked the minimal approach for "true ultralight missions" and noted that the current version's two hand pockets replaced an earlier single chest pocket, which they preferred. Rab sells a separate Borealis Alpine with a chest pocket and a stuff-into-pocket design for climbers.

**Weight in context.** At 273 g in a medium, the Borealis is not a windshirt-class piece. A true ultralight wind shell weighs considerably less. This jacket replaces a windshirt and a fleece or midlayer in one garment, and in that role the weight is easier to justify. For hikers who carry a windshirt purely as a rain-and-wind emergency layer, it is overbuilt.

**Warmth.** Both Switchback and Live For The Outdoors flag the same limit: the Borealis does little for you in cold temperatures. It cuts wind chill but does not insulate.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Excellent breathability for high-output hiking, scrambling and climbing
- Soft, stretchy fabric that is comfortable against the skin and dries quickly
- Good wind resistance and UPF 50+ sun protection
- Helmet-compatible hood and low-profile hem and cuffs that work under a pack or harness
- Recycled fabric with a fluorocarbon-free DWR
- Strong value, with street prices often well under the $130 list

**Cons**

- Not waterproof; DWR copes only with light rain
- Offers little warmth on its own
- Heavier than a dedicated ultralight windshirt
- Slim cut limits bulky layering
- Minimal feature set: no chest pocket, and no thumb loops or hood cinch

## Who Should Buy This

The Borealis suits the hiker or climber who is always too hot in a hardshell and too exposed in a fleece. Examples include fastpackers, alpine scramblers, ski tourers on the uphill and three-season backpackers in dry, windy country. It works best as the one outer layer for a fair-weather trip, or as a daily-driver wind layer that happens to pack small. Thru-hikers who carry a windshirt for emergencies, or who need dependable storm protection, should look elsewhere. Anyone needing a jacket that can take sustained rain should buy a proper rain shell, and anyone chasing the lowest possible base weight should buy a true windshirt.

## Verdict

The Rab Borealis Hooded Jacket is a well-executed, honest softshell. It does breathability, stretch and wind protection very well, and it makes no claim to do anything else. For the right kind of fast, dry-weather hiking it is hard to beat at its usual street price, and it is a poor choice for wet or cold conditions.

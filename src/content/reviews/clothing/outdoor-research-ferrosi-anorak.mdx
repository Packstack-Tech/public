## Overview

The Ferrosi Anorak takes Outdoor Research's well-liked Ferrosi softshell fabric and strips the hoodie down to a pullover: no full-length zipper, a deep front zip, a close-fitting hood and a big chest pocket. It suits hikers, climbers and backpackers who want a breathable layer that stays on all day. The trade-off is weight. At 329 g it is a soft, comfortable windbreaker for people who run warm, not a shell for gram counters.

## Performance

**Wind and weather.** The 90D woven ripstop is tightly woven enough to cut a ridge-top breeze, and the anorak's pullover cut leaves fewer seams and openings for wind to find than a full-zip jacket. Water resistance is a different matter. Reviewers who have worn the Ferrosi family on long trips describe it as a layer for light precipitation. Testers at The Trek wrote that the fabric trades water resistance for breathability, and that it dries quickly if the wearer keeps moving. In a sustained downpour it is not a rain jacket and shouldn't be treated as one.

**Breathability and mobility.** This is where the Ferrosi fabric earns its following. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers rated the Ferrosi Hoodie, which uses the same fabric, 8 out of 10 for breathability and 9 for mobility, and the whole team noted how well it shed heat. The 14% spandex gives the sleeves and shoulders freedom for scrambling or reaching for a hold. Retail reviewers describe the feel as closer to a thin sweatshirt than a crinkly shell. That is high praise from people who wear one all day.

**Warmth.** Outdoor Gear Lab's testers stayed comfortable in the Ferrosi Hoodie down to roughly 50°F on low-effort days. They went much cooler when hiking or running. The Trek's reviewer noted that the Ferrosi doesn't hold warmth once the wearer stops producing it. The anorak behaves the same way. It is a layer for moving, and a camp-side puffy still has to come along in shoulder season.

**Pockets and features.** The zippered chest stash pocket gets the most attention. Backcountry's editors named it their favorite feature, and customers say it swallows snacks, a phone and a map without bulging. The kangaroo hand pockets add a place to warm hands, and the snaps keep them closed. Because the front zip is deep but not full-length, venting is a matter of unzipping partway or pulling the hood down. Women's-model owners have called the elastic hem band unusually heavy for the jacket's size, and that detail adds to the weight total.

**Durability.** The Ferrosi line has a good record against hip belts and shoulder straps. The Trek's reviewer reported that the hooded jacket stood up to frequent pack contact, and friends with older Ferrosi pieces saw little wear on the fabric or zippers. The 90D ripstop is more robust than the 7- to 15-denier shells in the ultralight wind-jacket category.

**Fit.** Owners say the anorak runs a little roomier than the Ferrosi hoodie. One buyer in the women's model who normally wears a large found it noticeably bigger. Those between sizes who plan to layer a fleece underneath will be fine at their usual size, and hikers who want a trim fit should consider sizing down. UPF 50+ fabric is a quiet bonus on exposed, sunny terrain.

**Price.** The list price has been $115, and deal listings from Backcountry and Sam's Club have put it between roughly $30 and $46. At those prices it is hard to argue with.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Highly breathable stretch fabric that moves with the wearer
- Tougher than most ultralight wind shells; handles pack straps well
- Large zippered chest pocket plus hand pockets
- UPF 50+ and wind-resistant; sheds light drizzle
- Frequently discounted well below the $115 list price

**Cons**

- 329 g is heavy for a wind layer, roughly double what many dedicated wind shells weigh
- Not a rain jacket; wets through in sustained rain
- Pullover design is slower to vent than a full zip
- Runs slightly large according to owners
- Little warmth when the wearer stops moving

## Who Should Buy This

Hikers, climbers, cross-country skiers and trail runners who generate heat and want one stretchy, durable layer for wind and light weather will like it. It also works as a travel and town jacket that doesn't look like technical gear. Ultralight backpackers who carry a 100-gram wind shell and a separate rain jacket won't find the weight justified, and anyone heading into wet climates needs a real waterproof shell.

## Verdict

The Ferrosi Anorak is a comfortable, durable, very breathable softshell that cuts wind and tolerates light drizzle. It is not an ultralight piece and isn't trying to be one. Buy it as an all-day active layer, especially on sale, and look elsewhere if base weight or heavy rain is your main concern.

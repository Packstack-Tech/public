## Overview

Purell Advanced Hand Sanitizer Gel is the pocket-sized version of the brand America's drugstores have sold for decades: a 1 fl oz flip-cap bottle of 70% ethyl alcohol gel. It earns its place as a cheap, familiar, easy-to-find way to clean hands when there's no water to spare. The trade-off is the one every alcohol sanitizer carries into the backcountry: it's convenient, but it isn't a substitute for soap.

## Performance

**Germ-killing.** At 70% ethanol, the gel sits comfortably above the 60% alcohol minimum the CDC sets for sanitizers used when soap and water aren't available. Purell says it kills 99.99% of common germs that may cause illness, and markets the formula as outperforming other sanitizers ounce for ounce. That claim is the manufacturer's own, but the alcohol concentration is the number that matters, and it's a solid one.

**Where it falls short.** Trail hygiene is the context that matters here, and the guidance is consistent. The CDC says soap and water remove certain germs, including norovirus, Cryptosporidium, and C. difficile, more effectively than sanitizer does. Backpacker, GearJunkie, and The Trek all make the same point about norovirus, which sweeps through crowded thru-hike corridors like the Appalachian Trail every year. The Trek's headline on the subject is blunt: hand sanitizer is not the answer. Alcohol gel also works best on reasonably clean hands. Trail grime, sunscreen, and dirt give microbes places to hide, so a squirt on filthy palms after digging a cathole does less than it seems to.

**Where it earns its keep.** Sanitizer's real value on trail is the occasional moment when washing isn't practical: a trailhead after a pit toilet, a hitch into town, a gas-station stop, a shared resupply box. It needs no water, no rinsing 200 feet from a stream, and no lather. The flip-top is a one-handed operation, and the gel stays put on the palm rather than running off like a liquid or spray. Owners tend to use it as a supplement to soap, not a stand-in.

**Skin feel.** The formula includes four moisturizers and carries Purell's "clinically proven to maintain skin health" claim. Gel sanitizers still dry hands with repeated use, and hikers who are already cracking their knuckles in dry, high-altitude air should expect more of the same.

**Carry and durability.** At 3.4 inches tall and under an inch and a half wide, the bottle slips into a hip-belt pocket or the lid pocket without notice. The flip cap is a mild leak risk under pressure in a stuffed pack; the usual remedy is a zip-top bag. Purell recommends storing it below 110°F, which matters if the bottle rides on a dashboard or in a sun-baked pack on a desert section. Alcohol gels are also flammable, so the bottle belongs away from stove fuel and open flame.

**Weight.** At 28 g it's an ounce of product in a bottle, with no meaningful tare to shave. Ultralight hikers who track every gram can decant it into a smaller dropper or carry a few milliliters in a repurposed container. The bigger weight question is whether to carry it at all on a trip where soap leaves or a sliver of bar soap handle the job better.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 70% ethanol, well above the CDC's 60% threshold
- Flip-top opens and closes one-handed
- Gel stays on the hand and doesn't drip
- Moisturizing formula free of dyes, triclosan, parabens, and phthalates
- Widely sold, easy to replace in any trail town
- Compact enough to ride in a pocket

**Cons**

- Not effective against norovirus, which is the main hygiene threat on crowded trails
- Less effective on visibly dirty hands
- 28 g is a lot for a backup item on a sub-5-lb base weight
- Flip cap can pop open under pack pressure
- Flammable, and heat-sensitive above 110°F
- Repeated use dries skin

## Who Should Buy This

Weekend backpackers, section hikers, and anyone who passes through trailheads, shared shuttles, and town stops will find this a handy, familiar addition. It also suits people who want something they can grab at any pharmacy rather than order online. Thru-hikers can carry it too, but as a secondary measure used after pit toilets and before meals only when washing isn't possible. Anyone relying on it as their only hand hygiene, especially on busy trails in peak season, should pack soap instead. Gram counters who want a lighter option should look at a bar-soap sliver or soap leaves, or decant a small amount of sanitizer into a lighter container.

## Verdict

Purell's 1 oz gel does what an alcohol sanitizer is supposed to do, and the 70% formulation is a dependable one. The limits are inherent to the category: it's no replacement for soap and water, and its 28 g carry weight is hard to justify as a primary system. Pack it as a backup, not a plan.

## Overview

The Petzl Meteor is a lightweight in-mold climbing helmet built for anyone who wants a single lid that works for cragging, alpine routes, and ski touring without weighing down the pack. It's aimed at climbers and mountaineers who prioritize weight and ventilation but don't want to pay lightest-in-class prices for it — think of it as the helmet you'd buy if you climb often enough that a heavy, hot helmet becomes a real annoyance, but you're not chasing the absolute gram-weenie option.

## Performance

The Meteor's biggest selling point is how little you notice it's there. <cite index="13-1,13-2">The Meteor is comfortable in large part due to its scant weight, and it's so light that it's hard to feel on your head, let alone be annoyed by its presence.</cite> That's not marketing spin — it lines up with what I've felt on long alpine days where a heavy helmet starts to feel like a vice by hour six.

Ventilation is the other headline feature. <cite index="9-1">With 21 ventilation holes spread out over the helmet's front, sides, and rear, the Meteor is one of the most ventilated helmets you can choose.</cite> On hot approach hikes or sweaty ice-climbing belays, that airflow makes a real difference — this isn't a helmet that turns your scalp into a sauna the way some hard-shell ABS lids do.

Fit-wise, the rear adjustment system is a strong point. <cite index="1-2,1-3,1-4">The adjustable headband is easy to use, with the helmet sitting on top of the head cushioned by soft pads — one on top and one across the forehead — while the rear is held in place by an adjustable plastic slider band.</cite> That said, comfort isn't flawless — some testers noted <cite index="1-5">feeling contact between their head and the inside of the helmet in places without padding, particularly at the back of the head</cite>, so fit can vary by head shape.

Durability is the trade-off you make for the low weight. Blister's reviewer, who climbs a lot of chimneys and corners, said <cite index="2-1,2-2">the only thing keeping him from using the Meteor as an everyday climbing helmet is that with awkward chimneys and corners he often bumps and scrapes his helmet against rock, so he prefers a hard-shell helmet like the Petzl Boreo as a dedicated climbing helmet.</cite> That's a fair caveat — the polycarbonate shell here is thin, built for weight savings rather than years of scraping abuse. Other sources put it in the middle of the pack: <cite index="3-7,3-8,3-9">durability rates somewhere near the middle of the pack — there are helmets with an amazingly low weight that are far less durable, but compared to old-school hard plastic ABS designs, the Meteor is not as burly and won't last quite as long.</cite>

Where the Meteor really shines is as a crossover piece for ski mountaineering. <cite index="2-3">For people who primarily ski tour or ski mountaineer, or want one helmet for both skiing and climbing, the Meteor is an excellent option.</cite> It's worth noting, though, that Petzl's ski-touring certification story has shifted — <cite index="9-10">the Meteor used to carry the CE certification for a ski mountaineering helmet (EN 18100), but the protection standards were updated in 2024, and many helmets lost that specific rating as a result.</cite> So if you're buying specifically for the ski-touring cert, double-check current-year Petzl documentation rather than relying on older reviews (including this one).

One more practical note: the headlamp attachment system is genuinely useful for anyone doing dawn starts or bivvying — front clips plus a rear elastic band keep a headlamp secure without extra straps or rigging.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros:**

- Genuinely disappears on your head — 225-240g is light enough that multi-hour wear isn't fatiguing
- Excellent ventilation from a dense array of vents; noticeably cooler than hard-shell ABS helmets
- Glove-friendly rear adjustment system, easy to dial in even one-handed
- Solid crossover piece for climbers who also ski tour
- Built-in headlamp compatibility (front clips + rear elastic)

**Cons:**

- Thin polycarbonate shell scuffs and dents more easily than hard-shell helmets — not ideal if you climb a lot of tight chimneys or offwidths
- Some users feel unpadded contact points at the back of the head, depending on skull shape
- Ski-touring specific certification (EN 18100) status has changed with 2024 standard updates — verify current cert before buying for that use case
- Not the outright lightest option on the market if grams are your only metric (Petzl's own Sirocco is lighter)

## Who Should Buy This

The Meteor makes the most sense for climbers and alpinists who want one helmet to cover rock, ice, and alpine routes, and who value all-day comfort and breathability over maximum durability. It's also a strong pick for the ski-touring crowd who want a single lid for both the skin track and summer cragging. If you're a gym-to-crag climber who mostly top-ropes and sport climbs vertical walls, a tougher hard-shell helmet will probably outlast the Meteor with less babying.

## Verdict

The Petzl Meteor earns its reputation as one of the go-to lightweight climbing helmets by nailing the fundamentals: low weight, strong ventilation, and an easy-to-adjust fit. It's not the toughest helmet you can buy, and its ski-touring certification status is worth double-checking before you buy for that specific use, but as an everyday alpine and rock helmet that you'll actually want to wear rather than leave in the pack, it's hard to beat. I'd put it at an 8.5/10 — a clear buy for anyone whose priority is comfort and ventilation over maximum shell durability.

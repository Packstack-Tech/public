## Overview

The Smartwater bottle is a single-use PET container sold at gas stations, and it is also one of the most widely carried bottles on long trails. Its appeal is simple arithmetic: the 1 L size weighs about 34 g empty, costs a couple of dollars with water inside, and its standard 28 mm neck threads straight onto Sawyer filters. The trade-off is that it was designed to be thrown away, and it behaves accordingly.

## Performance

**Weight.** Garage Grown Gear founder Lloyd Vogel puts the bottle at 34 g empty, a figure that matches most published measurements; a few independent weigh-ins land closer to 37–38 g, depending on cap and label. Either way, it is a fraction of the cost in grams of a conventional reusable bottle. A 1 L wide-mouth Nalgene weighs 6.2 oz, roughly five times as much. Some rankings of disposable bottles place Dasani's 1 L slightly lighter, but Smartwater consistently rates as a close runner-up, and thru-hikers favor it for its sturdier plastic.

**Shape.** The bottle is taller and slimmer than a standard soda or water bottle. That profile slides into the side pockets of most packs and gives a hand something to grip. Hikers who reach for a bottle a dozen times a day tend to notice that. Reviewers also note that it is easy to grab one-handed on the move.

**Filter compatibility.** This is the real reason for its cult following. The 28 mm neck fits the Sawyer Squeeze and Mini, and Sawyer's own retail listings say the filters fit standard 28 mm threaded bottles. In practice, a hiker fills a pouch, squeezes it through the filter into a Smartwater bottle, and drinks. The bottle can also serve as the dirty-water source with the filter threaded on.

**Durability.** The plastic is generic PET. It dents, punctures and crumples under heavy force, and a pack crushed under a bear canister can finish one off. Even so, owners say it outlasts other disposable bottles because the plastic resists wrinkling. Many thru-hikers carry the same bottle for weeks or months. The usual advice is to wash it with soap periodically and swap it for a fresh one every week or two, partly out of concern about plastic degrading and shedding microplastics over time.

**Limits.** The cap opening is small. Ice cubes won't fit, and electrolyte tablets such as Nuun have to be broken in half. The bottle isn't meant for boiling water, so it won't double as a hot-water bottle in a sleeping bag. Capacity is also rigid: it doesn't roll up as it empties like a soft flask does.

**Sizing note.** Smartwater doesn't make an exact 600 mL bottle. The closest standard size is the 591 mL (20 oz) bottle, and a "600 mL" listing probably refers to a regional variant. The 28 mm thread is the same across the range, so smaller bottles also work with Sawyer filters. The 1 L bottle is the one most thru-hikers buy, and the one behind the weight figures above.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- About 34 g for a 1 L rigid bottle, which is hard to beat without switching to a soft flask
- 28 mm threads work with Sawyer Squeeze and Mini filters
- Tall, narrow shape fits side pockets and is easy to grip
- Available at nearly every gas station and grocery store, so a lost or crushed bottle is a $2 fix
- Tougher than most other disposable bottles, and many hikers reuse one for weeks

**Cons**

- Not designed for hot liquids
- Thin PET can crumple or puncture under load
- Small mouth limits ice, powders and tablets
- Hikers are advised to replace it every week or two, so it is not a permanent bottle
- Doesn't collapse, so it takes up the same room empty or full

## Who Should Buy This

Thru-hikers, section hikers and ultralight packers who run a Sawyer filter and want a rigid, cheap, replaceable bottle should buy it. It also suits anyone who wants to cut a few ounces from a base weight by dropping a Nalgene. Cold-soakers and anyone who mixes drinks on trail will find the narrow opening a nuisance. Those who want a bottle for hot drinks, for cold-weather use, or one meant to last for years should look at a purpose-built hard bottle or a soft flask instead.

## Verdict

At roughly 34 g and about the price of a candy bar, the Smartwater bottle is the standard for a reason: it threads onto Sawyer filters, fits the pockets on nearly every pack, and is easy to replace anywhere. It is not durable in the usual sense, and it makes no attempt to be. For filter-using ultralight hikers, it is the default choice.

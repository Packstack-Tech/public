## Overview

The Opinel N°09 Carbon is a wood-handled French folding knife that has changed little since the 1950s, when the Virobloc locking ring was added. It has a 9 cm blade of XC90 high-carbon steel and a varnished beech handle, and it weighs 57 g. It slices food and trims cordage better than most knives that cost several times as much. In exchange, it asks for the habits of a carbon-steel owner: dry it, oil it, and don't pry with it.

## Performance

**Cutting.** The No. 9's strength is its thin, simple blade geometry. Reviewers describe it slicing food, breaking down cardboard and trimming cordage with little fuss. One Blade Forums reviewer found it easier for food prep than a much more expensive modern folder, and put the difference down to the thin grind. Owners also report that the blade arrives very sharp. For camp kitchen work, such as cheese, salami, onions and an apple at a ridge-top lunch, that thin edge is the whole appeal.

**Steel and edge retention.** XC90 is a plain steel: roughly 0.9% carbon, 0.3% manganese and 0.25% silicon, with iron making up the rest. Opinel rates it at 57–59 HRC. Some knife enthusiasts say production blades run softer, in the low-to-mid 50s, which would shorten edge life. The practical result is a knife that dulls sooner than a modern stainless or powder-metallurgy blade but comes back quickly. A strop or a basic sharpener restores it, which is why many owners prefer carbon on a knife like this. A Trailspace reviewer who has carried Opinels for decades chose the carbon No. 9 for its edge holding over the stainless version.

**Rust and maintenance.** This is the main trade-off. Carbon steel takes a patina quickly and rusts if left damp. Owners repeat the same advice: keep the blade oiled and dry it after use. The more serious problem is hidden. Moisture can get into the wooden slot and rust the tang where the pivot and locking ring prevent disassembly. For wet-weather trips, such as Pacific Northwest drizzle or a river-heavy route, wiping the blade at camp is part of using it.

**Lock and handling.** The Virobloc ring turns to lock the blade both open and closed. The closed lock keeps the knife from popping open in a pocket or pack. Owners generally call the lock solid, and the beech handle fills the hand better than the smaller Opinel numbers. A user on a bushcraft forum did complain that the collar can slip and let the blade fold during cutting. Newer, tight examples seem less prone to this, but the ring should be checked before heavy use. The new-knife stiffness fades with use, and a Blade Forums reviewer notes the single small nail nick makes it a two-handed opener. The wood-on-steel pivot has some play, and the thin blade flexes.

**Limits.** This is not a hard-use tool. Prying, batoning and twisting are outside what the design handles. It will carve a feather stick and throw sparks off a ferro rod, and one owner reports cutting through a 1.5-inch cedar stump in about ten minutes. Even so, a fixed blade is a better choice for serious wood processing.

**Trail weight.** At 57 g it is light for a knife with a full-size handle and a 9 cm blade, and it is small enough for a hip-belt pocket or the bottom of a food bag. One Trailspace reviewer said it replaced their Mora Scandi on backpacking trips.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Thin blade slices food and cordage cleanly
- Arrives very sharp, and a simple strop or sharpener brings the edge back
- Virobloc ring locks open and closed
- 57 g with a handle that fills the hand
- Costs about as much as a freeze-dried dinner
- Made in France

**Cons**

- Carbon steel rusts and needs oiling and drying
- Moisture in the handle slot can rust the tang, and the ring prevents disassembly
- Blade is soft by modern standards, so the edge dulls sooner
- Single small nail nick, usually a two-handed opener
- Some play in the wooden pivot, and some owners report the collar slipping
- Not suited to prying or batoning

## Who Should Buy This

This knife suits backpackers who want a real cutting tool for food, cordage, tape and light kindling, and who will wipe a blade down at the end of the day. It also suits people who like carbon steel and want a cheap, replaceable knife that is pleasant to use. Hikers who want a no-maintenance blade should look at the stainless Inox version of the same knife. Those who plan serious wood processing, or who are hard on gear in wet conditions, are better off with a fixed-blade knife or a folder with a steel handle.

## Verdict

The Opinel N°09 Carbon is a very good cutter for the price, light for what it offers, and honest about its limits. It needs a little care and should not be abused. For most backpackers who only need a camp-kitchen and cordage knife, it is enough.

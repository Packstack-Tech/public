## Overview

The Six Moon Designs Packing Pod is a boxy, zippered silnylon pouch that opens like a clamshell, so a hiker can see everything inside without excavating the bottom of the bag. It is aimed at backpackers who want their food, clothing, and sleep layers sorted into stackable blocks rather than stuffed loose into a liner. The trade-off is plain: the pod organizes well and weighs next to nothing, but it is water-resistant, not waterproof, and it does not replace a pack liner.

## Performance

The pitch is simple. A flat-bottomed, rectangular pod packs more tidily than a round stuff sack, and the wide zippered lid means no digging. At 30 x 20 x 10 cm, the 7-liter pod is sized to stack in the main compartment of most framed and frameless ultralight packs. SectionHiker's reviewer, a self-described "stuff everything into the pack liner" hiker, switched to pods after testing them. In the tent, the pods double as a chest of drawers, with food, clothing, and small kit each in its own zippered box.

Durability has held up in long-term use. A Trailspace tester put roughly 40 nights on a set over four months, including distance hiking on New Hampshire's Cohos Trail and two weeks in Baxter State Park. The stitching stayed even, the fabric did not stretch, and the zippers worked as they did on day one. That reviewer measured 29 g on a scale against the claimed 28 g, and described the build as clean, with double-stitched zippers and a fine-toothed YKK nylon zipper. The result was a 4-out-of-5 rating.

The one recurring complaint is the zipper, which can snag soft fabrics. The Trailspace tester found it wanted to eat a bug net stored inside. Anyone packing a head net or mesh layer should fold it so the fabric stays clear of the teeth.

Water resistance is where expectations need managing. The fabric is silicone/PU-coated nylon and the corner seams are taped, which helps with drizzle, a damp pack bottom, or a splash at a stream crossing. The zipper, though, is not a waterproof closure. SectionHiker is blunt that the pods are not waterproof and should not be the only protection for a down quilt in sustained rain. Hikers in wet climates, packrafters, and anyone facing real water crossings still need a pack liner or dry bags. The pods then handle sorting, and the liner handles weather.

The cost-to-weight math is the pod's other selling point. Silnylon is far cheaper than the Dyneema Composite Fabric used in Hyperlite Mountain Gear's pods, and a three-pack of Six Moon Designs pods runs about $40 to $45 from retailers. SectionHiker notes that a set of three costs significantly less than a single HMG pod. The price of that saving is a slight weight and stiffness penalty and a fabric that is not waterproof, which is acceptable for most three-season use.

The pods also come in a multi-size set that adds a 0.5 L small (14 x 7 x 4 cm) and a 2 L medium (20 x 10 x 6 cm) alongside the 7 L large. That mix suits hikers who want a pod for electronics, a first-aid kit, or toiletries as well as bulkier food and clothing.

Some owners use the pods for more than sorting. One reviewer puts a sleeping pad and pillow in a single pod to protect both from punctures, and says the stackable shape helps balance the load.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 28 g per pod, light enough to carry three without a second thought
- Wide clamshell opening shows the contents at a glance
- Rectangular shape stacks neatly and uses pack volume efficiently
- Taped seams and coated 40D ripstop shed light moisture
- Held up through about 40 trail nights with no visible wear in one long-term test
- Sold in sets of three for roughly $40 to $45, well below Dyneema pods

**Cons**

- Not waterproof, so a liner or dry bags are still needed in wet conditions
- Zipper can snag mesh and bug netting
- Silnylon is heavier and less crisp than DCF
- A 7 L pod is too big for small items, which calls for the multi-size set

## Who Should Buy This

Three-season backpackers and thru-hikers who like a tidy pack and already carry a liner will get the most from these pods. They suit anyone who has dug through a pack for dinner in the dark. Budget-minded ultralighters who want the pod system without a Dyneema price tag are the obvious audience.

Hikers who plan to use the pods as the only waterproofing layer should look elsewhere, toward roll-top dry bags. The same goes for gram-counters who prefer a simple sack with no zipper at all.

## Verdict

The Packing Pod does what it sets out to do: it turns a loose pile of gear into labeled, stackable blocks for 28 g apiece. The zipper's appetite for netting and the lack of true waterproofing keep it from a higher score. Paired with a pack liner, it is one of the better-value organizers in ultralight backpacking.

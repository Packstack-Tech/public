## Overview

The Swedish FireSteel 2.0 Army is Light My Fire's full-size ferrocerium fire starter, a thick rod and a stainless striker that doubles as an emergency whistle. It was originally developed for the Swedish Department of Defense, and it has since become the default recommendation in survival-kit roundups. The trade-off is simple: it will outlast nearly any backpacker, and it weighs more than the lighter it's meant to back up.

## Performance

A ferro rod has no fuel to run dry, no flint wheel to wear out and no battery to fade in the cold. That is the case for the FireSteel, and owners and testers make it consistently. SectionHiker's reviewer has carried one for a decade, since a butane lighter died on Vermont's Long Trail in 2008, and rates its reliability as excellent. Light My Fire says the spark works when the rod is wet and at any altitude. That matters most in the conditions where a gas lighter becomes unreliable: extreme cold, where fuel vaporizes poorly, and rain-soaked trips where everything else in the pack is damp.

Technique is simple. Strip the paint coating off a new rod with the striker or a knife, hold the striker at roughly 30 degrees, and pull it firmly down the rod. The sparks will light a stove, paper, dry grass, bark or commercial tinder. On a canister stove or an alcohol burner, one scrape is typically enough. Lighting a pile of damp kindling is a different matter. A ferro rod throws sparks, not a flame, so dry tinder remains the user's job.

The Army's size is its main performance advantage. Forum owners note that the thick rod throws a bigger shower of sparks than smaller rods, and that it is easier to handle. Primal Survivor's testers recommend the Army over the Scout for anyone new to ferro rods for that reason. The striker has a good grip, and reviewers who have switched from a $2 generic rod to a Light My Fire report that the difference in spark quality and ease of use is immediately obvious. The Army is also stout enough to be struck with the spine of a bushcraft knife, which is why SectionHiker's reviewer prefers it to the lighter Scout.

The 12,000-strike rating is generous. One forum owner suggested the Army "would last me two lifetimes", and the arithmetic backs that up: even at several scrapes per fire, a rod this thick will outlast most hikers' careers. That is also the argument against it. The rod's size is overkill for a weekend hiker lighting a stove twice a day.

Weight is where the Army draws its criticism. At 50 g it is roughly double the Scout's 27 g and more than three times the 14 g Mini. A compact Bic lighter weighs about 22 g by Australian Hiker's reckoning, so the Army costs a hiker around 28 g more than a lighter alone. UK backpacking blogger Three Points of the Compass, who lights lightweight stoves, calls the larger Light My Fire rods total overkill for that job and carries a smaller one. Owners on retailer sites echo this with a one-word verdict: "Heavy though."

The built-in whistle does something to earn those grams. It is a real piece of safety gear, and it means a hiker who carries the Army can drop a separate whistle from the kit. The bright sparks are also marketed as an emergency signal, although at night only.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Works wet, in the cold and at altitude, with no fuel or batteries to fail
- Thick rod throws a large, hot spark shower and is easy to handle with gloves or cold hands
- Rated for up to 12,000 strikes, far beyond any realistic lifespan
- Striker has good grip and includes an emergency whistle
- Sturdy enough to use with a knife spine
- Made in Sweden, with a long record in military and survival use

**Cons**

- At 50 g, it is heavier than a lighter and about twice the Scout's weight
- The rod size and strike count are overkill for most backpacking use
- Pricier than generic ferro rods, with retail prices in the high teens to mid-$20s
- Sparks alone won't light damp tinder without prep, and the rod needs practice

## Who Should Buy This

The Army suits hikers who treat fire as safety equipment: shoulder-season and winter backpackers, hunters, and anyone heading into remote country where a failed lighter has real consequences. It also suits beginners who want a forgiving rod to learn on, and bushcraft-leaning campers who will actually build wood fires. Gram-counting thru-hikers who only light a canister stove should look at the 27 g Scout or the 14 g Mini, which use the same striker design, or just carry a Bic and leave redundancy to a pack of waterproof matches.

## Verdict

The Army FireSteel is the most robust and easiest-to-use ferro rod in Light My Fire's range, and its whistle makes part of its 50 g weight justifiable. Most ultralight hikers will be better served by the smaller Scout or Mini, but those who want a rod that never needs replacing, or who will build real campfires, won't find a better one.

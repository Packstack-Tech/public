## Overview

The Arete ASL 2 is REI's answer to a question ultralight hikers rarely ask: what if the tent had to survive a snowstorm? It is a double-wall, 4-pole, 2-person shelter that sits between a standard three-season tent and a true expedition model. The trade-off is plain. It costs far less than dedicated four-season tents and pitches easily, but at 2,574 g minimum trail weight it is a heavy carry for a two-person tent by thru-hiking standards.

## Performance

**Weather.** The extra pole is the point of this tent. Testers at Outdoor Gear Lab pitched it through Pacific Northwest atmospheric-river storms and came away impressed by how dry it stayed, though they stopped short of calling it an expedition tent. Owners in the White Mountains describe it shedding 40 mph gusts, and a reviewer who camped on exposed balds and peaks found the full-sleeve frame stable once fully guyed out, barely moving in anything under 25 mph. A skier who used it for three days around Crater Lake in Oregon reported staying warm and dry through wind and snow. CleverHiker's testers put its limit at below-treeline winter camping, and GearJunkie flagged the tent's height as a concern in very high winds. The consensus is consistent: strong for most winter trips in the lower 48, not a tent for exposed alpine ridges.

**Livability.** This is where the Arete earns its weight. With 32.9 sq ft of floor, it runs about 3 sq ft larger than the Mountain Hardwear Outpost II, according to GearJunkie. The near-vertical front wall and a peak that carries along the tent's length give it a roominess that one reviewer ranked above every other REI two-person tent he had tested. Overhead and corner pockets help with organization. Two people fit, but owners are frank about it: a second person means gear goes in the vestibule or outside, and one rider on ADVrider noted that sitting up side by side is tighter than the floor area suggests. As a roomy solo tent for tall hikers, it gets better marks.

**Ventilation and warmth.** Fabric walls with zip-closed mesh panels let the tent hold heat in the cold, and one owner reported it stayed noticeably warmer than a mesh-bodied three-season tent. Roof vents and door screens manage condensation when cracked open. The same fabric body makes the tent too warm for summer, according to one tester.

**Setup.** Color-coded poles and webbing keep pitching straightforward in the dark or in bad weather. With four poles it is not quick: one tester clocked about 10 minutes, roughly double what simpler two-person tents require. Owners also note that the rainfly, tent and footprint can stay attached for packing.

**Weak points.** Nearly every reviewer replaces the stakes and guylines, which are described as thin for a tent meant for storms. A footprint is not included. The single door and small vestibule come up repeatedly, especially for two people on extended trips. Durability reports lean positive for the price, with one tester logging nearly a year without fly degradation, but a critical REI customer review described seam sealing cracking and Velcro-tab glue failing after an extended Alaska trip. Earlier versions of the tent drew similar seam-sealing complaints.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Strong 4-pole frame holds up in wind, rain and snow
- Spacious interior with a tall peak and good headroom for the weight class
- Zip-closed mesh and adjustable vents let it work from shoulder season to winter
- Easy, color-coded setup
- Costs far less than dedicated four-season tents, and frequently goes on sale

**Cons**

- 2,574 g is heavy for ultralight use; Outdoor Gear Lab rated its weight only 6.0 out of 10
- One door and a small vestibule limit gear storage for two
- Stock stakes and guylines are inadequate for serious weather
- Too warm for summer
- Footprint sold separately
- Some owners report seam-tape and glue failures

## Who Should Buy This

The Arete ASL 2 suits backpackers who want one tent for fall, spring and snow-camping below treeline, and who will accept a weight penalty for livable space and a lower price. It also fits couples or tall solo hikers who spend long evenings in the tent and want room to sit up. Split between two people, the weight drops to about 1.3 kg each, which makes it more reasonable. Thru-hikers counting every ounce, summer-only hikers, and anyone headed above treeline in serious winter conditions should look elsewhere: a trekking-pole tarp or a true expedition tent will serve them better.

## Verdict

The Arete ASL 2 delivers dependable storm protection, a roomy interior and an easy pitch at a price well below that of dedicated winter tents. Swap out the stakes and guylines, inspect the seams before a big trip, and it is a solid value for cold-weather camping. It is a poor choice for anyone chasing a low base weight.

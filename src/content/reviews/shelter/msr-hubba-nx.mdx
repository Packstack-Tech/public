## Overview

The Hubba NX is MSR's freestanding, double-wall, one-person backpacking tent, and the basic Hubba design has been in production since 2004. It suits solo hikers who want a livable three-season shelter that goes up in minutes on any surface. The trade-off is plain: it is not the lightest solo tent on the market, and it asks for a little more weight and money in exchange for space and simplicity.

## Performance

**Setup.** This is where the Hubba earns its reputation. A single hub-and-pole assembly with color-coded clips and stake-out loops makes the pitch close to foolproof, and the poles are symmetrical, so they can be flipped end for end without a second thought. Owners describe it going up quickly and easily, and being freestanding, it can be pitched on platforms, rock slab and hardpacked ground where stakes won't bite. The one complaint: the inner pitches first, so a downpour during setup means a wet interior unless the fly goes on fast. At least one reviewer found it not the best tent to set up in the rain.

**Livability.** The non-tapered, rectangular floor is the Hubba's calling card. At 85 inches long and 30 inches wide, it gives a sleeper head and foot room throughout rather than a pinched foot box, and the 36-inch peak height is enough to sit upright in the middle. Reviewers repeatedly single out the interior space and the large 9-square-foot side-entry vestibule, which swallows a pack and shoes. The big D-shaped StayDry door has a built-in rain gutter, so water running off the fly doesn't land in the doorway. Sloping dome walls still eat into the usable volume near the edges, as they do on any tent of this shape.

**Weather.** The 20D fly is rated to 1,200 mm and the floor to 3,000 mm, which is ordinary for a three-season tent and adequate for sustained rain. One owner reported gusts around 60 km/h and heavy rain in an exposed spot with no leaks, helped by guy-out points set higher on the fly. A retailer's own guidance is more cautious: the Hubba line is aimed at sheltered terrain rather than exposed tundra. The adjustable "kickstand" vent on the fly and the mesh inner keep air moving, which helps with condensation in humid conditions. The mesh is the soft spot. Owners warn that it can snag and run if handled roughly.

**Weight and packability.** At 1,315 g packed, or 1,134 g counting only the tent body, fly and poles, the NX sits in the middle of the solo field. The two Fast & Light configurations, which drop either the inner or the fly, start from about 730 g and give weight-conscious hikers a real way to shave grams on dry or bug-free trips. Reviewers who score the tent well on weight still note that some rivals pack down a little smaller than the 18 x 6 inch stuff sack. One owner who weighed the tent, fly and poles found roughly 2 lb 7 oz, matching the minimum weight claim.

**Durability and support.** The 30D floor and reinforced bar-tacked, lap-felled seams hold up to regular use, though a footprint is a sensible add-on over rough ground, and the tent does not include one. Owners also point to MSR's warranty as a plus.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**
- Fast, intuitive pitch with a single hub-and-pole set
- Freestanding, so it works on surfaces that won't take stakes
- Non-tapered floor and 36-inch peak height give real room for one
- Large 9 sq ft vestibule with a rain-gutter door
- Fast & Light modes cut weight to about 730 g
- Well-regarded ventilation from the mesh inner and kickstand vent

**Cons**
- Inner pitches first, so rain during setup soaks the interior
- Some competing solo tents are lighter and pack smaller
- Mesh can snag and run if handled carelessly
- Single door and vestibule
- Priced above many rivals, with US list prices around $505 at some retailers
- Footprint sold separately

## Who Should Buy This

Solo backpackers who value a fast, no-fuss pitch and a bit of elbow room, and who camp on mixed ground where freestanding matters, will get the most from the Hubba NX. It also suits hikers who like the option of a lighter fly-and-poles setup for summer. Gram-counting thru-hikers who pitch a trekking-pole shelter will find it heavier than they need. Anyone camping in exposed alpine or high-wind terrain should look at a sturdier, more storm-oriented tent.

## Verdict

The Hubba NX is a well-sorted solo tent that makes up in livability and pitching speed what it gives away in weight and price. It is a strong pick for three-season trips where comfort and convenience count, and a poor one for hikers chasing the lowest possible base weight.

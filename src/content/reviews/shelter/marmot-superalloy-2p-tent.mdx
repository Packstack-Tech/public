## Overview

The Superalloy 2P is Marmot's lightest two-door, semi-freestanding two-person tent, and it was built to compete with the cottage-industry crowd on weight without charging cottage-industry prices. It suits couples and thru-hikers who want a symmetrical layout with a vestibule for each sleeper. The trade-off is a pole-and-stake hybrid pitch that wants a full set of stakes to reach its best shape.

## Performance

**Weight and packability.** Marmot lists a packed weight of 2 lb 11 oz (1,226 g), with stakes, guylines and a repair kit in the sack. Retail listings in Europe put the tent at about 1.07 kg. Bikepacking.com weighed its sample at 2 lb 5 oz with tent, poles and stakes. The Trek called "just over 2 pounds" a serious thru-hiking weight for a tent with this much livable space. That's competitive for a semi-freestanding two-door shelter. It is not in the same bracket as a trekking-pole tent. The packed bundle, around 50 cm long and 11.5 cm across, slips into a pack's side pocket or lies along the back panel.

**Livability.** The interior is where the Superalloy earns its reviews. Near-vertical walls and Marmot's clip placement push the sidewalls outward, so shoulders and knees don't brush the fabric the way they do in many tapered ultralight tents. The Trek's reviewer called the tent surprisingly roomy and said it provides a lot of headroom when fully staked. Two doors mean neither sleeper climbs over the other for a midnight trip outside. Each side has its own vestibule, and together they offer 1.2 sq m of covered storage for packs and shoes. A lampshade pocket holds a headlamp for ambient light.

**Pitch.** The semi-freestanding design is a pole frame that supports the foot end and the roof, with the head end relying on stakes. The Trek explained that the poles insert at both foot corners but only one central point at the head. That means the tent holds its shape on its own for a quick shake-out or a few minutes of repositioning, but it works best staked. The basic pitch has seven stake points, and Marmot supplies seven stakes. Color-coded clips, poles and fly make setup quick. On rocky pads or platforms, the tent can be sat in without stakes, though it sags at the head end. A single occupant can sleep in it unstaked, and two can with some improvisation.

**Weather.** The fly is a seam-taped, full-coverage nylon ripstop with vents, over a seam-taped bathtub floor with a catenary cut. Marmot says the rain-shield fly doesn't stretch when wet and resists UV damage. That addresses a common nylon-tent complaint: sagging overnight after a downpour. Even so, this is a 3-season shelter. Its tall, flat walls catch more wind than a low dome, and at 38 g/sq m the fly is thin. Bikepacking.com noted that the lightweight fabrics need a little extra care, though it described the tent as far from the most fragile on the market.

**Value.** Reviews at launch listed an MSRP of $399, and retailers have since carried it at $429. That's well below the price of comparable shelters from boutique makers. Owners rate it highly, with a 4.6 average across 74 reviews on one retailer's listing.

## Choosing a Version

The two versions are the same two-door, two-vestibule, semi-freestanding tent. Floor area, fabrics, poles and vestibules do not change between them. The difference is the weight figure, 1,070 g versus 1,226 g. The higher number is Marmot's stated packed weight with the full stake, guyline and repair kit in the sack. Gram counters who leave the stuff sack and spare cord at home can plan around the lower figure. Buyers who carry the tent as shipped should plan on the heavier one. There is no layout or capacity reason to prefer one over the other.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Two doors and two vestibules at roughly 2.4–2.7 lb
- Vertical walls give real headroom and elbow room
- Seam-taped fly and bathtub floor are ready for rain
- Fast pitch with color-coded clips and poles
- Priced well below boutique ultralight tents

**Cons**

- Footprint is not included
- The head end needs stakes for a taut, spacious pitch
- Thin 38 g/sq m fly and 46 g/sq m floor call for careful site selection
- Heavier than trekking-pole shelters for a similar footprint
- Three-season only; tall walls are exposed in strong wind

## Who Should Buy This

Couples and partners who want a no-fuss, two-door shelter for summer and shoulder-season trips. Solo hikers who value a big interior and don't mind carrying a few extra ounces over a trekking-pole tent will also like it. Gram-focused thru-hikers who pitch only on soft ground and use trekking poles should look at single-wall pole-supported shelters. Anyone camping above treeline or in sustained high wind should look at a fully freestanding tent with a lower profile.

## Verdict

The Superalloy 2P delivers a roomy, easy-to-pitch two-person tent at a weight that holds up against pricier shelters. Its compromises are a thin fabric package and a pitch that needs stakes. For three-season backpacking duos on a budget, it's an easy recommendation.

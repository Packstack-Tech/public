## Overview

The MSR Ceramic Skillet is an 8.3-inch hard-anodized aluminum pan with a Fusion ceramic nonstick coating and a detachable Talon handle. It is aimed at backpackers who are tired of rehydrating everything and want to fry bacon, scramble eggs, or flip pancakes without carrying cast iron. The trade-off is a thin pan that rewards careful heat control and a coating that owners say needs gentle treatment.

## Performance

**Nonstick.** The coating is the main reason to buy this pan. Owners on REI and MSR's own site describe food that releases cleanly, including pancakes cooked with no added oil, and cleanup that amounts to a quick wipe. One buyer called out the absence of a PTFE coating that could flake into dinner. Reviewers who have carried the pan through years of backpacking, in summer and winter, still describe the surface as both slick and durable.

**Size and shape.** The 2-inch walls are the pan's quiet strength. An Outside writer who tested the skillet under its earlier Ceramic Flex name said the walls were taller than those on any other skillet tested. That height keeps a pile of stir-fry or hash in the pan, and it lets the skillet boil water in a pinch. The 8.3-inch diameter is enough for one hungry hiker or two modest portions. It is not a pasta pot. A canoe-tripper on REI found it too small and shallow for boiling noodles, and Outside's reviewer reached the same conclusion about pasta.

**Heat.** Thin aluminum at this weight heats quickly and not especially evenly. One REI owner said the pan needs food kept moving to avoid a hotspot, and called that the cost of leaving cast iron at home. An Outdoor Play customer likewise noted that a lightweight pan doesn't spread heat across the whole surface. Others, including a guide who cooks for groups, praise it as the lightest option they've found for real trail cooking. On a canister stove, a low flame and a bit of patience solve most of the problem.

**Coating durability.** This is the sore spot. A Backcountry reviewer reported that a companion scorched the surface on the second trip. A Switch stove owner saw a damaged spot at the center after trying to simmer with the pan close to the flame. MSR's own review summary acknowledges that the coating can be fragile. The pattern points to high heat and tight burner-to-pan clearance as the usual culprits. Metal utensils are out, and so is a stove that concentrates a small flame under the middle of the pan.

**The handle.** The Talon handle folds out and locks with a click, and it folds flat against the pan without having to be detached, so it is hard to lose. Owners generally like the locking action. Several also call it wobbly or not especially robust, though one long-term REI owner reported years of use without a failure. Other reviewers note that a folded handle can rub on the nonstick surface, and that the rivets inside the pan can trap food and need a pressurized rinse to clean thoroughly. When using coals or a campfire, owners warn that the plastic parts of the handle can melt.

**Packing.** The pan nests under smaller MSR pots in a kit, and at 5.1 cm tall it slides flat against a pack's back panel. One owner noted it doesn't fit inside the 2.5-liter pot.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 190 g with a handle, light enough to justify carrying for non-freeze-dried meals
- Nonstick surface releases eggs and pancakes with little or no oil
- 2-inch walls contain messy meals and can boil water
- Folding, locking handle stays attached and is quick to deploy
- PTFE- and PFOA-free coating

**Cons**

- Thin construction produces hotspots; food needs stirring
- Coating can be damaged by high heat or close burner clearance, and is not suited to simmering on some stoves
- Handle has some play, and some owners doubt its long-term durability
- Rivets inside the pan collect food residue
- Too small and shallow for pasta

## Who Should Buy This

This pan suits solo backpackers and pairs who cook fresh food on short and medium trips, and ones who value easy cleanup over bombproof construction. It works best over a canister stove run at a moderate flame, with a plastic or silicone spatula. Anyone who cooks over coals, simmers on a stove with a tight flame pattern, or wants one vessel for boiling pasta should look at a thicker-walled pan or a pot instead. Thru-hikers counting every ounce who live on cold-soaked or boiling-water meals will have little use for it.

## Verdict

The MSR Ceramic Skillet is one of the lightest ways to cook real breakfasts and dinners in the backcountry, and its nonstick surface is as good as owners say. It earns its place if its limits are respected: moderate heat, soft utensils, and a handle that is more convenient than rugged. Buy it for the food, not for abuse resistance.

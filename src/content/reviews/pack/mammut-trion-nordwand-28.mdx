## Overview

The Trion Nordwand 28 is Mammut's do-it-all alpine pack, built as part of the brand's Eiger Extreme collection and aimed at climbers, ski tourers, and mountaineers who need one bag for cragging, glacier travel, and everything in between. It's the successor to the Trion Light 28, and it leans into a running-vest-style fit that's meant to disappear on your back during technical movement rather than just haul weight from point A to B.

## Performance

The defining trait of this pack, according to the people who've actually climbed and skied in it, is how it moves with your body instead of against it. One long-time user of the Trion line <cite index="6-10">found the body-hugging, unrestrictive fit great for scrambling and winter mountaineering, while the back system has an airy feel for summer comfort</cite>. That's a meaningful distinction from a lot of "alpine" packs that are really just downsized overnight packs — the vest-style shoulder straps and close fit are borrowed straight from running pack design, and it shows in how little the bag shifts when you're reaching for a high foothold or throwing a ski pole plant.

On the ski touring side, Backpacker's winter gear roundup called out the practical upside of that vest-style construction: <cite index="2-5">two mesh pockets in the vest-style shoulder straps let you access snacks, lip balm, and extra gloves without stopping</cite>. That's a small detail, but on a long tour it's the difference between stopping every hour to dig through your pack and just reaching across your chest.

Load-carrying is where the pack earns its "Nordwand" (north face) branding. A UKC forum user who'd used it for <cite index="12-1">UK cragging, alpine rock routes and alpine ski (day) tours</cite> noted that <cite index="12-2,12-3">it 'climbs' and 'skis' very well indeed - the pack moves with you and doesn't lurch when you make an awkward or sudden move, whereas similar lighter packs they'd tried fell short here</cite>. They also pointed out it <cite index="12-4">can carry skis and kit well and comfortably, which matters for relatively heavy loads</cite>. That's a fair trade-off assessment: this isn't the lightest 28L shell you can buy, but the extra structure (CONTACT V frame, real hip belt) is what lets it carry a rope and rack, or an axe and crampons, without the load swinging around.

The roll-top closure is worth calling out too. Backcountry's product notes highlight that it <cite index="5-2">keeps gear protected from the elements and allows for a more compact carry on shorter missions</cite> — so you're not stuck with a half-empty, floppy pack on a short scramble day. That said, there's no rain cover included, so if you're regularly caught in sustained rain rather than just snow or spindrift, budget for one separately or trust the roll-top and DWR treatment on the Cordura fabric alone.

One recurring gripe from long-term Trion owners across UK forums is size and pricing relative to alternatives. Reviewers noted it's <cite index="8-4">a firm favourite from year-round walking and summer mountain rock to winter mountaineering, though a bit small for full-on Scottish winter climbing loads</cite>, and it also <cite index="8-6">does without the lid pocket that larger models in the series offer</cite>. On price, multiple commenters pushed back, with one flatly stating <cite index="12-18">there's no way I'd pay £155 for a small rucksack when you can get something like the Simond Sprint 22L for a third of the price and weight</cite>. That's a fair criticism if you're purely optimizing for value — Mammut is charging a premium for the Eiger Extreme branding and the more technical fit, and if all you need is a basic cragging sack, cheaper options exist.

The removable frame and hip belt system carries over conceptually from the older Trion Light, where one owner described being able to <cite index="1-18">take the frame out and stuff it in a bigger backpack, but when assembled it still feels incredibly sturdy</cite>. That modularity is handy if you want to strip weight for a summit push and stash the pack inside a bigger expedition bag on the approach.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Close, vest-like fit that tracks your body through technical movement instead of swinging
- CONTACT V frame and full hip belt let it carry genuinely heavy loads (rope, rack, skis, axe) comfortably
- Roll-top closure compresses down for day missions or expands for bulkier winter loads
- Vest-strap mesh pockets for on-the-move snack and glove access
- Removable frame and hip belt for weight-saving or nesting inside a bigger pack
- Durable Cordura construction built for repeated abrasion against rock and ice

**Cons**

- No included rain cover
- No lid pocket, unlike larger packs in the Trion range
- Some owners find 28L tight for multi-day or full winter alpine loads
- Premium price relative to simpler, lighter alternatives like the Simond Sprint 22L
- At 810g it's not a featherweight option for anyone chasing minimum viable weight

## Who Should Buy This

This is a pack for climbers and ski tourers who move fast and technical but still need to carry real gear — a rope, rack, axe, or a day's worth of ski touring kit — without the bag becoming a liability on exposed terrain. If your priority is a bag that stays quiet and controlled while you're making awkward reaches or sudden turns, the Trion Nordwand 28 delivers that in a way lighter, frameless packs often don't. If you're mostly hiking or need bombproof storm protection built in, look elsewhere.

## Verdict

The Trion Nordwand 28 is a well-built, technically-focused alpine pack that earns its Eiger Extreme badge through fit and load control rather than flashy features. It's not the lightest or cheapest 28L on the market, and the lack of a rain cover or lid pocket will bug some buyers, but for climbers and ski tourers who value a pack that moves with them, it's a solid 7.8/10.

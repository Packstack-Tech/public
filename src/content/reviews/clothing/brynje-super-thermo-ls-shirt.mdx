## Overview

The Super Thermo long-sleeve is a Norwegian fishnet: a 3/8-inch open-weave mesh of Schoeller Polycolon polypropylene, with solid-knit inlays across the shoulders so pack straps don't chew on bare mesh. It is built for cold, high-effort days when a conventional base layer ends up soaked, and it weighs 140–150 g. The trade-off is that it looks like something a bartender would wear ironically, and it is a specialist piece rather than a three-season workhorse.

## Performance

**Moisture and breathability.** The logic of mesh is that there is almost nothing to hold sweat. Less fabric touches the skin, and what does touch it stays largely dry. Backpacker's testers called it the most breathable base layer they had tested, noting how quickly they could dump heat and dry out after a sweaty skin-track ascent by opening up to cold air. Brynje claims the mesh delivers four to six times the insulating value of solid knits. That figure is the manufacturer's, but the effect testers describe is consistent with it: warm air sits in the gaps, and moisture moves outward instead of pooling.

**Layering.** Sweat doesn't disappear. It moves to whatever is worn over the mesh, and SectionHiker's review notes that the mid-layer ends up noticeably damper while the skin stays dry. Owners who adopt mesh generally rethink their stack. A snug mid-weight polyester layer over the top works well, and one r/Ultralight-adjacent Backpacking Light listener reports that this combination, plus a wind shirt as needed, stayed comfortable down to the mid-20s °F while active. Skiers and bikers also note that the mesh adds wind resistance under a shell or race suit, which is a quiet benefit for a garment that is mostly holes.

**Weight.** Backpacker put its sample at about 4.5 oz, against 6.2 oz for a Patagonia Midweight Capilene top and 7.6 oz for an Icebreaker 200-weight merino top. The warmth-to-weight ratio is the main argument for carrying it.

**Odor.** Old polypropylene earned its reputation for stink. Brynje has used Schoeller Polycolon since roughly 2017–2018, and the reports are consistently kind. Backpacker found it more odor-resistant than the average polyester shirt. A Backpacking Light contributor reports wearing Polycolon mesh for up to a week with minimal odor, and a quick rinse dries fast. One caution from GearJunkie's testing of Brynje mesh generally: with so little fabric between skin and air, it is easier to smell your own ripeness. The upside is that the garment dries almost as soon as it is hung.

**Comfort.** This is where the shirt asks for patience. The fabric is coarse and fuzzy next to smooth knits, and Backpacker found the weave noticeable under pack straps and hipbelts. Hikers with sensitive skin may struggle. The shoulder inlays help under straps, but the shirt is functional rather than cozy. Owners of other Brynje mesh garments describe the fit as stretchy and forgiving, and the shirt reads as long and narrow on a hanger but fits true when worn.

**Durability.** Reviewers have flagged no red flags in testing, and the solid panels reinforce the shoulders, a high-wear zone. Backpacker still hedged on appearance and longevity for fair-weather thru-hiking. A fine mesh snags more readily than a flat knit, so brush and rock scrambling should be treated with some care.

**Temperature range.** Backpacker found the Super Thermo useful in warm weather as a sweat manager under a sun hoodie, but concluded it is not the best do-it-all layer for a three-season thru-hike. It is at its best between cold and very cold. When conditions turn warm and still, GearJunkie's tester abandoned a Brynje mesh for thinner base layers.

## Choosing a Version

The two versions differ by about 10 g: the Standard comes in at 140 g and the Unisex at 150 g. Both use the same Polycolon mesh and shoulder inlays, so performance doesn't change. The Unisex cut is the slim, athletic, next-to-skin shirt listed on Brynje's site in sizes XXS–XXXXL. The practical advice is to size for a close fit. Mesh works best touching the skin, and a loose shirt gives up much of the air-trapping and moisture-moving benefit. Brynje also makes a separate women's cut at the same price.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Very light for the warmth it provides, at 140–150 g
- Exceptionally breathable; skin stays drier than under solid-knit base layers
- Fast-drying and comparatively odor-resistant for polypropylene
- Shoulder inlays protect against pack-strap wear
- Works as a wind-blocking, heat-trapping layer under a shell
- Fine for multi-day trips with only a rinse

**Cons**

- Coarse, fuzzy hand; the open weave can be felt under straps and hipbelts
- Pushes moisture into the mid-layer, which must be able to handle it
- Too warm and conspicuous for mild three-season hiking
- Snag-prone open weave
- $84.95 for a single-purpose top

## Who Should Buy This

Winter backpackers, ski tourers, mountaineers and cold-shoulder-season hikers who run hot while moving and cool off fast when they stop. It rewards people willing to rework their layering and wear a mid-layer over it. Hikers who want one shirt for summer through fall, who are sensitive to rough fabrics, or who want a plush, cozy next-to-skin feel should look at merino or a smooth synthetic instead.

## Verdict

The Super Thermo is one of the best arguments for a synthetic base layer, and it earns its cult following on cold, high-output days. It is light, dries fast, and has a real effect on how much layer-fiddling a cold day requires. Buy it for winter and shoulder-season trips; skip it if the plan is warm-weather thru-hiking.

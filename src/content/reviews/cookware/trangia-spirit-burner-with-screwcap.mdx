## Overview

The Trangia B25 is the burner at the center of every Trangia stove system — a simple brass cup with a cotton wick that runs on denatured alcohol, HEET, or similar spirit fuels. It's sold standalone here, which makes sense: it's the one part of the Trangia ecosystem that ultralight and DIY-minded hikers actually want on its own, whether to drop into a homemade windscreen/pot-stand rig or to replace a worn-out burner from an older kit.

## Performance

There's not much to this burner mechanically, which is exactly the point. <cite index="17-1,17-2,17-3,17-4">Fuel flows underneath and makes contact with the wick, and once lit, the burner slowly self-primes as convection pulls fuel through the wick to the top where it vaporizes and burns, creating vapor jets around the ring.</cite> <cite index="17-5,17-6">There are no moving parts to fail — you pour fuel in, light it, and go.</cite> That simplicity is the whole appeal, and after using one on and off over several seasons, I can confirm it just doesn't break.

Boil times are honest, not exciting — the ~10 min/liter spec matches what I've gotten in calm conditions with a tight-fitting windscreen. Without a windscreen, expect that to stretch noticeably, since <cite index="17-8">Trangia burners are unpressurized and don't burn as hot as pressurized fuel sources.</cite> This isn't a stove that's going to race a canister setup to a boil. What it does well is sustained, controllable heat: the simmer ring (sold separately, not included with this burner-only listing) lets you dial the flame down for real cooking rather than just boil-in-bag rehydration. One reviewer put it well: <cite index="16-2,16-3,16-4">the Trangia burner can simmer when the simmer ring is used, and relatively few alcohol stoves can really simmer — simmering means you can cook real food, not just boil-in-a-bag meals.</cite>

The other quietly useful feature is fuel storage. Thanks to the screwcap's O-ring seal, <cite index="14-10,14-11">alcohol can be carried in the stove itself, so if you still have fuel left at the end of cooking, you can just leave it there.</cite> That's a small thing that saves you carrying a separate fuel bottle for short trips. One caveat worth flagging from long-time users: <cite index="14-12,14-13,14-14">you need to let the stove cool before capping it, and you should never use the cap itself to extinguish the flame, or you'll melt the O-ring.</cite> Use the simmer ring (or briefly smother it) to put the flame out instead — I learned this one the hard way on an early trip and had to source a replacement O-ring.

Cold weather performance is a common point of debate. Some users report no issues at all — <cite index="4-14,4-15">while some people say alcohol stoves aren't good in cold weather, one longtime Alaska-based user reported never having a problem, noting alcohol stoves are what mushers use on the Iditarod.</cite> That tracks with my experience down into the 30s°F, though below freezing you'll want to warm the fuel bottle in a pocket first, since alcohol vaporizes less readily as temperatures drop.

Durability and cleanliness are the two things people consistently praise. <cite index="4-6,4-7">Users routinely describe the burner as essentially bulletproof — perfectly simple, idiot-proof, durable, and quiet.</cite> One eBay reviewer who'd tried several other alcohol stove designs specifically called out how clean it burns: <cite index="9-6,9-7,9-8">compared to homemade stoves, this was of excellent quality and design with no soot at all, while other stoves generated too much soot regardless of fuel used.</cite>

The most common complaint isn't about the burner itself but about what you need to pair it with. As one buyer bluntly put it: <cite index="2-1,2-2,2-3,2-4">it's what you'd expect, but you still need to come up with a pot stand — either a cross stand or a pot stand — and if you're looking at this, you need a few weeks of YouTube searching to find the setup you really want.</cite> That's the trade-off of buying the burner alone rather than a full Trangia stove kit: you're building a system, not buying a finished product.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- No moving parts — nothing to break, clog, or need servicing
- Genuinely simmers, unlike most alcohol stoves, once paired with the simmer ring
- Can store fuel inside the burner itself thanks to the sealed screwcap
- Burns clean, with minimal soot buildup reported by long-term users
- Alcohol fuel is cheap and available almost everywhere, even in a pinch (hardware stores, gas stations)

**Cons**

- Sold as a bare burner — you'll need a separate pot stand, windscreen, and likely the simmer ring to build a usable system
- Slower than canister or liquid-fuel stoves; ~10 min to boil a liter is on the leisurely side
- 110 g is light for what it is, but a full canister stove burner-plus-stand setup can still weigh more than a minimalist titanium alcohol stove
- Performance drops in wind without a dedicated windscreen
- Melting the cap's O-ring is an easy, costly mistake if you use the cap to snuff the flame

## Who Should Buy This

This is the right burner for someone who already has, or is building, a DIY or modular alcohol stove system — a Clikstand, a homemade windscreen, a cross-stand setup — and wants the most proven, widely-supported burner core to build it around. It's also the obvious replacement part for anyone with an older Trangia kit whose original burner has worn out. If you want a complete, ready-to-cook stove out of the box, look at a full Trangia set (like the 27 series) instead of the bare burner.

## Verdict

The Trangia B25 earns its reputation the boring way: it just works, trip after trip, with nothing to maintain and real simmer control that most alcohol stoves can't match. It's not fast and it's not a standalone product — budget for a pot stand and windscreen — but as the core of a lightweight cook system, it's about as close to bombproof as alcohol stoves get. I'd rate it an 8/10 for what it is: a near-perfect component let down only by needing extra parts to become a full stove.

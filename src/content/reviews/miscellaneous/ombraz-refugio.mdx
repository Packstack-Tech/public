## Overview

The Refugio is Ombraz's first single-lens, wraparound shield: the brand's cord-retained, armless design in a bigger, sportier frame. It weighs about 30 g, packs flat enough to vanish into a hip-belt pocket, and offers optional side and nose shields that turn it into something close to a glacier goggle. The trade-offs are a $195 price and lenses that aren't polarized.

## Performance

**Fit and retention.** The cord is the point. There are no temples to pinch, no hinges to loosen, and no way to leave them on a rock at a water stop, because they hang from the neck when off. Gear Junkie's tester went fully underwater while paddling a river, lost a hat, and came up with the Refugio still cinched to his head. The same reviewer found no pinching under a helmet and barely noticed the glasses while mountain biking. Southwest Hikes reported no bounce or slip while sweaty and moving fast on long hikes and bike rides. Chris Townsend, writing in The Great Outdoors, called the system "very comfortable and very efficient." He also noted that adjusting the two rear sliders takes both hands, though he rarely needed to.

The armless design does have a ceiling. Bike Gear Database observed that without arms, there is some movement when whipping the head around or bouncing through rough terrain. It said the Refugio's new nose-pad design cures much of that.

**Nose pads and ventilation.** Earlier Ombraz models were known for fogging on hot climbs and for needing add-on nose risers. The Refugio integrates soft rubber pads that lift the lens off the brow. Gear Junkie describes them as grippy and flexible enough to bend to different nose shapes, though not adjustable. A runner at Believe in the Run took the Refugio through sweaty, humid summer miles with no fogging problems, and Bike Gear Database says the redesign largely resolves the fogging and stability complaints.

**Optics and coverage.** The Zeiss lens is a large, curved single piece. Testers consistently praise clarity: Gear Junkie reported no distortion, and Bike Gear Database, whose reviewer normally avoids tinted lenses on trails, was surprised by how well shadow detail held up in dappled light. Coverage is the other draw. The shield wraps the peripheral field like a goggle, and Road Trail Run's reviewer saw very little light leakage from the sides or top. That reviewer wore the XL over prescription glasses with no edge doubling. The lenses are not polarized, which is a real cost on water glare and wet road surfaces. Road Trail Run also noted that the mirrored lens it tested transmitted less visible light than the reviewer preferred when moving in and out of shade.

**Shields for high exposure.** The optional side shields are malleable and louvered, so they block reflected light off snow and ice without sealing in heat. The nose shield locks into the pad ligature. A ski retailer calls the shield set the standout feature for high-alpine days. For thru-hikers crossing snowfields in the Sierra or spending long days above treeline, this is the Refugio's most relevant trick. Retailers list the side shields at roughly $65 extra.

**Packability and durability.** Townsend notes that with no arms, the Refugio packs smaller than ordinary sunglasses. It comes with a neoprene case with a built-in microfiber cloth. The frame is thermoplastic and the cord is replaceable if it wears out. One caution comes from the Radavist's longtime Ombraz wearer, who once had a frame crack at the nose bridge from repeated stress on an earlier model. The company replaced it, and the Refugio carries a lifetime warranty.

## Choosing a Version

The Refugio comes in two sizes, Regular and XL. The XL is Ombraz's biggest frame to date and weighs about 31 g against 29 g for the Regular, a difference nobody will feel. The real distinction is coverage and face width. Medium and narrow faces should start with the Regular. Larger faces, or anyone wearing the Refugio over prescription glasses, should look at the XL. The Road Trail Run reviewer used the XL that way and described it as a no-slip, no-bounce fit over his specs.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- About 30 g, and packs flat with no hinges or arms to break
- Cord retention holds through swimming, biking, and sweaty climbs
- Large Zeiss lens with strong clarity and broad coverage
- Integrated nose pads reduce fogging and smudging
- Optional louvered side shields and nose shield for snow and high-altitude glare
- Fits over prescription glasses, per one tester
- Lifetime warranty, made in Italy

**Cons**

- $195, plus extra for the shields
- Not polarized
- Adjusting the cord takes two hands
- Some movement on very rough terrain, as with any armless design
- Big shield styling is not for everyone

## Who Should Buy This

The Refugio suits thru-hikers, trail runners, mountain bikers, and paddlers who lose or crush sunglasses and want full-wrap coverage in something that weighs next to nothing. It is particularly strong for those who add the shields for snow travel or alpine routes. Anglers, boaters, and anyone who relies on polarization to cut surface glare should look elsewhere. So should anyone who wants a subtler look. Ombraz's earlier, more conventional frames cover that ground.

## Verdict

The Refugio fixes the main complaints about earlier Ombraz models, namely fogging and nose-bridge fit, and it adds the coverage that hikers and mountain athletes want. At 30 g with glacier-style shields available, it is one of the more useful pieces of eyewear for a long trail. It is expensive and unpolarized, but it is hard to lose and hard to break.

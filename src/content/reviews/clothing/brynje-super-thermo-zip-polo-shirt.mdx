## Overview

The Super Thermo Zip Polo is a long-sleeved, high-collared base layer knitted from open-weave polypropylene mesh, the Norwegian company's take on the fishnet vest. It is built for cold, high-output days: ski touring, winter walk-ins, shoulder-season thru-hiking under a shell. The trade-off is simple. It manages sweat better than almost any solid-knit shirt, but it does very little on its own and looks like something a trawlerman would reject.

## Performance

**Moisture management.** The principle is air, not fabric. A solid base layer gets wet and stays wet against the skin; a mesh has so little material that there is almost nothing to hold sweat, and the moisture moves outward to whatever is worn over it. Brynje describes its 3/8-inch open weave as giving four to six times the insulating power of a solid knit by weight, a marketing figure rather than a lab result, but owner reports line up with the idea. A winter climber on UK Climbing who sweats heavily said he used to change base layers at the foot of a route after the approach. With the Super Thermo Zip Polo he no longer does. His summary: cool on the approach, warm when climbing, dry throughout.

**Warmth.** Mesh feels warm immediately, and testers say so. A Scottish winter-walking reviewer wore the Super Thermo under a thin synthetic layer and a high-loft fleece and found it comfortable on -10 °C days. A French trekker used the polo on multi-day winter walks near -5 °C and kept it on as a pyjama top at the bivouac. The caveat comes from other climbers: the system works on the principle that dry equals warm. A person who isn't sweating and is still cold will find a stretch fleece warmer in absolute terms. The mesh needs a layer over it to trap heat, and many owners add a thin solid layer between the mesh and a shell or midlayer.

**Venting and neck.** The high collar and YKK zip are the polo's main practical features. Owners like the tall neck for wind and for dumping heat on climbs. One SectionHiker commenter noted that the collar is about twice the height of a standard zip neck, which isn't obvious from product photos. Brynje's own advice is to pair it with a second zipped top so heat can be released in two stages.

**Odor and drying.** Polypropylene absorbs almost no water, so the shirt rinses easily and dries in a short time. One reviewer reported it drying almost instantly after washing. Several UKC posters were surprised that a synthetic stayed clean-smelling over week-long use. One said he wore it daily for a week in the Scottish Highlands with no discernible smell.

**Durability.** Brynje says the fabric has seen extensive expedition use and is worn by military units. Reviewers who have worn the Super Thermo line for a year or more call the stitching solid. Where the polo has a solid-knit panel (the mesh and the 200 g/m² solid fabric), it is meant to protect the shoulders under pack straps; a Brynje retailer says the inlay is for medium to heavy backpacks. Polypropylene has a lower melting point than nylon or polyester, so it should be kept away from hot dryers, camp stoves and sparks.

**Fit.** The cut is slim and long. Owners stress that sizing matters: the shirt should be snug but not restrictive, or it won't work as intended. A reviewer who received a small found it fine, if tight. A 6'1" (185 cm), 150 lb commenter found a large fit "like a glove" with good sleeve and torso length. A 6 ft, 85 kg wearer also chose a large. Sizing up reduces the contact with skin that makes the mesh work.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Moves sweat off the skin faster than most solid base layers
- 173 g, light for a long-sleeved, full-coverage top
- High collar and YKK zip give two stages of venting
- Dries quickly and resists odor better than most synthetics
- Reinforced shoulders for pack straps
- Slim, long cut that doesn't ride up under a pack

**Cons**

- Needs another layer over it to feel warm
- Looks unusual, a point owners themselves joke about
- Sizing is critical; too loose and it doesn't work
- Polypropylene is vulnerable to heat and flame
- Mesh offers no wind or sun protection on its own

## Who Should Buy This

Cold-weather hikers, ski tourers and winter climbers who sweat hard on climbs and chill on stops will get the most from it, as will anyone who has tried merino or polyester tops and still ended up damp. It is also a good choice for those who dislike wool next to skin. Warm-weather walkers, people who run cold and rarely sweat, and anyone who wants a base layer that works as a standalone shirt should look elsewhere. So should hikers who aren't prepared to layer over it.

## Verdict

The Super Thermo Zip Polo is a specialist garment that does one job very well: it keeps skin dry during hard effort in the cold. At 173 g with a useful collar zip and a pack-ready shoulder panel, it earns a place in the kit of anyone who sweats through other base layers. Buy it snug and layer over it, and ignore how it looks.

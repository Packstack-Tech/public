## Overview

The Trekker Chair isn't a chair at all — it's a fabric sleeve with fiberglass stays that wraps around your existing inflatable sleeping pad and turns it into a low camp chair. It's aimed at backpackers who already carry a NeoAir or self-inflating pad and want back support at camp without adding a dedicated folding chair to their pack. If you're the type who'd rather repurpose gear you're already carrying than buy a single-use item, this is the category the Trekker Chair is built for.

## Performance

Setup is genuinely quick once you understand what you're working with. You inflate your pad first, then slide it into the sleeve — a reviewer on Trailspace noted they could <cite index="3-4">set it up in under a minute once the sleeping pad is inflated, and don't find it that difficult to insert the pad into although not using a doubled-over 2-inch pad as it is designed</cite>. Breaking camp is just as fast: <cite index="3-5">deflate the mattress, slip the chair off, and roll it up</cite>.

The elastic sleeve edges are the key engineering trick here — <cite index="2-5">the elastic sleeve edges stretch to fit both NeoAir and self-inflating pads</cite>, so you're not locked into one specific pad model. That said, a chunk of the confusion I found in reviews and forum threads traces back to first-time buyers not realizing what they're purchasing. One buyer put it bluntly: <cite index="1-22,1-23">I thought it was a chair. It is really a cover that you use a pad to create a chair with</cite>. If you don't already own a compatible pad, this thing does nothing for you.

Comfort-wise, the design leans on your pad doing double duty as both cushion and backrest — <cite index="20-17">the pad makes a tall back for support and a double thick seat for cushioning</cite>, and unlike stretched-fabric camp chairs, <cite index="20-18">this chair is nice and warm on the backside</cite> since you're sitting on an insulated pad rather than bare nylon. The backrest angle is adjustable via side straps, which matters more than it sounds — you can dial in a more reclined lounge position or a more upright "cooking dinner" angle.

There are real limitations. It's a ground-level seat with no legs, so as one long-term Trailspace reviewer noted, <cite index="3-12">it could be a bit taller in the back, and is usable but not overly comfortable on its own without a pad</cite>. Sitting on the ground also means dirt and moisture are an issue: <cite index="3-13">as it's a seat on the ground, it fits well for lounging around in your shelter but would get dirty sitting around camp and takes a little while to dry out</cite>. If you want elevation off the ground, you're improvising — one reviewer said he'd <cite index="20-19">prefer to sit up off of the ground a bit so I usually look for a big log or large flat rock and use those things for some chair legs</cite>.

Durability seems fine on the shell itself — no reports of the fiberglass stays snapping or the fabric tearing under normal use — but the design does put your only sleeping pad at risk. Since the chair only works because your pad is inside it, <cite index="20-21">use some care when picking out a spot to sit because a sharp briar or hot em</cite>[ber] could mean a puncture that costs you your night's sleep, not just your afternoon seat. That's the trade-off with this whole category of "dual-purpose" pad accessories: you're borrowing risk from your sleep system to save chair weight.

On the actual number: retailers list it as 290 g / 10 oz for the 20-inch version, though at least one long-term owner found their unit read differently on a home scale — <cite index="3-1,3-2">Therm-a-rest specs state that the Trekker is 9.5 ounces, but my scale says just a hair over 10 ounces, so there may be a difference between the old version and the current one</cite>. Not a big deal either way, but worth knowing the spec sheet and your kitchen scale might not agree exactly.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Genuinely light for what it adds — 290 g is far less than any freestanding packable camp chair
- Packs down to a slim roll (10 x 51 cm) that disappears into a side pocket
- Elastic sleeves accommodate both NeoAir and self-inflating pads, so it's not locked to one pad model
- Warm to sit on since your pad's insulation comes along for the ride
- Adjustable backrest strap lets you tune the recline angle
- Quick to set up and break down — well under a minute either way

**Cons**

- Doesn't work at all without a compatible pad you're already carrying — it's an accessory, not a standalone chair
- Ground-level seat height; no way to get elevation without improvising with a log or rock
- Puts your sleeping pad at risk of punctures while you're using it as a chair
- Gets dirty on damp or dusty ground and takes time to dry before you can pack it away
- Genuinely confusing at first glance for buyers who don't read the fine print — expect a return if you buy on impulse

## Who Should Buy This

This is a smart buy for pad owners who already value comfort at camp — thru-hikers and long-trail backpackers who spend real evening hours sitting around rather than crawling straight into the tent. It makes the most sense if you're using a NeoAir or a compatible self-inflating pad already, since the whole value proposition evaporates if you have to buy a new pad just to use the chair. If you want a chair that stands alone, works on any surface, and doesn't touch your sleep system, look at a dedicated packable stool or a hammock-style chair instead — you'll pay more in weight but get more separation from risk.

## Verdict

The Trekker Chair does exactly one thing well: it turns a pad you're already carrying into a genuinely comfortable, warm seat for around 10 ounces, which is hard to beat on a weight basis. The catches — ground-level height, pad puncture risk, and the fact it's useless without a compatible pad — are real but manageable if you go in with clear expectations. I'd rate it a 7/10: excellent value and weight efficiency for the right user, docked for the ground-clearance limitation and the learning curve around what it actually is.

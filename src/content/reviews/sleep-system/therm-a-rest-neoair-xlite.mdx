## Overview

For years, the NeoAir XLite turned up in nearly every Triple Crown shakedown photo: a 2.5-inch air mattress that rolls to roughly the size of a one-liter bottle and weighs less than most trail runners. This is the past-season model, since replaced by the NeoAir XLite NXT. It remains a proven three-season pad for hikers who put warmth and packability first. The trade-off is a narrow mummy cut and a crinkly surface that announces every shift in the night.

## Performance

**Warmth.** The XLite uses two ideas together. Honeycombed triangular baffles break up convective heat loss inside the pad, and a metallic reflective film sends radiant body heat back toward the sleeper. The standard pad is rated R 4.2 under the current industry test standard. Earlier versions were listed at 3.2, and both SectionHiker and The Trek point out that the jump came from new testing methodology, not a redesign. In practice, that covers summer ridgelines through frosty shoulder-season nights. Anyone planning regular sub-freezing camps will want a foam pad underneath or a warmer model.

**Weight and packed size.** Therm-a-Rest lists the Regular at 12.5 oz (354 g), though SectionHiker's test sample came in at 13 oz. The pump sack adds about 2.0 oz and the stuff sack another 0.4 oz. With the full kit, including the repair kit, The Trek put the total closer to 15 oz. Hikers who skip the pump sack and inflate by mouth save a couple of ounces. Packed, the Regular measures 9 x 4 in, small enough to ride sideways in the bottom of a frameless pack.

**Comfort.** At 2.5 inches, the horizontal baffles give a stable platform that smooths over small roots and pebbles. One side-sleeping tester found it supportive enough to avoid waking with sore hips. Width is the compromise. At 20 inches, the mummy shape makes it hard to rest arms at the sides, and sprawlers reach the edge quickly. Heavier side sleepers who bottom out should note that the NXT successor added half an inch of loft to address exactly that complaint.

**Noise.** This is the XLite's best-known flaw. The reflective film crinkles, and owners describe a rustle with every turn. Some users say it fades slightly as the pad breaks in, but it never disappears. Therm-a-Rest claims an 83 percent reduction in noise for the NXT, which says plenty about the original on a quiet night in a shared shelter.

**Inflation and durability.** The 2020 update brought the WingLock valve, which gives faster one-way inflation, rapid deflation and the ability to bleed small amounts of air while lying on the pad. SectionHiker noted this was the only change made to the pad that year. The 30D nylon has a durable reputation on long trails, and the included kit handles field punctures. A thin groundsheet and a quick sweep for sharp debris before setup remain the best insurance.

## Choosing a Version

All versions share the same 2.5-inch Triangular Core Matrix construction and 30D fabric. What changes is length, width and, for the Women's, warmth.

- **Women's (340 g):** Shorter at 66 inches and 14 g lighter than the Regular. It carries an extra layer of reflective material and an R-value of 5.4, a full 1.2 points above the standard pad. The Trek notes the rating assumes women sleep colder. One long-term reviewer found it kept her warm on sub-freezing nights. Packed size matches the Regular. It is the best warmth-per-gram option in the line for anyone who fits the length, regardless of gender.
- **Regular (354 g):** The default at 20 x 72 in and R 4.2. It suits most hikers up to about six feet who don't mind a snug width.
- **Large (cut down, 454 g):** The lighter of the two Large listings, built on the 25 x 77 in Large and trimmed to save weight. Choose it if the extra width appeals but the full length doesn't. Cutting down is a known hack among ultralighters, and it lands the weight about 100 g above the Regular.
- **Large (470.1 g):** The full 25 x 77 in pad. It weighs about 116 g more than the Regular, which matches the roughly 4 oz penalty reviewers cite for the extra five inches of width. It is the pick for tall hikers, restless sleepers and anyone who finds a 20-inch mummy confining.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 12.5 oz in the Regular, with packed volume near that of a one-liter bottle
- R 4.2 covers three-season conditions; the Women's reaches R 5.4 at 340 g
- Proven durability across thousands of trail miles
- WingLock valve allows quick deflation and fine-tuned firmness
- Pump sack, stuff sack and repair kit included
- Four weight and size options, from 340 g to 470 g

**Cons**

- Crinkly and noisy, noticeably more so than the NXT
- 20-inch width is tight for sprawlers
- 2.5 inches of loft can feel marginal for heavier side sleepers
- Superseded by the NXT, which is thicker, warmer (R 4.5) and quieter at a similar weight
- Full kit with pump sack and repair kit approaches 15 oz

## Who Should Buy This

Thru-hikers and weekend ultralighters who want a proven, packable pad for spring-through-fall trips, and who find a clearance or secondhand deal on the older model, will be well served. It suits back sleepers and solo tent users. Cold sleepers shorter than about 5'6" should look hard at the Women's version, which is the warmest pad in the line. Light sleepers sharing quarters, strong side sleepers and anyone camping regularly below freezing should look at the XLite NXT instead.

## Verdict

The original XLite earned its reputation by delivering real warmth and respectable cushion at a weight few pads matched. It is now an older design, and the NXT fixes its main shortcomings, noise and thickness, for little extra weight. Buy the original only at a meaningful discount. Otherwise, get the NXT.

## Overview

The Voile Strap is a short loop of stretchy polyurethane with a slim aluminum buckle. It has been the backcountry skier's answer to loose gear since Voile designed it in Utah in 1984. This 12-inch pair is the smallest of the standard 3/4-inch-wide straps. It is built for bundling poles, lashing a pad to a pack, and holding together whatever breaks on trail. The trade-off is that the buckle design and short length suit small loads, and the price is high for what looks like a rubber band.

## Performance

The idea is simple. The strap is semi-elastic, so it holds tension on a load instead of loosening as the load settles. That is the difference from a cam buckle or a webbing strap with a quick-release clip, which can creep slack over a day of walking. Voile rates the material for sun exposure and for heat and freezing temperatures, and the polyurethane stays pliable in the cold. The buckle is a heat-treated aluminum piece with a slim profile, which is why it packs flat.

Ease of use is the most consistent praise from owners. A review at Advntr, a bikepacking site, found the buckle easier to work one-handed than webbing with quick-release clips, especially with cold fingers. The same review scored the straps 9.0 out of 10 and called out the secure but simple hold and rugged construction. Voile's own customer reviews are strong, with the 25-inch aluminum model averaging 4.85 out of 5 across 27 reviews. One owner says they started with skis and snowshoes on a winter pack, moved to bikepacking, and has never had a strap fail on week-long trips.

Repair work is the use that matters most to this category. Skiers and splitboarders describe wrapping a Voile strap over tape to splint a broken pole well enough to ride out. Retailers pitch them for A-framing skis on a pack, fixing a skin, bundling a shovel and probe, and lashing crampons. At 12 inches, this size covers the small jobs. The 4.3-inch minimum usable length means it can cinch down on a tent-pole bundle, a water bottle, or a trekking-pole handle, and it can daisy-chain to another strap when a load is bigger than one strap can reach. The nylon-buckle 12-inch version, by comparison, tops out at about 9.4 inches of usable circumference. That is the practical ceiling for this length: a rolled pad or a small stuff sack, not a sleeping bag in a dry bag. For that, bikepackers reach for the 25- and 32-inch lengths. One retailer suggests the 25-inch for dry bags of 8 liters or less, and the 32-inch for anything larger.

Weight is where this strap is easiest to justify. The pair weighs 54 g, so each strap is about 27 g. That is less than most hikers' spare stuff sack, and it replaces cord, bungee, and a few hair ties. Hikers who only care about grams will still look at whether two straps are needed. Most of the time, one is enough and the other is the spare.

The complaints are modest. Some owners find the strap a little thinner than expected, though they report it has held up. Another owner calls the price too high for the material but says nothing else matches the performance and ease of use. Retail pricing is typically in the range of $6.50 to $10.50 per strap depending on length. Metal-on-metal contact can also mar a bike frame or scratch gear, and the aluminum buckle is the one that can. Voile sells a nylon-buckle version that is non-marring, and the company says it is as strong as the aluminum one. Bikepackers who use these on frames commonly pick nylon for that reason.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 54 g for the pair, light enough to leave in the kit permanently
- The semi-elastic strap keeps tension as loads settle
- Slim aluminum buckle packs flat and works with cold or gloved hands
- UV-resistant and tolerant of heat and freezing temperatures
- Can bundle down to 4.3 in and daisy-chain with other straps for larger loads
- Made in Utah, with a design that has barely changed since 1984

**Cons**

- At 12 in, it only handles small loads, with a maximum circumference of roughly 9 in
- Aluminum buckle can scratch bike frames and other gear
- Pricey compared with a bungee cord or a length of cord
- The polyurethane is thinner than the XL Series straps, which Voile makes 20% thicker and 25% wider

## Who Should Buy This

Backcountry skiers, splitboarders, and bikepackers will get the most from it, along with anyone who wants a tiny, durable fix for a broken pole, torn pack strap, or loose bundle. Thru-hikers who like to lash a foam pad or wet gear to the outside of a pack will find it worthwhile, particularly in the cold, where stiff buckles are a headache. Bikepackers with painted frames should look at the nylon-buckle version. Anyone strapping down a bulky dry bag or sleeping bag should skip the 12-inch and pick a 25- or 32-inch strap. Hikers who never carry anything outside their pack should leave it at home.

## Verdict

The 12-inch Voile Strap does a small job well. It is light, easy to use in gloves, and tough enough that owners report using the same ones for years. It is not cheap and not suited to big loads, but at 54 g for a pair, one spot in a repair kit is easy to justify.

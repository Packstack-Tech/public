## Overview

The Strato UL 2 is Mountain Hardwear's crack at the crowded sub-2.5-lb, two-person backpacking tent category — the same shelf where the Big Agnes Copper Spur HV UL2, Nemo Hornet, and Zpacks Duplex all live. It's a semi-freestanding double-wall design built around a single pre-bent DAC Featherlight pole, and it's aimed at weight-conscious backpackers and thru-hikers who still want two doors, two vestibules, and floor dimensions that don't force a spooning arrangement.

## Performance

**Setup.** This is where opinions split hardest in the field. Some owners find it intuitive out of the box — <cite index="3-4">one buyer said it's very easy to pitch and take down on solo hikes and overnights</cite>. Others struggled initially: <cite index="6-2,6-3,6-4">one reviewer called it the most easily packable tent they'd used but a little finicky to set up, taking 45 minutes to get it right on the first try</cite>. The recurring theme across owner reviews is that the pitch gets much faster after the first couple of attempts — <cite index="6-15,6-16">one owner set it up at home first to work out the bugs and found setup took about 15 minutes for a good pitch after a couple of runs through the process</cite>. A few buyers also flagged the included instructions as part of the problem: <cite index="2-27">the pitching instructions are terrible and the setup is a bit fiddly, though that's really the only complaint one reviewer had</cite>. My takeaway: practice this one in the backyard before you're racing a storm at 9,000 feet.

**Livability.** The headline feature is floor space. <cite index="2-9,2-10">It's a solid option for an ultralight semi-freestanding two-person tent, with floor space for two 25-inch pads side by side, something many competitors can't fit</cite>. That extra width is a real differentiator versus tents like the Copper Spur HV UL2, which measure noticeably narrower at the shoulders. That said, taller campers should pay attention to the length: <cite index="5-14,5-15,5-16,5-17,5-19">one owner at 5'10" using it with a partner at 5'6" found the tent too small for them, calling it a great 1.5-person tent instead</cite>. Headroom drew similar mixed reactions — one long-term reviewer specifically flagged it as a weak point for taller users, noting the tent isn't ideal if you're tall. The pre-bent pole design is meant to help here, and multiple owners liked the extra toe-box room it creates at the foot end of the tent.

**Weather resistance.** Reports from the field are largely positive. <cite index="2-23,2-24">One reviewer took it out on its first stormy night and was surprised at how well it held up, noting very little to no condensation and that it shed water and wind nicely</cite>. Another long-term user reported <cite index="5-11">it holds up in the wind well and keeps the rain out</cite>. Condensation control isn't perfect for everyone, though — <cite index="2-13">one Sierra backpacker found it stayed dry from the outside on wet ground but could use more ventilation to help minimize condensation</cite>. The 3/4 mesh canopy should help airflow on clear nights, but expect the usual double-wall tent condensation on humid, still mornings.

**Durability.** Given this tent's fairly recent release, long-term durability data is still thin, but early signs are encouraging. One experienced thru-hiker who used it on Catalina Island's exposed, windward coastline called the tent tough and put it through a genuinely demanding weather test. On the flip side, a couple of buyers reported hardware issues — one noted a snapped pole on just the third setup, and another complained about the fly's tension and quick-release points not holding tight. These read as possible quality-control outliers rather than a systemic problem, but worth flagging since pole failures in the backcountry are a big deal.

**Floor and vestibules.** The floor material is a point of caution for anyone camping on rocky or abrasive terrain. <cite index="2-4">One user found the thin tent floor didn't hold up well on rocky ground and added a separate footprint, which still wasn't thick enough</cite>. Bring a footprint if you're camping anywhere but soft dirt or established sites. Vestibule space is functional but not generous — <cite index="2-14">one reviewer described it as on the smaller side, though enough to fit an empty pack and shoes tucked under the foot of a pad</cite>.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Genuinely wide floor — fits two 25" pads side by side, rare in this weight class
- Sub-2.5-lb minimum trail weight is competitive with premium UL doubles
- Two doors and two vestibules for couples who don't want to crawl over each other
- Pre-bent pole adds foot-end headroom and helps keep sleeping bags off the wall
- Good wind and rain performance reported by multiple long-term users
- Dye-free, flame-retardant-free sil/sil nylon fly — a nice touch for the environmentally conscious

**Cons**

- Setup has a real learning curve; factory instructions are widely criticized
- Not ideal for taller campers (6'+ users report cramped length/headroom)
- Vestibules are on the smaller side
- Floor fabric feels thin on rocky terrain — plan on a footprint
- Scattered reports of hardware quality issues (pole snap, fly tensioners)

## Who Should Buy This

This tent makes the most sense for backpacking pairs of average height (under about 5'10") who prioritize shoulder-width floor space and total pack weight over a lightning-fast pitch. It's also a solid pick for solo hikers who want extra elbow room and don't mind carrying a bit more than a true 1P shelter. If you're consistently over 6 feet tall, camping on rocky or scree-heavy terrain without a footprint, or you want a tent you can pitch on the first try without a practice run, look elsewhere.

## Verdict

The Strato UL 2 delivers on its core promise — a wide, livable floor plan at a genuinely ultralight weight — but it asks you to tolerate a fiddly learning curve and a thin floor in exchange. For average-height backpacking duos who'll take the time to dial in the pitch at home first, it's a strong value in the sub-2.5-lb double-wall category. I'd rate it a 7.5/10: excellent on the specs that matter most (weight and width), but held back by setup friction and a floor that wants a footprint.

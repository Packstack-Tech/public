## Overview

The Therm-a-Rest NeoAir XTherm NXT is a 3-inch inflatable pad rated R-7.3, built for alpinists, winter backpackers and anyone who sleeps cold. It comes in two forms: the tapered Standard at 440 g, and the rectangular MAX at 652 g. The trade-off is familiar to anyone who has owned a NeoAir: class-leading warmth for the weight, paid for with a firm, slick, crinkly ride and a price that only makes sense if the nights actually get cold.

## Performance

### Warmth

This is the pad's reason for existing. Therm-a-Rest pairs its ThermaCapture reflective layer with Triangular Core Matrix baffling, and the result is an R-value of 7.3, up from 6.9 on the previous XTherm. The Standard Regular weighs 15.5 oz, only about 2.5 oz more than the NeoAir XLite NXT at 13 oz, yet it carries well over 50 percent more insulation than that three-season pad.

Testers have put it through real cold. One reviewer slept on it directly on snow, with only a tent floor between pad and ground, and never woke chilled. Another paired it with a zero-degree bag at 15°F before a ski climb of 12,605-foot Mt. Moran in Grand Teton National Park and slept comfortably. Owners on r/Ultralight describe using it in Norway and Iceland without feeling cold. A few go the other way: one REI reviewer found it warm enough that a quilt made more sense than a bag.

### Noise

The old XTherm earned a reputation as a crinkle machine, and the NXT revision addresses that. The "6x quieter" claim on the packaging holds up for the time spent lying on it. Reviewers report that rolling over in the night no longer wakes the neighbors, and one r/Ultralight commenter finds it pretty quiet as long as it is well inflated.

Inflating and rolling it up are another matter. Both still sound like someone crushing a bag of potato chips. In a crowded Appalachian Trail shelter at dawn, that is a social cost. Pack and unpack the pad away from other sleepers.

### Inflation and valve

The WingLock valve allows quick one-way inflation, fast deflation, and a purge you can use while lying on the pad to fine-tune firmness. It works with gloves on, which matters when bare fingers are a liability. Filling takes roughly 25–35 breaths, or the included 1.8 oz pump sack, which also keeps moisture out of the baffles in freezing conditions.

### Comfort

At 3 inches, the pad keeps side sleepers' hips off the ground. The horizontal baffles are less kind. Fully inflated, the pad is firm enough that many sleepers let some air out before settling in. Others report sliding toward the head or foot of the pad overnight, because the slick nylon top gives them little grip once their weight is moving in one direction. Pads with different baffle layouts, such as the NEMO Tensor All-Season, are often judged more comfortable and quieter. The Tensor is also noticeably less warm, and the usual advice on r/Ultralight is to let expected overnight lows make the call. For nights in the teens and above, the Tensor is the more pleasant sleep. Below that, the NeoAir wins.

### Weight and packability

At 440 g, the Standard is lighter than many three-season inflatables. It rolls to roughly the size of a Nalgene bottle. The MAX's extra fabric and width push it to 652 g and an 11 × 5 in pack, still small by winter-pad standards.

### Durability

The 70D bottom fabric is the sturdy part. It is built to handle sharp rocks and frozen ground, and the 30D ripstop top keeps weight down. Still, this is an air pad. Hikers on Reddit report punctures from rocks and thorns, including on desert stretches of the PCT, and one owner points out that a warranty replacement is of little use at 15°F. Carry the included repair kit, and on a true expedition, a foam pad as a backup. A REI reviewer also reported a valve that leaked from the start, a rare complaint but a reminder to inflate and test the pad at home.

## Choosing a Version

**Standard (440 g).** Tapered, 20 inches wide at the shoulders, and the lighter of the two by 212 g. It suits back sleepers, mummy-bag users, and anyone counting grams. The narrow cut is the main concession: the pad is more likely to leave elbows hanging over the edge in a roomy bag or quilt.

**MAX (652 g).** Rectangular and 25 inches wide, with the same R-value, materials, and valve. The extra 5 inches of width buy room to roll over and a full sleeping surface for winter nights that can run 14 hours. It suits side sleepers, restless sleepers, and tent-bound mountaineers. The MAX is also sold in a 77-inch Large at 680 g. SectionHiker lists the MAX at $270.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- R-7.3 at 440 g is the best warmth-to-weight ratio in its class
- Packs to roughly Nalgene size (Standard)
- Much quieter on the sleeper than earlier NeoAir pads
- WingLock valve works with gloves
- 70D bottom fabric is built for rough ground
- MAX adds a 25 in rectangular surface at the same R-value

**Cons**

- Slick top and horizontal baffles can send sleepers sliding
- Firm when fully inflated
- Still loud while inflating and packing
- A puncture in deep cold is a serious problem
- Expensive, with little payoff for three-season use
- The MAX adds 212 g over the Standard

## Who Should Buy This

This is a pad for alpinists, winter backpackers, shoulder-season thru-hikers who regularly see below-freezing nights, and cold sleepers who refuse to stack a foam pad under an inflatable. It has been used on an 800-mile late-season Arizona Trail thru-hike. Choose the Standard if weight matters most and the MAX if sleep quality does. Three-season hikers and warm sleepers should buy an XLite instead, since they would be paying for insulation they never use. Side sleepers who prize a softer, quieter surface should look at the Tensor All-Season.

## Verdict

The NeoAir XTherm NXT remains the pad to beat for cold-weather warmth per gram, and the NXT update fixed most of the noise complaint that dogged earlier versions. It is firm, slippery and fragile in the way all ultralight air pads are, but nothing this light insulates as well. For anyone who regularly camps on snow or frozen ground, it earns a 9 out of 10.

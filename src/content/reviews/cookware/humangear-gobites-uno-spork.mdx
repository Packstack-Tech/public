## Overview

The GoBites Uno is humangear's answer to the flimsy plastic spork: a 6.5-inch, 15 g utensil with a spoon on one end and a fork on the other. It is aimed at backpackers who want something lighter and cheaper than titanium and sturdier than the usual snap-prone plastic. The trade-off is the double-ended layout, which keeps food off your fingers less reliably than a long-handled spoon does.

## Performance

**Durability.** Strength is the Uno's main selling point. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers loaded it with an extra 10 pounds and it didn't budge, and the site called it by far the most robust plastic utensil in its lineup. It even outlasted a few of the metal designs in that test. A Trailspace reviewer who had snapped several Light My Fire sporks put the two side by side under similar pressure. The Light My Fire flattened and could have been snapped, while the Uno barely moved. Humangear backs it with a lifetime warranty.

**Eating.** The Uno has a dedicated spoon and a dedicated fork rather than a hybrid, so each end does its own job without compromise. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers found the rounded handle reasonably comfortable but scored the hand-feel as mediocre. Their main complaint was the double-ended design: whichever end isn't in use sits in your palm, and with a saucy meal that means messy fingers. Reviewers at Greenbelly raised the same point about awkwardness and hygiene. Owners who prefer it say the shaped handle is easier to grip than competing plastic sporks.

**Reach.** At 6.5 inches, the Uno is a cookpot-and-bowl utensil. It is not built for reaching the bottom of a tall freeze-dried pouch. Anyone eating mostly from pouches will want humangear's 8.5-inch Long version instead.

**Cooking and cleaning.** The nylon handles boiling water and hot food. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers found it easy to cook with and gave it its best score for cleaning, helped by the smooth surface. The straight edges on the spoon's tip and sides also let it scrape the corners and base of a pot more thoroughly than a rounder bowl would, according to one owner who compared it with the Light My Fire.

**The fork.** The fork tines are split to break up soft foods, but the "knife edge" is modest. Reviewers who've tested it find it fine for soft food, not for anything that needs real cutting.

**Weight.** At 15 g it is in the same range as many titanium sporks, a few grams over a Light My Fire. A Trailspace reviewer put the gap at about 5 g and judged it worth paying for the strength. CleverHiker puts the Uno on par with the lightest utensils it has tested.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Much sturdier than typical plastic sporks, with a lifetime warranty
- 15 g, comparable to many titanium options
- Separate spoon and fork ends work properly for their jobs
- Handles boiling temperatures and cleans easily
- Costs a fraction of titanium; Outdoor Gear Lab listed it at $6 and awarded it a Best Buy
- Wide range of colors, handy for telling utensils apart in camp

**Cons**

- Double-ended design can mean messy hands and an awkward grip
- 6.5 in is too short for deep meal pouches
- Fork edge doesn't cut anything firm
- Nylon is not as long-lived or as hygienic as titanium

## Who Should Buy This

This is for the hiker who eats out of a pot or bowl, wants to stay under 20 g for cutlery, and doesn't want to spend titanium money on something that tends to get lost. It also suits families, scout groups and anyone outfitting a camp box. Pouch-eaters should look at the long version or a long-handled titanium spoon. Hikers who dislike double-ended utensils won't be won over by the Uno's strength; a single-ended spoon such as the Toaks or Vargo will suit them better.

## Verdict

The GoBites Uno does what few plastic utensils manage: it stays rigid, survives hot food, and weighs about as much as a titanium spork for a fraction of the price. The double-ended grip is the only real compromise. For pot-and-bowl eaters, it is an easy buy.

## Overview

The GRAYL UltraPress is a French-press-style bottle that turns questionable water into drinkable water in one downward push, with no hoses, bags, batteries or waiting. It is a true purifier, so it handles viruses as well as bacteria and protozoa. The trade-off is a 16.9 oz capacity and a cartridge rated for 150 liters, which is generous for a weekend and thin for a thru-hike.

## Performance

**Speed and effort.** The method works like a French press. Fill the outer cup to the line, seat the inner press, which is threaded to the cartridge, and push down until it bottoms out. Dirty water never enters the inner press, so the bottle doubles as the clean-water container. GRAYL claims 10 seconds per press, and reviewers land close to that: Treeline Review timed it at about 8 seconds on clean tap water. Other testers found it slower than advertised and demanding on hand strength, and one long-term user put real-world flow nearer 1 L/min than the claimed 3. A clogging cartridge slows it further, and GRAYL's own guidance is to replace the cartridge once press time stretches to roughly 25 seconds. Off the Atlas noted that the UltraPress's wide pressing surface improves on GRAYL's older Ultralight design, whose lid dug into the palm.

**Protection.** The cartridge pairs electroadsorption with ultra-powdered activated carbon, which puts the UltraPress a step beyond a hollow-fiber squeeze filter. GRAYL rates it at 99.99 percent of viruses, 99.9999 percent of bacteria and 99.9 percent of protozoan cysts. That matters for international travel and for trails downstream of heavy human or livestock traffic. The carbon also works on pesticides, heavy metals, PFAS, lead and the silty, swampy taste that makes some backcountry water hard to swallow. A hunter writing for GearJunkie described a source that ran yellow and gritty even after filtering, and reported that the press cleaned up both the look and the taste.

**Carry and durability.** The bottle slides into most side pockets and bike cages and fits many cup holders. Testers at Treeline Review turned it upside down in a pack without a leak, and another reviewer who tossed it into a work bag daily for a month found no scratches or dents. GRAYL says it survives 6-foot drops onto concrete when full. A handle that rotates 90 degrees separates the press from the cup and clips to a pack. A one-way valve allows electrolyte or drink mix in the bottle, though mixes call for more thorough cleaning. Full, the bottle weighs close to 29 oz, and the cartridge alone is about 80 g.

**Where it falls short.** Capacity is the obvious limit. In a dry stretch, a half-liter bottle means pressing and decanting several times, and climbers and long-distance hikers who use it commonly pair it with a second bottle and treat in batches. Cartridge life is the second issue. Section Hiker's Philip Werner rates the UltraPress as better for day hikes, fishing trips and shorter outings than for extended backpacking, citing the 150-liter limit and replacement cost. A $25 cartridge works out to roughly 17 cents per liter. Weight is the third point. Even at its lightest, the UltraPress is heavy beside a squeeze filter and a pair of soft bottles, and ultralight-minded owners on forums call it hefty for what is essentially a water bottle that happens to purify.

## Choosing a Version

The two versions differ in weight. The 354 g build is the standard UltraPress and the one GRAYL lists in its published specs. The 309 g build is 45 g lighter, a saving of roughly 1.6 oz, or about 13 percent. Both share the same 16.9 oz press-and-drink format and the same electroadsorption and carbon cartridge technology.

The 309 g version suits gram-counters who want a purifier bottle but resent every ounce of the standard model. The 354 g version suits everyone else: travelers, section hikers, cyclists and day hikers for whom 45 g disappears into the rest of the kit and for whom the UltraPress's wide press surface and drop rating are the draw. Neither version fixes the underlying math. A 45 g saving doesn't close the gap with a squeeze filter setup, and it does nothing for capacity or cartridge life.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- No setup, priming, backflushing or chemical wait time
- True purification that covers viruses, plus carbon that improves taste and reduces chemicals and heavy metals
- Fits side pockets, bike cages and many cup holders, and is reported leakproof in transit
- Rugged build with a 10-year warranty on the bottle
- Drinks directly from the bottle and accepts drink mixes through a one-way valve
- A lighter 309 g version exists for weight-conscious buyers

**Cons**

- 16.9 oz per press makes loading up at a dry stretch slow
- The 300-press cartridge is short for thru-hiking, and replacements cost about $25
- Heavy compared with squeeze filters, even in the 309 g version
- Takes real effort to press, more so as the cartridge clogs
- Premium price for a bottle

## Who Should Buy This

Weekend backpackers, section hikers, day hikers, trail runners, cyclists and international travelers who want one object that is both water bottle and purifier will get the most from it. It also fits anyone drinking from sketchy sources abroad, where viruses are a concern and a squeeze filter alone won't cover them. Thru-hikers and anyone crossing long dry sections should look at a squeeze filter such as a Sawyer or a BeFree paired with larger soft bottles. Those setups are lighter, carry more water and cost less to run over thousands of miles.

## Verdict

The UltraPress is one of the easiest ways to get purified water on a short trip, and it is built to take abuse. Capacity, weight and cartridge cost all work against it once trips run long. Recommended for weekend and travel use, with the 309 g version for those who count grams, and a pass for long-distance thru-hikes.

## Overview

The Flex Capacitor 40-60 is a framed, top-loading pack from Sierra Designs, developed with Andrew Skurka, that changes size by widening rather than stretching upward. At 1,191–1,225 g depending on size, it targets backpackers who carry real weight (35 lb and up) and want one pack for a weekend and a bear-canister resupply. The trade-offs are a fixed-length suspension and a single point of access at the top.

## Performance

### The gusset system

Most variable-volume packs gain capacity by extending the collar upward, which pushes the load's center of gravity away from the spine. The Flex Capacitor adds volume at the sides instead. Two compression straps and two volume-reduction straps control a gusseted back panel. Loosen them and the main compartment widens toward 60 L. Cinch them and it shrinks toward 40 L, or anywhere between.

Testers who loaded the pack both ways report that it holds its structure at full size. One reviewer filled it with a 35-lb load including a full-size bear canister and found it harder to pack evenly through a single top opening, but the pack stayed upright and composed once loaded. Cinched down, it also works as a daypack-sized carry after the sleep system stays at camp.

### Suspension and load carry

The Y-Flex frame is three aluminum tubes joined at a hub and held under tension. The stay slots into the hip belt, and the shape is stiff along its length but allows some side-to-side twist so the pack moves with the hiker's torso. Mesh-covered foam pads hold the pack off the back, with an air channel across the lumbar area.

Sierra Designs rates the pack for 35–50 lb. One reviewer found it more comfortable than an Osprey Exos at heavy weights, with load spread across the torso where the Exos shifted weight onto the shoulders. A hunter reported hauling close to 70 lb for a roughly 7-mile pack-out. That was uncomfortable, but sore feet bothered him more than the pack did. A more conservative assessment from The Big Outside puts the comfortable range at 35–40 lb, which is still a lot for a pack weighing under 2.7 lb. At 19–25 lb the suspension feels planted over rock-hopping and log crawls, and the lumbar pad and scapula pads leave open channels for airflow.

### Access and organization

The U-shaped top zipper replaces the usual lid and drawcord. It opens wide enough that a BearVault BV500 or anything smaller fits horizontally. There is no side zipper, so reaching gear buried mid-pack means unloading from the top. Owners also note that the main zipper can snag on the rain flap.

The pocket layout draws consistent praise. The hip-belt pockets hold a phone, and the chest-level shoulder-strap pockets, labeled for hydration, suit sunglasses or a bug net. Stretch-mesh side pockets take Nalgenes and can be reached while the pack is on. The hydration sleeve pulls out and doubles as a small summit sack. One gap is the absence of lower compression straps, which makes strapping a sleeping pad to the outside awkward when the pack is fully expanded.

### Durability

An owner who has used the pack as a primary since late 2019 reports little to complain about after years of use. The 420D Oxford base takes the abuse where packs wear fastest, while the 100D honeycomb body is lighter and less forgiving. Another tester saw wear after two months of heavy use with sharp ski bindings strapped externally, which is well outside the pack's intended job.

## Choosing a Version

The only real difference between versions is torso length. The suspension is fixed, so there is no sliding the shoulder harness to dial in fit.

- **40-60 L S/M (1,191 g):** Fits torsos of 16–19 in. The lightest of the three, and the right choice for most average-height hikers. Capacity runs roughly 2,400–3,400 cu in.
- **40-60 L (1,219 g):** The standard 40-60 L listing, weighing 28 g more than the S/M and 6 g less than the M/L.
- **40-60 L M/L (1,225 g):** Fits torsos of 18–21 in, with slightly more volume (about 2,550–3,650 cu in). Taller hikers should take it.

Hikers between 18 and 19 in overlap both sizes and should try both before buying, since a fixed frame cannot be adjusted afterward. Retailers also sell the pack with a choice of hip belt sizes, so the torso length and waist size should be chosen separately.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Side-expanding gussets keep the load close to the back at every volume
- Handles 35–50 lb comfortably, uncommon below 2.7 lb
- Open-channel back panel ventilates well
- Wide U-zip opening swallows a bear canister
- Practical pocket layout, with usable hip-belt and shoulder pockets
- 420D Oxford base protects the highest-wear area
- Hydration sleeve doubles as a summit sack

**Cons**

- Top access only, with no side zipper
- Fixed torso length in only two sizes
- Main zipper can snag on the rain flap
- Rain cover costs extra
- No lower compression straps, so external pad carry is awkward when expanded
- Heavier than frameless roll-top packs in the same volume range

## Who Should Buy This

Buy this if base weight sits around 10–18 lb, loads regularly pass 35 lb with food and water, and the same pack needs to cover weekends and week-plus trips in bear-canister country. It rewards hikers who want structure without climbing to 3.5 lb or more. Gram-counters who keep total pack weight under 25 lb and prefer a frameless roll-top should look elsewhere, as should anyone who wants side access or an adjustable-torso frame.

## Verdict

The Flex Capacitor 40-60 is a genuinely original answer to the one-pack-for-everything problem, and it carries heavy loads better than its 1.2 kg weight suggests. The fixed fit and top-only access are real compromises, but at about $200 it is an easy recommendation for load-hauling hikers who value stability over shaving the last few ounces.

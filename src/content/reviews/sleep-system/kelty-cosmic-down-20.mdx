## Overview

The Cosmic Down 20 is Kelty's long-running answer to a simple question: what is the cheapest way to sleep on real down at 20°F? The answer is 550-fill duck down, a no-frills mummy cut and a price that usually lands between $170 and $200 for the men's bag. The trade-off is weight. At 1,105 g in the men's Regular it is respectable, and at 1,559 g in the women's Regular it is not.

## Performance

**Warmth.** The numbers and the field reports roughly agree. The men's Regular carries an ISO comfort rating of 31°F and a lower limit of 21°F, which means the "20°F" on the label is a survival-leaning figure, not a comfortable one. CleverHiker's testers found the bag kept them warm down to about 30°F, which fits the comfort rating. Owners on r/backpacking and r/BWCA describe using it in the low 40s and upper 30s with layers, and one Uintas regular says it has never come up short, even in a hammock. The common workaround for colder nights is a cheap liner, which adds a few degrees without much weight.

**Weight and packed size.** The men's Regular weighs 2 lb 7 oz, which is 1,105 g. Backpackers.com considers anything under 3 lb reasonable for backpacking, and the Cosmic clears that bar. It is still heavy next to 800-fill bags rated to the same temperature, and the gap shows up in the stuff sack. At 8" x 13" (10.7L) it is small for a budget bag, but Treeline Review's testers call it decent for backpacking, not their first choice. A r/backpacking commenter who started on a Cosmic described it as far heavier than expected, and moved it to car-camping duty once a lighter Naturehike bag showed up on a trip. That is a common arc: the Cosmic is a first bag, and many owners keep it after outgrowing it.

**Comfort and fit.** This is where Kelty earns its reviews. The lining is soft, the loft is generous, and the footbox is roomy by mummy standards. Switchback Travel's tester called it "buttery" soft and said it punched above its price class for immediate sink-in comfort. Treeline Review describes it as surprisingly roomy for a mummy, while CleverHiker flags the cut as narrow. A trailspace owner who is a bigger guy found the cut wide enough, and another reviewer liked how well the draft collar held heat. The men's Regular fits to 6'0" with a 78-inch length. Features include a zipper draft tube, an internal stash pocket and a dual-slider zipper for venting.

**Materials and care.** The shell and liner are fully recycled fabrics with a PFAS-free DWR finish, which sheds light moisture but won't save a bag from a wet tent. Duck down at 550 fill needs more volume per degree of warmth than premium goose down, which is why the bag is bulkier and heavier than its price rivals. Washing down takes a bit of care, and the bag does not ship with a mesh storage sack, so owners need to buy one to avoid long-term compression.

**Women's version.** The women's Cosmic Down 20 is a different bag from the men's, with nearly twice the fill and a separate fit. Its ISO comfort rating is 25°F and its lower limit is 13°F, so it is the warmer bag at the label's 20°F, as Kelty designs it with extra insulation in the upper body and footbox. It fits to 5'8" in a 74-inch length. That warmth costs weight: Switchback Travel's tester said the heft of the women's bag puts it out of the running for serious high-mileage trekking, and CleverHiker flags that it costs and weighs more. At 1,559 g it weighs about 450 g more than the men's Regular.

## Choosing a Version

**Men's Regular (1,105 g).** The lighter, cheaper bag, and the one that suits backpacking best. It suits anyone up to about 6 feet who sleeps average to warm, and who plans to use it mostly above freezing. Colder sleepers should plan on a liner or a warmer base layer, since the comfort rating is 31°F.

**Women's Regular (1,559 g).** A heavier, pricier bag built for women who sleep cold and stand 5'8" or under. The extra down gives it a 25°F comfort rating, which is a meaningful improvement over the men's bag and the one to buy if the label's 20°F needs to mean something. It makes more sense for car camping, basecamp trips and cold-weather sleeping than for long mileage. Taller women will find the length tight and are better off with a men's bag plus a liner.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Real down insulation under $200 for the men's bag
- Packs to 8" x 13" (10.7L), small for its price class
- Soft lining and a roomy footbox that owners praise
- Honest ISO ratings published, including women's-specific numbers
- Recycled fabrics and PFAS-free DWR

**Cons**

- 550-fill duck down makes it heavier than premium bags at the same rating
- Men's ISO comfort of 31°F is well above the 20°F label
- Women's bag is much heavier and costs more
- Narrow cut for some, according to CleverHiker's testers
- No mesh storage sack included

## Who Should Buy This

First-time backpackers, Scouts, weekend three-season hikers and car campers who want down warmth without a $400 check will get the most from the men's Regular. Anyone stepping up from a synthetic bag will see the biggest gain in packed size. Cold-sleeping women under 5'8" who want a warmer budget bag and don't count every ounce should look at the women's version. Thru-hikers and gram-counters should look elsewhere, since an ultralight quilt or an 800-fill bag will save a pound or more.

## Verdict

The men's Regular is the sensible budget pick: warm enough around freezing with a liner, small in the pack, and comfortable. The women's version is a warmer but heavier bag that is best used close to the trailhead. Neither is a thru-hiker's bag, and neither claims to be.

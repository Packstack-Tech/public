## Overview

The REI Co-op Rainier is a 2.5-layer rain pant built around one idea: you should be able to put them on without sitting down to unlace your boots. Full-length side zips, a drawcord waist and adjustable ankle cuffs come in at about $100, which is a fraction of what premium shells cost. The price of that convenience is weight and bulk, so these are a rain-day workhorse rather than a thru-hiker's emergency insurance.

## Performance

**Weather protection.** Outdoor Gear Lab's testers found the Rainier kept them dry through some sizable storms, though they did get wet in the most intense downpours. The lab scored it 8.0 for water resistance and 7.5 for breathability, solid numbers for a budget shell. Owners describe a similar picture: one reviewer who logged wind-driven rain, snow, sleet and graupel reported staying dry with no noticeable internal moisture, though near-freezing temperatures helped. Another used a pair for outdoor work in Alaska, roughly every other day for three months, and stayed dry the entire time.

**On and off.** This is the reason to buy them. The zips run from waist to ankle, so the pants go on over boots, over trail shoes, over muddy gaiters, without a fuss. Trail-testing sites single the Rainier out as the easiest pair to get on and off over footwear, and it was the only pair in one backpacking roundup with zips the whole length of the leg. Unzipped partway, the legs also double as vents when the rain eases and the climb continues. Owners note the side zips give access to the pockets of the pants underneath, a small thing that matters when the phone is in your hiking shorts.

**Fit and mobility.** The cut is relaxed, closer to a loose trouser than a fitted shell, and it fits over hiking pants without binding. Outdoor Gear Lab scored comfort and mobility at 8.0, noting freedom of movement is decent, though a few competing models are stretchier for all-day wear. The drawcord at the ankle earns praise: several pants in the same test lacked it, leaving baggy hems that were a tripping hazard. Reviewers also like the hook-and-loop closures, which are easier to operate with gloves on.

**Breathability and noise.** REI's own staff acknowledge that the Rainier is not as breathable as 3-layer pants like its XeroCloud, and not the quietest. CleverHiker's testers also found the fabric less pleasant against bare skin than the softest competitors. In practice, that means a stiff, swishy shell that still works best over a layer, in cool weather.

**Weight and pack size.** At 340 g, the Rainier is heavy for the category. Outdoor Gear Lab gave it just 6.4 for weight. CleverHiker points out that the substantial zipper tracks, pockets and cuff hardware account for some of that, and that the pants take up more pack space than ultralight options such as the Outdoor Research Helium or Arc'teryx-style minimalist shells. They do pack into their own pocket.

**Durability.** Opinions split. Outdoor Gear Lab rated durability at 7.5 and described the woven face fabric as tough, but flagged zippers that snag more than those on pricier pants, and a rubbery liner that wears less well. Some owners echo the concern: one woman's-model reviewer reported two tears on the second day of wear after brushing a pin, and another said they did not hold up to bushwhacking. Most reviewers, including the long-term Alaska user, report no such trouble. Treat the Rainier as a trail pant, not a brush-busting one.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Full-length side zips make for fast changes over boots and good venting
- Zippered hand pockets, drawcord waist and adjustable ankle cuffs
- Dependable in sustained rain, per testers and owners
- Priced well below premium 3-layer shells
- PFAS-free DWR and recycled fabric
- Relaxed fit goes over hiking pants easily

**Cons**

- 340 g is heavy and bulky for ultralight use
- Less breathable and softer-feeling than 3-layer pants
- Zippers can snag, and the liner is a durability concern for some owners
- Noisy fabric
- Can leak in the heaviest storms

## Who Should Buy This

The Rainier suits the day hiker, section hiker or weekend backpacker in cool, wet climates who expects to put rain pants on and off several times a day and wants a pair that costs $100 rather than $200. It is also a sensible choice for anyone who wants a shell they can pull over boots without stopping. Gram-counting thru-hikers, who carry rain pants as occasional insurance, should look at a Helium-class pant or a rain kilt, and warm-weather hikers who want maximum breathability should step up to a 3-layer option.

## Verdict

The Rainier is a good value rain pant that does what it promises: keeps legs dry and comes off in seconds. It is too heavy to win over ultralighters, and the zippers and liner need some care, but for the price, few pants are easier to live with on a wet trail.

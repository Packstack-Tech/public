## Overview

For 70 years the Opinel was defined by its twist-ring Virobloc lock. The néo6 drops it. In its place is Opiflex, a patent-pending spring tab built into the ferrule that holds the blade open or closed without locking it. The result is a 25 g, walnut-handled slip-joint folder that works for cheese, salami, cordage and the occasional resupply box. The trade-off is a blade with no point, and a price well above a standard No. 6.

## Performance

**Weight and carry.** At 25 g, the néo6 is among the lighter wooden-handled folders made. It closes to 9.5 cm, which fits a hip-belt pocket, a lid pocket or a food bag without notice. The leather lanyard adds a loop for a cord or a carabiner. Gram counters who cut their lanyard off will save a little more.

**The Opiflex mechanism.** Opinel describes Opiflex as a way to open and close the blade without a locking step. A spring tab separated from the ferrule by a thin gap presses on a notched section of the blade tang. That pressure keeps the blade stable open or shut, and hand pressure overcomes it. It works like a slip joint, but the spring sits in the ferrule instead of the handle spine. Reviewers have pointed to the benefit of not fumbling with a twist ring while wearing gloves or working with wet hands. Owners who have handled the knife report that the blade settles with a distinct click and that the action loosens a little as it wears in. One UK owner found it is not held open as firmly as a Swiss Army Knife blade. Another noted that the closed blade is held tightly enough to defeat the old "Opinel tap" trick. A non-locking knife can fold on the fingers if pushed hard against the spine, so the néo6 is a tool for slicing, not prying or batoning.

**Blade and edge.** The Sandvik-based stainless sits at 55–57 HRC, which means easy sharpening more than long edge life. Owners report blades arriving razor sharp and holding an edge as well as any Opinel. Enthusiasts on Blade Forums praise the thin full-convex grind for how readily it takes a new edge. Stainless also means no patina management, and no black stain on a cheese wedge. The rounded tip is the most divisive choice. It is safer in a pack, easier to explain to a hostel clerk, and less likely to snap if levered. It also removes the point that many hikers use to open packaging, spear a bag seal, or dig out a splinter. Commenters on a prominent review video said they would pass on the knife for that reason alone. Blade Forums posters have already started filing the tip back to a point.

**Handle and finish.** The varnished walnut gives the knife a classic Opinel look, and the finish is described as food-safe. The handle is thin and light, which is an advantage in a pocket but not for long cutting sessions. A UK owner reported that it looks sturdier than a regular No. 6 but is the same size. The varnish seals the wood against trail moisture better than bare beech, though it will still scuff in a pack.

**Legal carry.** Because the blade does not lock, the néo6 is aimed at places with strict rules on locking folders. Opinel pitches it as an everyday knife for cities as well as the outdoors. Laws vary by jurisdiction, so hikers who cross borders should check local rules for blade length and mechanism before carrying it.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 25 g and 9.5 cm closed
- Opiflex lets the blade open and close one-handed without a twist ring
- Stainless blade needs no upkeep and sharpens easily
- Walnut handle and leather lanyard give it a good finish for the price
- Non-locking design suits regions that restrict locking knives

**Cons**

- Rounded tip rules out piercing and detail work
- Cost is about three times a standard carbon or stainless No. 6, according to UK owners
- Retention is firm but not as firm as a Swiss Army Knife slip joint
- Non-locking, so not suited to hard pressure on the spine
- Thin handle becomes tiring in long food-prep sessions

## Who Should Buy This

The néo6 suits section hikers, weekend backpackers and thru-hikers whose knife duty is mostly food, cord and packaging. Anyone who passes through places with restrictions on locking blades, or who wants a good-looking and light knife for town stops, will also find it fits. Gear-cutters, bushcrafters and anyone who spends time carving, feathering sticks or working on gear should look elsewhere. A standard Opinel No. 6 or No. 7 with a Virobloc lock and a pointed blade costs much less and does more. A small Swiss Army Knife is the better choice for those who want tweezers and scissors along with a blade.

## Verdict

The néo6 is a well-made, handsome 25 g food knife with a sensible, spring-based retention system. The rounded tip and the price keep it from being the default Opinel for trail use. Buy it for a light kitchen blade or where locking knives are a problem, and keep a pointed No. 6 for everything else.

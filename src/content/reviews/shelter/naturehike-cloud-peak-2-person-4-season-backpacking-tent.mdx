## Overview

The Naturehike Cloud Peak 2 is a freestanding, three-pole hexagonal tent aimed at backpackers who want more livable space than a typical tunnel-style budget tent without jumping into four-figure cottage-brand pricing. It's built from 20D silicone-coated nylon with a 7001 aluminum pole set, and Naturehike markets it as a 4-season shelter — a claim worth unpacking below, because "4-season" means different things depending on who's selling the tent. This one's best thought of as a 3-season-plus tent that can handle a surprise snow squall, not a basecamp mountaineering shelter.

## Performance

**Setup:** This is a freestanding design, which is a genuine advantage over Naturehike's cheaper tunnel tents — <cite index="7-15">the tent's design is also freestanding, making it easy to pitch and move around</cite>. That said, more than one Reddit user pointed out that the three-pole cross structure takes longer to wrangle than a simpler two-pole design. One reviewer summed it up bluntly: <cite index="6-1">"The CP2 is a bit of a drag to put up after a hard day's hike, the flip side of this, is that it's a really nice, sturdy tent (from the budget end) to be in."</cite> If you're coming from a trekking-pole mid or a simple dome, budget an extra few minutes at camp, especially solo.

**Space:** This is where the Cloud Peak 2 actually earns its "2-person" label, unlike a lot of budget tents in this price bracket. Naturehike's hexagonal floor plan and dual side-entry doors give it noticeably more shoulder and gear room than the brand's own Cloud Up 2, which multiple reviewers and even CleverHiker's testing flagged as cramped for two adults. One Reddit thread comparing the two put it plainly: <cite index="6-15">the Naturehike Cloud Peak is better than the Cloud Up because it has side doors rather than an end door, basically a clone of the MSR Hubba</cite>, and another called it <cite index="6-16">"very roomy inside, free standing."</cite> If you've read complaints about the Cloud Up 2's tight interior, the Cloud Peak 2 is Naturehike's answer to that.

**Weather resistance:** The fly and floor use the same 20D silicone-coated nylon Naturehike puts on its other Cloud-series tents, and one detailed write-up cites a <cite index="7-9">flysheet made of 20D nylon ripstop, silicone coated, providing excellent water-resistance with a rating of 4000 mm</cite>. That's a respectable hydrostatic head for the price point and should shrug off sustained rain without seam-sealing paranoia. Wind is a different story — this is a tall, hexagonal freestanding tent with a lot of sail area, and nothing in user feedback suggests it's meant for exposed alpine ridgelines. Treat "4-season" here as marketing shorthand for "can handle a light snow load and shoulder-season cold," not a Hilleberg-grade storm shelter. Stake it out and use the included guylines in anything beyond moderate wind.

**Ventilation and condensation:** The design includes <cite index="7-11,7-12">a ventilation opening at the top of the flysheet, which helps prevent the accumulation of condensation and ensures a comfortable sleeping environment</cite>, and the inner tent's mesh panels are meant to boost airflow. I'd still expect some condensation on cold, still nights — that's true of nearly every double-wall tent in this fabric weight — but the top vent plus dual doors should help you manage it better than a single-door tunnel design.

**Durability:** No long-term durability data specific to this model surfaced in user reports, but Naturehike's broader Cloud-series track record is decent: one owner of the related Cloud Up noted it <cite index="9-10,9-11">has lasted 4 years so far with fairly heavy use, calling it a great budget tent</cite>. Zippers and pole ferrules are the usual wear points to watch on any budget tent — inspect them each trip and carry a repair sleeve.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Freestanding three-pole design pitches without needing perfectly staked ground
- Genuinely spacious for two people, with dual side doors and vestibules — a real upgrade over Naturehike's Cloud Up 2
- 20D silicone-nylon fly rated around 4,000mm hydrostatic head, solid rain performance for the price
- Top vent helps manage condensation better than single-door budget tents
- Compact packed size (Φ15 × 50 cm) despite the added headroom

**Cons**

- At 2,260g it's heavier than most dedicated ultralight 2P shelters (many trekking-pole tents in this category run 1,000–1,500g)
- Three-pole setup is slower and fiddlier than simpler two-pole tunnel or dome designs
- "4-season" rating is optimistic — fine for shoulder-season cold and light snow, not a true winter mountaineering tent
- Limited independent long-term durability testing available for this specific model

## Who Should Buy This

This tent makes sense for backpackers and couples who prioritize interior space and easy freestanding setup over shaving every last gram, and who camp mostly in 3-season conditions with occasional cold-weather trips. If you're chasing a sub-3-lb base weight or planning serious winter mountaineering, look elsewhere — but if you want a genuinely roomy 2-person shelter that won't wreck your budget, the Cloud Peak 2 is a sensible pick.

## Verdict

The Cloud Peak 2 delivers on the thing most budget 2P tents fumble — actual livable space for two people — while keeping weight and pack size reasonable, even if it's not going to impress the gram-counting crowd. Treat the 4-season label as aspirational rather than literal, and this is a solid, well-reviewed value shelter. I'd put it at a 7.3/10: strong on space and setup ease, docked for weight and an inflated season rating.

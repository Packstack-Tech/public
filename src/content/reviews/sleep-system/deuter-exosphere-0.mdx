## Overview

The Exosphere 0° is Deuter's synthetic-fill mummy bag built around a stretchy "ThermoStretch" shingle construction instead of the usual sewn-through or box-wall baffles you see on most synthetic bags. It's aimed at three-season backpackers and bikepackers who want down-like packability and fit without down's moisture sensitivity or price tag. If you camp somewhere damp — the UK, the Pacific Northwest, shoulder-season alpine trips where condensation is a given — this is the kind of bag that's designed to shrug that off.

## Performance

The headline feature is the stretch. Deuter builds the bag from shingled, elasticated chambers that expand by up to 25% in width and improve comfort, fit and efficiency in the process. In practice, this is the part that surprises most reviewers. One tester who was skeptical going in said he was a bit dubious about the stretchiness of the bag, but it actually works really well, with the stretch being quite gentle but enough to make the bag hug your body nicely. That close fit isn't just about comfort — it's doing thermal work too, since the elastic seams stretch up to 25% in width for added comfort, and this also makes them more thermally efficient by eliminating dead space. The trade-off is that it feels a little odd on the first night. Testers of the related -10° model noted when you first climb into this bag, its elasticated chambers result in a slightly unusual body-hugging sensation that feels strange at first — expect an adjustment period, not a dealbreaker.

Warmth distribution is the other thing Deuter gets right here. The 360° fill means the synthetic Thermo ProLoft fill is distributed totally evenly top and bottom, so the bag conforms to its temperature rating regardless of sleep position — no rolling over and getting a cold back. For side sleepers (most of us, if we're honest), that's a genuinely useful design choice that a lot of synthetic bags skip in favor of concentrating loft on top only.

Where the Exosphere 0° struggles is the same place every synthetic bag struggles: weight-to-warmth against down. One long-term UK reviewer was blunt about it, noting at over 1kg this synthetic-filled bag is a lot heavier for its insulating performance than the best lightweight down-filled alternatives, with one down model of equivalent temperature rating weighing less than half as much. That's the calculus you're signing up for with any synthetic bag in this temperature range — you're paying a weight tax for moisture resistance and a lower price. The same reviewer, though, was quick to add some perspective: the weight isn't excessive for a synthetic bag, and it hasn't been enough to worry him in a rucksack.

Packability is better than you'd expect for synthetic fill. The bag comes with its own compression sack and packs down really neatly for backpacking and bikepacking, feeling less bulky than other synthetic bags the reviewer had used. At a 16 x 32 cm packed size, it's not going to disappear in your pack the way a premium down bag would, but it's respectable for the category.

On the durability and weather-resistance side, synthetic fill continues to earn its keep. Unlike many down bags, testers report no fill leakage with the Exosphere, and overall it feels like a bag you don't have to treat with real care. That's echoed in the moisture performance too — for UK use, the obvious advantage of synthetic fills is that they tend to maintain their performance better than down when things get a bit damp. The shell fabric backs this up: the 30D nylon outer feels durable enough for a sleeping bag and dries quickly, with a patch of water-repellent Dryzone fabric at both ends where your head or feet might nudge the tent inner.

Value is a genuine strong point. At £145, this sleeping bag is a lot more affordable than most down bags of similar warmth — and that price gap between synthetic and down-of-equivalent-rating hasn't really closed in recent years.

One veteran reviewer summed up her take succinctly: "This is my new favourite synthetic sleeping bag - snug, well-designed, and not overly heavy for backpacking and general outdoor use".

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Stretchy ThermoStretch seams give a snug, body-hugging fit without feeling restrictive, and reduce dead air space that would otherwise need warming
- 360° fill distribution means consistent warmth no matter what position you sleep in — good news for side sleepers
- Synthetic fill shrugs off dampness and packs down smaller than most synthetic competitors
- Durable 30D nylon shell with reinforced water-repellent panels at head and foot
- Noticeably cheaper than a down bag with a comparable temperature rating

**Cons**

- Over 1kg puts it well behind ultralight down bags in warmth-per-gram — not a bag for gram-counting thru-hikers
- The stretch-hug fit takes a night or two to get used to; some sleepers find it odd at first
- Packed size (16 x 32 cm) is good for synthetic but still bulkier than comparable down bags
- Temperature ratings, as with all EN/ISO tested bags, are based on a mannequin lying on its back — expect the real-world lower limit to run warmer for side sleepers

## Who Should Buy This

This bag makes the most sense for backpackers, bikepackers, and car campers in wet or unpredictable climates — think the UK, Pacific Northwest, or shoulder-season trips where a soggy tent wall or damp night in a bivy is a real possibility. If you're chasing base weight and mostly camp in dry, predictable conditions, a down bag rated to 0°C will beat it on weight and pack size every time. But if you want a bag you don't have to babysit — one that keeps insulating even if it gets damp and doesn't demand a dry sack obsession — the Exosphere 0° delivers that at a fair price.

## Verdict

The Exosphere 0° won't win any weight weenie awards, but it does what a synthetic bag should: keep working when things get wet, hold its temperature rating regardless of sleep position, and cost noticeably less than a comparable down bag. For UK-style three-season use where damp is a given, I'd call it a smart, if not featherlight, choice. Rating: 7.5/10.

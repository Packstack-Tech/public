## Overview

The Copper Spur has been a default freestanding tent on American long-distance trails since the series debuted in 2008. The HV version uses a high-volume hub and pre-bent pole sections to push the walls near-vertical, giving it the interior of a much heavier shelter. The trade-off is familiar: thin 15D and 20D fabrics, a mesh-heavy body that favors summer over shoulder season, and a price that runs from roughly $450 for the solo to about $800 for the five-person.

## Performance

### Weight and packability

At 1,417 g (3 lb 2 oz), the UL2 is a couple of ounces lighter than MSR's updated Hubba Hubba 2, which comes in at 3 lb 4 oz. It is heavier than the semi-freestanding Fly Creek HV UL2 (2 lb 4 oz), the Tiger Wall UL2 (2 lb 8 oz) and the NEMO Hornet (2 lb 6 oz), but those tents give up interior volume and are slower to pitch. For a fully freestanding double-wall tent with two doors, reviewers consider the weight penalty reasonable.

Packed, the UL2 measures 6 x 19.5 in, which is competitive for the class. Hikers short on pack space can split the pole bag from the tent body, since the pre-bent, hubbed poles account for much of the bulk.

### Livability

This is why the Copper Spur has its reputation. Near-vertical sidewalls and a large, flat roof make the interior nearly rectangular, and the freestanding frame keeps the body taut on rock or hardpan where stakes are useless. The pocket layout is well thought out: a device pocket with cord routing, standard mesh pockets and a 3D bin pocket that swallows a surprising amount of gear.

The UL2 floor tapers from 52 inches at the head to 42 at the foot. That fits two regular mummy pads, but wide rectangular pads can fill the floor so completely that the door zippers become awkward to work.

Each side has its own door and vestibule, with double zippers that open one-handed so one sleeper can leave without climbing over the other. The vestibules are big enough to keep wet, muddy boots and packs out of the sleeping area.

### Weather

The tent keeps testers dry through ordinary storms and handles wind well once the guylines are tensioned. It is not a storm tent. The body is mostly mesh, which is excellent for ventilation and condensation control in warm weather but lets cold air and wind-driven spray through in shoulder season. The fly vent has a prop bar for maximum airflow. Most owners treat it as a 2.5-season shelter in practice.

Pitching is inner-first, so the mesh body is exposed to rain until the fly is on. Adding the footprint enables a fast-fly pitch of fly, poles and footprint only, which is the better option on wet days and cuts weight. On the UL1, that configuration drops the weight to about 737 g.

### Setup and durability

Owners report pitching the tent in five minutes or less after watching a setup video once. The TipLok corner buckles combine pole-tip capture, fly attachment and stake-out in one fitting, and the fly arrives with guylines pre-cut and attached.

Durability is the limiting factor. The floor, fly and solid body panels use mixed 15D and 20D nylon, and the body mesh is 15D. Sharp gravel and sticks need to be avoided, and a footprint is sensible insurance. Some Reddit users report pole breakage under hard use, and PCT hikers have complained about zipper wear in dust. Big Agnes has a good reputation for warranty and repair service, often charging little more than shipping.

## Choosing a Version

**UL1 (964 g).** A true solo tent: 88 x 38/28 in floor, 20 sq ft, 38 in peak height, one door and one 9 sq ft vestibule. It is the lightest of the four and packs to roughly 17.5 x 5.5 in. It suits solo hikers who want a freestanding double-wall tent without a big weight penalty, though the 28-inch foot is tight for bulky quilts or wide pads.

**UL2 (1,417 g).** The core model, with 29 sq ft of floor, 40 in of headroom, two doors and two vestibules. It works for a couple on regular-width mummy pads, and it is genuinely spacious for one person who wants room for gear or a dog.

**UL3 (1,620 g).** A 90 x 70/62 in floor, 41 sq ft and 43 in of peak height. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers singled out its space-to-weight ratio, and it is a sensible choice for two people with wide pads or a dog. At only 203 g heavier than the UL2, it is the fix for couples who find the UL2 floor too narrow. The vestibules stay at 18 sq ft total, which gets crowded with three people's gear.

**UL5 (3,118 g).** A 108 x 86 in floor, 65 sq ft and a 60 in peak height, with two doors and two 17 sq ft vestibules on a two-pole frame. It is a group or family tent: room for five, or a very spacious four. Spread across five sleepers, the weight works out to roughly 620 g each. A fast-fly pitch with the 12 oz footprint brings it to about 5 lb 9 oz (2.52 kg), and the packed bundle is 9 x 24.5 in, so it needs to be split among packs. Solo and pair hikers gain nothing from it.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Near-vertical walls and 38–60 in of headroom give the best interior volume in the ultralight freestanding class
- Fully freestanding, with five-minute pitching on any surface
- Dual doors and vestibules on the UL2, UL3 and UL5 keep sleepers from climbing over each other
- Mesh body and prop-bar fly vent control condensation well in warm weather
- Pockets, a 3D bin and cord routing are well designed
- Strong Big Agnes repair and warranty support

**Cons**

- The tapered UL2 floor is tight for two people on wide pads
- Thin 15D/20D fabrics demand careful site selection
- Inner-first pitch exposes the mesh body to rain unless the footprint is added
- Mesh-heavy body runs cold in shoulder season and exposed alpine conditions
- Pricey, from about $450 to $800 depending on size
- Reports of pole breakage and zipper wear

## Who Should Buy This

The Copper Spur suits the backpacker who wants a freestanding tent that is easy to pitch, comfortable during bad-weather waits, and light enough for long trail miles. Solo hikers will like the UL1 or UL2, and couples on regular mummy pads are well served by the UL2. Couples with wide pads or a dog should step up to the UL3, and families or groups can split the UL5. Hikers who are hard on gear, camp regularly below freezing or in exposed alpine terrain, or want the lightest possible pitch should look at a sturdier four-season design or a trekking-pole shelter.

## Verdict

The Copper Spur HV earns its place as a trail staple by combining real livability, quick freestanding setup and competitive weight across a size range that covers solo hikers to groups of five. Buy the footprint if rain is likely, and check the floor width against your pads before committing. For most three-season backpackers, it is the safe and comfortable choice.

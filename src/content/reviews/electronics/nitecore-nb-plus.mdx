## Overview

The NB Plus is Nitecore's waterproof take on the ultralight 10,000mAh power bank: carbon fiber panels, a composite frame, and a body just 11.7 mm thick. It is built for hikers, trail runners and cyclists who want several phone charges in a pocket-sized slab. The trade-off is a modest 18W input ceiling, which makes refilling the bank itself a slow affair.

## Performance

**Weight and form.** At 155 g, the NB Plus is not the lightest 10,000mAh bank on the market, but it is close, and the shape does a lot of work. PackHacker's testers called it palmable, noting that it is far more compact than banks from even five years ago. A review at The Technology Man measured it as the thinnest 10,000mAh bank the reviewer had handled, about as thick as a modern large phone with its camera bump. It slides into a running vest, a hip-belt pocket or a back pocket without announcing itself. PackHacker also worked out the capacity-to-weight math against Nitecore's smaller NB Air: about 1,828 mAh per ounce for the NB Plus versus 1,592 for the Air, so the bigger bank is the more efficient carry.

**Water resistance.** The headline change from the NB10000 Gen 3 is the IPX7 rating, which covers temporary immersion. One reviewer put the cost at roughly 5 g over the previous generation. For a bank that lives in a rain-soaked shoulder-strap pocket or rides on a bike in sideways drizzle, that is a fair price. The "X" in the rating means dust ingress was not tested, so beach sand and fine desert grit are a less certain proposition.

**Real-world capacity.** Rated watt-hours are not delivered watt-hours. The Technology Man discharged the bank near its top output, 9V at 2A, and recovered 26.99 Wh, roughly 70 percent of the 38.5 Wh rating. That is typical for a power bank and close to Nitecore's own claims, and it is enough to recharge a recent iPhone from empty a few times over. Reviewers at ZeroAir noted that 10,000mAh is large enough for most phones and will partly refill bigger-battery devices such as tablets. For a long section hike carrying a phone, a headlamp and a GPS messenger, it covers several days between town stops.

**Ports and charging.** The bank has one USB-C and one USB-A port. USB-C handles PD and QC 3.0 output and also supports pass-through charging, which helps in a hostel or motel when one outlet has to serve both bank and phone. The USB-A port is the useful part for old headlamp cables and the cord that came with a cheap GPS device. It also makes the body slightly thicker around the port, though the bank stays slim overall.

**The slow part.** Input tops out at 18W, so refilling a depleted bank takes hours. Testing of the related NB10000, which shares the same 18W ceiling, put a full recharge from empty at just over four hours on a QC 3.0 wall charger. In a town stop with a two-hour laundry cycle, that is a squeeze. Plan on charging the bank overnight and the phone from the bank.

**Interface and durability.** There are no buttons. A touch-sensitive power icon wakes the charge indicator for a few seconds, and touching anywhere else on the body does nothing. Owners of earlier Nitecore banks have complained that the small blue LEDs are hard to read in daylight, and that only a few indicator lights make rationing a trip's power difficult; reviewers say the NB Plus indicators are noticeably brighter than those on the Gen 2. The double-sided carbon fiber sheets set in a composite frame give it a rigid, premium feel that ZeroAir reviewers rated very good. One small gripe from a Three Points of the Compass reviewer: the yellow accents that ringed the ports on earlier models are gone, which makes finding them in a dark tent slightly harder. The rated operating range is -10 to 35°C, enough for shoulder-season and mild winter use, though lithium-ion capacity drops in the cold and the bank belongs inside a jacket or sleeping bag on frosty nights.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 10,000mAh in a 155 g, 11.7 mm slab that disappears in a pocket
- IPX7 rating for rain, river crossings and sweaty running vests
- USB-C and USB-A ports, with PD and QC 3.0 output and pass-through charging
- Rigid carbon fiber construction, with brighter LEDs than the Gen 2
- Rated for -10 to 35°C operation

**Cons**

- 18W maximum input means a slow refill, around four hours or more from empty
- Few features: no buttons, no display, and only coarse charge-level indication
- Roughly 70 percent of rated energy was delivered at high discharge in one test
- Not dust-rated, and no longer carries the high-visibility port accents
- Nitecore's own NB10000 Gen4 is lighter, at 143 g

## Who Should Buy This

The NB Plus suits backpackers, trail runners and bikepackers who want one bank for a week of phone, headlamp and GPS use, and who want some insurance against water. The USB-A port makes it a practical choice for hikers still carrying older cables and gadgets. Gram counters willing to spend more should look at the 143 g NB10000 Gen4, and weekend hikers who only need one phone charge can drop to the 89 g NB Air. Anyone who needs fast turnarounds at resupply stops, or wants to charge a laptop, should look for a bank with a higher input and output rating.

## Verdict

The NB Plus is a well-built, waterproof 10,000mAh bank that gives up little weight for its durability and ports. The slow recharge is the main flaw, and it is manageable if the bank charges overnight. It earns a recommendation for most three-season hikers.

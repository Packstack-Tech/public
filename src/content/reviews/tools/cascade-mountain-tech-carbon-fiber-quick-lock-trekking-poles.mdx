## Overview

Cascade Mountain Tech's Carbon Fiber Quick Lock poles are three-section, flick-lock telescoping poles with cork grips, aimed at hikers who want carbon's weight savings without carbon's usual price tag. They typically sell for under $50 a pair, which is roughly what many brands charge for a single aluminum pole. The trade-off is that they are a value pole, not an ultralight one: heavier and longer when packed than the folding carbon poles thru-hikers favor, and less proven under heavy, long-term abuse.

## Performance

**Weight and swing.** At 8 oz per pole, the pair lands near 15 oz. Independent reviewers have put it at 15.2 to 15.4 oz on their own scales, in line with the claim. That is clearly lighter than comparable aluminum poles, and owners who bought them for exactly that reason report they are noticeably lighter in hand. Trailspace reviewers describe a good swing weight. Still, against purpose-built ultralight carbon folders, the Cascades are a step heavier. Advnture's testers called them relatively heavy for backpacking and said they would rather use them on day hikes than on a thru-hike.

**Grip.** The cork is the best part of the package. Section Hiker notes that cork runs less sweaty than rubber or foam and weighs less. Trail to Peak's reviewer said it felt a bit plasticky at first but molded to the hand over a few months of use. Advnture also reports some initial rubbing before the grip breaks in. The EVA foam extension below the cork lets hikers choke down on steep climbs instead of stopping to shorten the poles. CleverHiker's testers called that extension ideal for uphill sections, and a Jerking the Trigger reviewer said the same, though he could feel the transition from cork to foam. He never got a hot spot or blister from it. CleverHiker scores comfort slightly above average, citing the cork, the hand-fitting grip shape and soft wrist straps.

**Locks and adjustment.** The flick locks are quick to operate and easy to tighten, and a 6'1" reviewer found plenty of adjustment range. Reliability is where the criticism concentrates. Outdoor Gear Lab's bottom line calls these budget carbon poles with a generous accessory kit, but says durability and lock reliability limit them for heavy or long-term use. Older Trailspace feedback adds smaller gripes: the tension-adjustment piece is easy to lose, the poles can rattle on hard surfaces, and the wrist straps are long and fiddly to adjust. Section Hiker passed along a friend's report that the straps slip under heavy load.

**Durability and tips.** One Trailspace reviewer with several hundred miles on a Cascade carbon pole found the lower shaft scuffed up, a concern with carbon because deep scratches can weaken it. He also noted Cascade sells replacement shafts and other parts, which keeps a worn pole from becoming trash. The carbide tips bite well and hold up on granite, according to one owner, and can flex a little without breaking.

**Packability.** Three sections and a 26-inch collapsed length mean these poles ride long on a pack. Advnture flagged the pack size and bulky lock hardware, and the poles have no anti-shock feature. The included travel bag is useful for car trips and for corralling the baskets and tip covers.

**Accessories.** The kit covers more terrain than most poles at this price: rubber tip boots for pavement, mud baskets, and snow baskets. Reviewers cite this as a real advantage for hikers who also snowshoe or ski.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Carbon construction at a budget price, usually under $50 a pair
- Cork grip with extended foam lower grip for climbing
- Fast, easy flick-lock adjustment across a 26"–54" range
- Rubber tips, mud baskets and snow baskets included
- Replacement parts available from the manufacturer

**Cons**

- About 454 g per pair, heavier than ultralight-focused carbon poles
- Long collapsed length at 26" and bulky locks
- Lock reliability and long-term durability questioned for heavy use
- No anti-shock feature
- Straps are long and can slip; cork can rub until broken in

## Who Should Buy This

Day hikers, weekend backpackers, and first-time pole buyers who want carbon weight and cork comfort without spending $100 or more. It also suits hikers who want one set of poles to cover dirt, mud and snow thanks to the baskets in the box. Thru-hikers and gram-counters should look elsewhere, toward lighter folding carbon poles with a shorter packed length. So should anyone who leans hard on their poles for tent pitching or river crossings, where lock reliability matters most.

## Verdict

The Cascade Mountain Tech Carbon Fiber Quick Lock poles are an honest budget carbon pole with a good grip, easy adjustment and more accessories than they have any right to include at the price. They are not a thru-hike tool, and their locks and shafts are not built for years of hard use. For casual and moderate hiking, they are one of the better values going.

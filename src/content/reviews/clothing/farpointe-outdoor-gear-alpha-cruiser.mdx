## Overview

The Alpha Cruiser is FarPointe's flagship: a plain, fitted hoody cut from 90 gsm Polartec Alpha Direct, the open-mesh fleece that has become a fixture in ultralight kits. It is built for hikers who generate heat while moving and shed it at every stop. The trade-off is built into the fabric: it breathes superbly and weighs almost nothing, but it offers no wind resistance of its own.

## Performance

**Warmth and breathability.** Alpha Direct is a lofted, open-knit polyester with no inner lining, and that is the whole trick. The gaps in the weave trap warm air in a manner SectionHiker's reviewer likened to mesh Brynje baselayers, and the hoody is comfortable even against bare skin. Moisture vapor escapes readily, so a hiker grinding uphill on a cold morning doesn't end up in a clammy cocoon. Southwest Hikes' reviewer found it noticeably less stuffy than traditional fleece when climbing. Farlite claims it is as warm as a fleece shirt of double the weight; the more defensible takeaway is that at 125 g, it punches well above that number for its warmth.

**Wind and weather.** This is where the open weave bites back. The Alpha Cruiser has no wind resistance and has to go under a shell to avoid heat loss. Worn under a wind shirt or rain jacket, it becomes a very efficient system; worn alone on a breezy ridge, it leaks heat. Owners who treat it as a mid layer rather than a jacket tend to be happiest. In practice, that means pairing it with a wind shirt of 2 to 3 oz, which most ultralight hikers carry anyway.

**Drying and wet use.** The fabric is hydrophobic and wicks quickly, and FarPointe leans on that in its marketing. Retailers report hikers who were saved by an Alpha layer after a downpour, since it retains little water and warms back up fast under body heat. It is not waterproof or even water-resistant in the shell sense, but a soaked Alpha hoody is far more usable than a soaked fleece.

**Packability.** The same open structure that makes it breathable makes it compress to a very small bundle. FarPointe lists excellent compression as a feature, and Farlite notes that the pack size is very small for the warmth on offer.

**Construction and details.** The sewing is neat, and the design is minimal by necessity: Alpha Direct is gauzy enough that there's little to do beyond doubling the fabric at the hem, wrists and neck and adding a drawstring channel for the hood. The hood is fitted rather than oversized, which suits wearing it under a shell hood. One recurring complaint is the paracord drawstring: it is knotted at the ends but can retreat into the hood channel. SectionHiker's reviewer fixed it by threading both ends through a cordlock and tying them together, an inexpensive, two-minute modification.

**Durability and versatility.** The open mesh is less general-purpose than a tighter-weave fleece. It can snag on rough surfaces, and it is not a garment for bushwhacking in the way a denser grid fleece might be. Treat it as technical underwear-plus rather than as a rugged outer layer.

**Sizing.** Retailer guidance is to size up if between sizes, as the cut runs small. Since it's meant to layer over a baselayer and under a shell, a trim fit is intended, but a hiker planning to layer a puffy over it should size accordingly.

**Fabric weight note.** FarPointe also sells a lighter 60 gsm Alpha Cruiser; a 2023 Road Trail Run review measured a medium at 112 g. The 90 gsm tested here trades a small weight penalty for noticeably more insulation, which is the better choice for shoulder-season and three-season use.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Very high warmth for 125 g
- Excellent breathability under effort
- Dries fast and stays functional when damp
- Compresses small
- Comfortable against bare skin
- Fitted hood works under a shell

**Cons**

- No wind resistance whatsoever; requires a shell
- Open weave is delicate and less versatile than tighter fleece
- Drawstring ends can disappear into the hood
- Runs small
- $105 is a fair amount for a garment this simple

## Who Should Buy This

Three-season thru-hikers and backpackers who already carry a wind shirt or rain jacket and want a sleeping-and-hiking layer that doesn't punish them when they sweat. It also suits fast-moving, stop-start days (alpine starts, cool mornings, camp evenings) when a puffy would be overkill and overheating within a mile. Hikers who want one standalone jacket for windy, exposed conditions, or who prioritize abrasion resistance over weight, should look at a tighter-weave fleece or a wind-resistant hybrid instead.

## Verdict

The Alpha Cruiser does one job, active insulation, extremely well, and at 125 g it's hard to justify leaving at home from a three-season kit with a shell. Its lack of wind protection and delicate weave keep it from being a do-everything layer, but for its intended use it is among the better values in cottage-made Alpha.

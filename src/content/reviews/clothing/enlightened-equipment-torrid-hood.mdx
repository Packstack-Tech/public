## Overview

A quilt saves weight by dropping the hood, and on a cold night that choice shows up at the top of your head. Enlightened Equipment's Torrid Hood fills that gap with a roughly 2-ounce synthetic cap-and-collar that sleeps under any quilt or bag. The trade-off is that it adds a small item to carry, and it is a sleep accessory more than a daytime hat.

## Performance

The hood is built to solve the quilt sleeper's two problems: a cold head and a drafty neck. Most quilts stop at the shoulders, and the hood covers the gap. The collar runs long, and its three snaps let a sleeper open it for airflow on a mild night or close it to block drafts on a cold one.

Fit is the main selling point. A shock cord runs over the forehead and around the back of the head. Cinched down, it keeps the hood in place through a night of rolling over. Loosened, it can be pulled across the eyes for sleeping. One Backpacking Light forum poster who uses the hood below 30°F said it snugs and seals better than any sleeping bag hood they had owned. That matters because a hood that rotates off the face as you turn is worse than none.

The insulation is synthetic, which suits a garment that sits next to your breath. CLIMASHIELD APEX keeps its loft when damp, so condensation inside a tent or exhaled moisture won't collapse it the way it can with down. EE says the fill dries quickly, and the hood is easy to wash. Both are useful for something that gets sweat and skin oil every night. Down hoods have a better warmth-to-weight ratio, but they need more careful handling.

The standard configuration uses 4 oz/yd² APEX, a mid-range choice. The 2 oz option is for shoulder-season trips, and the 6 oz is for sleepers who run cold or camp in real winter. EE lists its APEX weights as varying by up to 10 percent from production variance. The brand doesn't publish temperature ratings for the hood, so how much warmth it adds depends on the person and the quilt. Hikers generally layer it over a thin beanie or buff and drop it from the kit when nights stay above freezing.

The no-sewn-through seam construction is a quiet strength. Without stitch lines running through the insulation, there are no thin lines of cold on the head. It also explains why the hood stays so light: there is no extra baffle fabric. The 10D nylon is delicate, which is fine inside a sleeping system and a poor reason to wear it as a daily-drive beanie.

It can double as a camp hat. EE pitches it for extra warmth during the day, and the snaps and cord make it adjustable around a stove at dusk. But it's shaped like a hood, with a long collar and a cord-cinched opening, and looks the part. Anyone who wants a trail beanie will be happier with a normal one.

## Choosing a Version

The versions differ in size, and size is the one decision that matters.

- **Small (18–20 in / 46–51 cm):** Small heads. Order this only if you measure in range, since extra fabric makes the hood bunch when the cord is pulled tight.
- **Medium (20–22 in / 51–56 cm):** The size most adults land in. A UK retailer lists the medium at 59 g.
- **Large (22–24 in / 56–61 cm):** Larger heads, or anyone planning to wear a thin beanie underneath.
- **X-Large (24–26 in / 61–66 cm):** The biggest heads, and a sensible choice if you sleep in a thick balaclava under the hood.

Fill weight is the other lever, and it matters more than the size for warmth. The 2 oz/yd² suits three-season hikers and anyone counting grams. The 4 oz/yd² is the all-around choice. The 6 oz/yd² is for cold sleepers and winter use, at the cost of a bulkier hood.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Weighs about 2 oz, which is little for the coverage it gives
- Synthetic fill keeps working through breath moisture and damp tents
- Forehead shock cord keeps the hood on the head and can close over the eyes
- Three-snap collar adjusts for ventilation or draft sealing
- No sewn-through seams, so no cold lines
- Customizable fabric, color, and fill weight

**Cons**

- A sleep-specific item that adds a piece of gear for hikers already carrying a puffy hood
- Synthetic fill packs larger and carries less warmth per gram than a comparable down hood
- No published temperature rating, so choosing a fill weight takes some guesswork
- Custom orders can take weeks to ship, and the 10D nylon needs careful handling

## Who Should Buy This

Quilt users who sleep cold, especially shoulder-season hikers in damp climates, are the audience. It also suits anyone whose puffy jacket doesn't have a hood, or whose hood sits awkwardly when lying down. It is a reasonable pick for a hiker who sweats a lot at night and wants fill that handles moisture.

Hikers who sleep in a mummy bag with its own hood don't need it. Neither do three-season hikers whose nights seldom drop below 40°F and who already carry a beanie. Anyone who wants one item for sleeping and for walking should buy a beanie and a hooded puffy instead.

## Verdict

The Torrid Hood does a narrow job well. For about $70 and under 2.5 oz, it gives quilt sleepers a hood that stays on and closes up the neck gap. Buy it if you sleep cold under a quilt, and skip it if your puffy's hood and a beanie already keep you warm.

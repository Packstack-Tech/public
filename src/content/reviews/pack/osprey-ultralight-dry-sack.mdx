## Overview

The Osprey Ultralight Dry Sack is a rectangular, roll-top stuff sack in 40D recycled nylon. It comes in five sizes, from a 20 g, 3-liter bag for electronics to a 54 g, 35-liter bag for a sleeping bag or a full clothing kit. It's aimed at backpackers who want a liner that stacks neatly and survives a wet trail. The trade-off is that IPX5 protects against splashes and rain, not against being dunked.

## Performance

**Water protection.** IPX5 means the fabric and closure are rated to handle water jets, which covers sustained rain and a pack set down in a puddle. It does not cover submersion. Owner reports follow that line. One reviewer on a lazy-river trip was generally happy with the bag but found a bit of water had gotten in, and was glad the phone had gone in a zip bag first. Another reviewer floated in an inner tube and reported everything stayed dry. The roll-top is the variable: the more turns it gets and the more cleanly it's clipped, the better it holds up. In an earlier Outdoor Gear Lab comparison of ultralight dry sacks, the Osprey repelled water significantly better than Outdoor Research's rival model, which took on water in a lake dunk.

**Shape and packing.** This is where the Osprey earns its place. Buyers keep pointing to the rectangular profile: it fits easily inside a pack and allows for stacking, rather than leaving the dead space a tapered stuff sack does. One reviewer reported it fit well in an Osprey pack. The sack works well as a liner organizer for a food bag, a clothing layer, or a quilt, because the square footprint lines up against the pack's back panel.

**Fabric and weight.** The 40D high-tenacity nylon is thicker than the whisper-thin fabrics on many "ultralight" sacks. Reviewers on the retail sites note the thickness and packability. A cyclist who carried sleeping gear in one on a rear rack for 333 dusty miles called it roomy and dust-free. The material is also 100% recycled and bluesign approved. The weights are low in absolute terms: the 3L is 20 g, the 12L is 34 g, and the 35L is 54 g. A full set of all five comes to under 180 g, though few hikers would carry more than two or three.

**Closure and details.** The roll-top uses a quick-release plastic clip that owners describe as easy to operate. There's also a loop at the base. One owner who stores a 20-degree quilt in the sack mentions that the loop lets it hang upside down from a bear line with the roll-top at the bottom.

**Durability.** Nothing in owner feedback suggests a weak point beyond the usual. Like any sub-60 g sack, it belongs inside the pack, not strapped to the outside against rock and brush.

## Choosing a Version

All five sizes share the same fabric, shape and closure. Only volume and weight change, and the difference between the largest and smallest is just 34 g.

- **3L (20 g):** Phone, headlamp, battery bank, charging cables, or a first-aid kit. It's the sack for items that must stay dry and be easy to find.
- **6L (26 g):** Insulating hat and gloves, a spare base layer, or a day's worth of snacks and toiletries.
- **12L (34 g):** A midlayer and spare clothes, or a small quilt on a summer trip.
- **20L (45 g):** The usual choice for a sleeping bag or quilt on three-season trips, and big enough for a puffy plus extra layers.
- **35L (54 g):** Winter bags, a shared clothing sack for a couple, or a pack-liner role for a smaller frameless pack.

Most thru-hikers will get the most use from one mid-size sack for the sleep system and a 3L or 6L for electronics.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Weighs 20–54 g across the range
- Rectangular shape stacks and fills a pack more efficiently than a tapered stuff sack
- 40D recycled nylon is thicker and more reassuring than the thinnest sacks on the market
- Five sizes cover everything from a phone to a winter sleeping bag
- Quick-release clip makes the roll-top easy to close, and the base loop is a useful extra

**Cons**

- IPX5 is not submersible, so packrafting, canoe trips and river crossings call for something else
- Some owners report minor water ingress after prolonged immersion
- Fabric is not a dedicated waterproof laminate, so a sleeping bag in a soaked pack depends on good roll-top technique
- The weight and cost add up if every item gets its own sack

## Who Should Buy This

Backpackers and thru-hikers who want an organized, rain-resistant pack interior will like it. It suits people who already carry a trash-compactor liner but want a sleeping bag or electronics protected a second time, and it works for bikepackers who keep gear in a rack bag. Anyone who expects to dunk the pack, such as packrafters, kayakers and boaters, should look at a fully submersible dry bag. Hikers who count every gram and use a single pack liner may find the extra sacks unnecessary.

## Verdict

The Osprey Ultralight Dry Sack does what a pack-interior organizer should: it weighs next to nothing, stacks cleanly and keeps rain off the contents. Treat it as weather protection, not a flotation-proof bag, and it's an easy recommendation.

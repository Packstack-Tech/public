## Performance

The first thing worth clarifying: this is a **cooking** utensil set, not an eating set. The 21 cm handle is useful for stirring a pot over a flame, not shoveling noodles out of a pouch. Keep that framing in mind when you evaluate the weight.

<cite index="1-5">
  The wood of coconut trees is described as very light and of high strength
</cite>
, and that holds up in practice.
<cite index="5-1,5-2">
  The robust wood of the coconut palm is very light and very strong, making it
  well suited for cooking utensils and representing the use of materials from
  renewable raw materials.
</cite>
The spoon feels solid in hand — no flex, no creaking — and the 0.5 mm thickness
keeps it slim enough to slot into most stuff sacks.

The wood's real advantage is in what it _doesn't_ do. <cite index="12-17">Wood is naturally antimicrobial, doesn't scratch cookware, and doesn't conduct heat — so it won't burn your hands.</cite> If you're running a coated pan in camp, these are genuinely the right tool. <cite index="14-8">If you use a nonstick cook pot, wooden utensils won't damage Teflon coatings,</cite> which is more than you can say for a stainless steel spoon or a titanium spatula.

The spatula's thin profile works fine for flipping pancakes or scrambled eggs, though the 5 cm width is on the narrow side for anything bigger than a small crepe. The cooking spoon's bowl is adequately sized for stirring and serving. <cite index="7-2">One user noted the set is lightweight, comfortable to use, and easy to clean</cite> — though "easy to clean" in a wood context means prompt hand-washing, not throwing it in a camp basin and forgetting about it overnight.

Weight is the honest sticking point. At 45 g for two pieces, <cite index="13-16">a titanium three-piece cutlery set can come in at around 1.69 oz (roughly 48 g) for three pieces</cite> — meaning you're paying near-equivalent weight for just two wood cooking tools. For dedicated ultralight backpackers, that math is painful. For basecamp cooking, car camping, or a group kitchen kit where the tools are shared, it's a non-issue.

<cite index="12-18">
  Wood requires more care than metal — hand washing and occasional oiling — and
  may absorb flavors over time.
</cite>
<cite index="12-30">
  Applying food-grade mineral oil occasionally helps prevent drying and
  cracking.
</cite>
Skip this step repeatedly and you'll start to see surface checking. It's a small
ask, but it's an ask that titanium never makes.

<cite index="14-23">
  Some campers find wooden utensils harder to clean, and others note the
  uncomfortable feeling of wood on teeth
</cite>
— though since this is a cooking set rather than an eating set, the
tooth-contact issue is largely irrelevant here.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Won't scratch non-stick or coated cookware
- No heat conduction — handle stays cool while stirring
- Made from a renewable, natural material with no plastics or metals
- <cite index="5-4,5-5">
    Coconut palm wood is very light and strong, making it suitable for cooking
    utensils and representing use of renewable raw materials
  </cite>
- Hanging hole in handle keeps it organized at camp
- Very affordable price point (~€8–10)
- Slim profile packs flat

**Cons**

- 45 g for two pieces is relatively heavy compared to titanium or alloy alternatives
- Requires hand-washing and periodic oiling to prevent cracking
- <cite index="12-18">May absorb flavors over extended use</cite>
- No folding mechanism — 21 cm is fixed length, which can be awkward in tight kit configurations
- Minimal third-party reviews; long-term durability data is thin

## Who Should Buy This

This set makes the most sense for car campers, van-lifers, and basecamp trekkers who cook real meals over a stove — think sautéed onions, pancakes, or sauces — particularly with non-stick cookware. <cite index="12-19">It's best suited for eco-conscious campers who don't mind additional maintenance.</cite> Ultralight and thru-hikers who eat straight from a pot or freeze-dried pouch will find the 45 g weight hard to justify when a single titanium spork weighs a fraction as much. But if you care about natural materials, want to avoid scratching a pan, and cook in a style that actually benefits from separate spoon and spatula tools, this is a solid, no-fuss pick.

## Verdict

The Tatonka Cooking Spoon Set is a niche product that does exactly what it promises: two well-made, natural wood cooking tools at a price that won't make you wince. The coconut wood is genuinely appropriate for the job — heat-safe, non-scratching, renewable — but the weight and maintenance requirements put it out of contention for serious weight-conscious backpackers. Think of it as the right tool for a basecamp kitchen, not a multi-day trail kit.

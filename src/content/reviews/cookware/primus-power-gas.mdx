## Overview

Primus Power Gas is a threaded, self-sealing canister fuel built for spring-through-early-winter cooking, and it fits just about any screw-on backpacking stove. Primus calls it its most versatile blend, aimed at trips where the weather is hard to predict. The trade-off is chemistry: half the mix is n-butane, which gives up ground to isobutane-heavy canisters as soon as the thermometer nears freezing.

## Performance

**Fuel chemistry.** Butane and isobutane are the weak links in a cold canister. Isobutane boils at roughly 11°F (-12°C), while n-butane boils near 31°F (-1°C). Below that point it stops contributing, and the stove runs on whatever propane is left. Power Gas carries 25% propane for starting power, but 50% n-butane is a lot of dead weight on a frosty morning. A premium isobutane/propane canister such as MSR IsoPro holds pressure better as temperatures fall. The -15°C rating is the point at which the canister will still fire. It isn't the point at which it performs like it does at 10°C.

**Cold-weather numbers.** The gap depends heavily on how full the canister is. In a kitchen-freezer test at -20°C with full 230 g cans, UKClimbing's reviewer timed a litre of water to a boil in 9:35 on Power Gas and 9:06 on Winter Gas, a small difference. Primus's own freezer-room testing at the same temperature shows the picture changing as the can empties: Winter Gas, its VaporMesh-lined sibling, delivers about 9% more power after an hour of cooking and about 15% more after two. At -8°C with quarter-full canisters, a UKC-reported test timed Power Gas at 10:20 against 6:40 for Winter Gas. A JetPower canister in the same test hadn't boiled after 12 minutes. A half-empty Power Gas can in the cold is where the blend shows its limits.

Owner reports match that. Trailspace reviewers praise it in warm weather and at altitude. One who used it on winter day hikes with a Jetboil Flash described erratic burning and hard lighting compared with Jetboil's own fuel.

**Above freezing.** This is where Power Gas does its job. Prices run below those of many competing canisters, and the blend is well suited to spring-to-fall use. Primus says it still delivers enough power to run a stove below freezing. Expect that to be true for short boils, not for long melt sessions that chill the canister through evaporative cooling. In cold conditions, a canister kept in a jacket pocket or sleeping bag before use will vaporize more readily.

**Compatibility and fuel tracking.** The threaded, self-sealing valve means the canister can come off the stove mid-meal without leaking. Tare weight works out to about 160 g on the 230 g can and about 98 g on the 100 g can, so a pocket scale at camp tells a hiker how much remains. There's no other reliable way to check. The thread won't work with pierce-type stoves.

**Environmental footprint.** Primus describes all of its fuel as climate-compensated through carbon offsets, and the SIP version sold in the US uses biogas made from food waste. Offsets remain a debated tool, but few canister brands make any claim at all.

## Choosing a Version

The three listings come down to two canister sizes.

- **100 g (198 g full):** The overnight and weekend size, and a good match for stoves and pot sets designed to nest a small canister. It weighs roughly 98 g empty, so the shell eats a bigger share of the weight than in the larger size. Best for solo trips of one or two nights.
- **230 g (390 g full):** The standard backpacking size and the better value per gram of fuel. It suits solo hikers on trips of several days and pairs well with partners cooking together.
- **390 g listing:** This is the 230 g canister under its full-weight designation. It carries the same fuel and the same 4.2" x 3.3" body, and it weighs the same 390 g. There is no separate product to weigh against it.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Fits virtually every threaded canister stove
- Self-sealing valve allows mid-meal removal without leaks
- Dependable above freezing, with enough propane for reliable lighting
- Usually priced below premium isobutane blends
- 100 g size is compact and light for short trips
- Primus backs the fuel with carbon offsets

**Cons**

- 50% n-butane fades faster than isobutane blends near and below freezing
- Output drops noticeably as the canister empties in the cold
- Shell weight is high on the 100 g size, around 98 g of the 198 g total
- No gauge; fuel tracking needs a scale
- Won't work with pierce-style stoves

## Who Should Buy This

Spring-to-fall backpackers who want a dependable, widely available canister at a fair price will be well served, especially solo hikers who can match the 100 g or 230 g size to trip length. Anyone whose overnight lows regularly reach freezing, or who plans extended stove time for melting snow, should look at Primus Winter Gas or an isobutane/propane blend like MSR IsoPro instead.

## Verdict

Power Gas does what a three-season canister should: it lights, it burns, and it costs little. Push it toward winter conditions and the butane-heavy mix becomes the reason to buy something else. A solid default for warm and shoulder-season trips, and the wrong choice for cold ones.

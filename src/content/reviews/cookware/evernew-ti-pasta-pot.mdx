## Overview

The Ti Pasta Pot (ECA548) is Evernew's 1-liter titanium cookpot, pressed from 0.3 mm sheet in Japan. A perforated lid doubles as a strainer, so noodles and rice can be drained without a second vessel. It suits solo hikers and pairs who boil water for freezer-bag meals but sometimes want real food. The price is steep for what is, at heart, a very thin cup with a lid.

## Performance

**Weight and build.** Evernew's pitch is the wall thickness. Its Ultralight series uses 0.3 mm titanium, which the company says is about 20 percent lighter than the 0.4 mm industry norm. It also says it cold-forges the metal to keep that skin durable. At 116 g with lid, the pot is light enough that a Finnish retailer compares picking it up to holding a paper plane. Backcountry users agree on the thinness: a long-running NWHikers review calls the titanium "really thin" and still reports about 20 days of use without complaint.

**Boil times.** One liter is the filled-to-the-rim figure, so the working capacity for a boil is somewhat less. Even so, owners find it enough for two freezer-bag meals, or for one meal plus a hot drink. The NWHikers reviewers paired it with a Soto Windmaster and said a liter came to a boil so quickly that they learned to have everything ready before lighting the stove. A thin titanium wall responds quickly to a flame. Like all uncoated titanium, it is unforgiving of a hard simmer, and anything sticky will scorch if left unstirred.

**The lid.** The perforated lid is the feature that sets this pot apart. It snaps on firmly. A buyer who planned the 2018 Appalachian Trail thru-hike noted the lid was very tight out of the box. Other owners like how it stays put in a pack. The NWHikers reviewers said it takes two hands to pry loose, so they usually set it on loosely while cooking. For pasta, the draining holes let a hiker pour off water directly from the pot. The same holes mean the lid is no good as a splash guard for a rolling boil, since steam and spatter escape.

**Packing.** The pot is a tall, narrow shape, 11.5 cm high and 12.5 cm across at its widest point. It is sized to swallow a 230 g gas canister and a small stove, and it fits the side pocket of many packs. One owner reports it holds an original Bushbuddy wood stove. Another says a homemade reflective-insulation cozy for about a dollar and a half turned it into a complete system. The folding silicone-sleeved handles lock out of the way, though the top knob and handles add heat protection rather than serious insulation. One reviewer found the included mesh stuff sack much bigger than the pot. It does the job of keeping the pot from rattling, but it could be tighter.

**Durability and value.** The thin wall dents more easily than a heavier pot would, and owners treat it with the care that implies. The tradeoff is price. US retailers have listed it between roughly $66 and $94, and Japan-sourced units on eBay often run higher. It is hard to call that cheap for a pot that mostly boils water, though the Japanese manufacturing and the strainer lid account for some of it.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 116 g with lid, handles and strainer built in
- Pasta, rice and ramen drain straight from the pot
- Nests a 230 g canister and stove, with interior volume marks
- Snug lid stays on in a pack
- Silicone-sleeved folding handles
- Hand-pressed in Japan, with a stuff sack included

**Cons**

- Expensive for a 1-liter pot
- Thin titanium wall dents and scorches with careless handling
- Strainer holes make the lid a poor splash guard
- Lid can be stubborn to remove
- Mesh stuff sack is oversized
- 1 L rim capacity limits group cooking

## Who Should Buy This

Solo backpackers and couples who boil water for dehydrated meals but also cook pasta, couscous or instant noodles will get the most from it. Thru-hikers who carry a canister stove and want the pot to hold both fuel and burner suit it well. Anyone cooking for three or more, or simmering real recipes, should choose a wider, heavier pot with better heat spread. Hikers who only ever pour boiling water into a bag can save money with a simpler titanium mug.

## Verdict

The Evernew Ti Pasta Pot is a well-made, featherweight cookpot that adds pasta-draining capability at almost no weight cost. It is pricey and fragile-feeling, but both complaints come with the thin titanium that makes it so light. For solo canister-stove cooks who like a hot dinner that isn't always freeze-dried, it earns a place in the pack.

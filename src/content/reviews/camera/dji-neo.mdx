## Overview

The DJI Neo is a 135 g quadcopter that launches from an open palm, tracks a hiker down the trail and lands back in the hand, with no controller required. It is the lightest and cheapest camera drone DJI has made, which makes it one of the few aerial cameras that can ride in a pack without bumping a base weight. The trade-off is a small sensor, an 18-minute battery and flight that gets nervous in wind, which is the normal condition on a ridge.

## Performance

**Weight and packability.** At 135 g with the guards fitted, the Neo weighs less than most phones, and DJI's own pitch is that it can go in a backpack or even a pocket. Each spare battery adds 45 g, so a two-spare kit adds about 90 g on top of the airframe. That is well under what a typical mirrorless camera and lens weigh, and the drone can film the hiker as well as the view. Its weight also keeps it under the threshold at which recreational pilots in the US generally don't need to register, a point PC Mag noted at launch.

**Flying it.** The signature feature is controller-free operation. Press the mode button, pick a shot, and the Neo lifts off the palm and flies the pattern on its own. Voice control through the DJI Fly app responds to a "Hey Fly" wake phrase, and the app on a phone adds manual flight. DJI rates each battery for at least 20 palm takeoffs and landings. Reviewers and owners consistently describe it as easy to fly and a good fit for beginners. Tom's Guide gave it an Editor's Choice. For more control, the Neo pairs with DJI's RC-N3, RC Motion and Goggles 3, but every accessory adds weight and cost, and it erodes the grab-and-go appeal.

**Image quality.** The 1/2-inch sensor produces 4K/30 footage that Tom's Guide called gorgeous in good conditions. Owners are more measured. Several buyers on retail listings describe the image as fine for a phone screen but short of what a larger screen demands, and adequate only with plenty of light. The Neo has no optical stabilization and a small sensor, so dusk and forest shade are weak spots. Stabilization is electronic (RockSteady or HorizonBalancing). With palm control or the app, only HorizonBalancing is available and it can't be switched off. That is a usable trail-vlog camera, not a replacement for a Mini-series drone.

**Wind and range.** Wind is the Neo's weak point. DJI rates it for Level 4 wind (8 m/s). Tom's Guide listed susceptibility to gusts as a con. A 135 g aircraft with 2-inch props is at the mercy of ridgeline and pass conditions, and the guards act partly like sails. Hikers get the best results in the calm hours around sunrise and in sheltered basins. The 1,000-metre-plus summits where thru-hikers want a shot are the places it is least happy.

**Battery and safety.** DJI quotes 18 minutes of hover, or 17 with the guards, measured in still air at near sea level. Real flights with tracking and wind will come in shorter. Tom's Guide flagged the short flight time and the lack of obstacle avoidance. The full prop cage makes bumping a branch survivable, though, which is the reason it works for tracking shots near trees. Takeoff is rated to 2,000 m, which covers most trail altitudes but not the high Sierra passes.

**Storage and charging.** The 22 GB of internal memory holds roughly 40 minutes of 4K/30 or 55 minutes of 1080p/60, so a microSD card isn't needed. Batteries charge over USB-C, and the optional three-battery hub supports USB PD fast charging. That suits a power bank on a resupply-to-resupply stretch, though the Neo's three-battery kit adds grams to the pack.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 135 g takeoff weight with full propeller guards, 45 g per battery
- Palm launch and landing with no controller or phone required
- 4K/30 video and 12 MP stills at $199
- Enclosed props tolerate bumps in trees and on rocks
- 22 GB of built-in storage

**Cons**

- Gusts upset it, and Level 4 wind resistance is a low ceiling for exposed terrain
- 18 minutes at best, shorter in practice
- No obstacle avoidance; downward sensing only
- Small sensor and no optical stabilization limit low-light footage
- Remote, goggles and spare batteries are extra

## Who Should Buy This

The Neo suits the weekend or section hiker who wants a few follow shots and a group photo from above, and who will carry 135 g to get them. It is also a good first drone for content creators who plan to film people rather than landscapes. Thru-hikers counting every gram will have to decide whether 180 g with a spare battery earns a place in the pack. Photographers who want clean files, long flights or reliable performance above treeline should look at a larger, sub-250 g drone with a bigger sensor.

## Verdict

The Neo is the lightest way to get a drone into the backcountry, and its palm-launch system makes it easy to use. It is held back by weak wind tolerance, brief flights and image quality that suits phones better than large screens. Recommended for casual trail filming and group shots, but not for serious aerial landscape work.

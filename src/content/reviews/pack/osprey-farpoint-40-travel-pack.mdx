## Performance

Let's address the elephant in the room first: 1,587 g (3 lb 8 oz) empty is a significant chunk of base weight for ultralight-minded travelers. By comparison, a frameless travel pack like the Tom Bihn Synik 30 comes in around 900 g. The Farpoint 40 earns most of that weight back in carrying comfort — but if grams are your religion, this isn't your pack.

**Suspension and Comfort**

Where the Farpoint 40 genuinely earns its reputation is on your back under load. <cite index="26-2">These packs adopt serious backpacking features from Osprey's more technical packs — like fine-tunable torso adjustments, load-lifting LightWire frames, and breathable harnesses and hipbelts — blended with practical travel functionality.</cite> The result is a pack that handles heavy packing lists far more gracefully than most travel bags.

<cite index="26-17">
  The 4 mm LightWire peripheral frame transfers load from the harness to the
  hipbelt, while an Atilon framesheet spreads it across the entire backpanel,
  and the mesh-covered ridged foam AirScape backpanel allows maximum ventilation
  while keeping the load close to the body.
</cite>
<cite index="10-39,10-40">
  The harness system does a superb job of dispersing weight and keeping you cool
  — you'll still sweat on a hot day, but less than if they hadn't included all
  the extra aeration and air channels.
</cite>

One genuinely clever feature: <cite index="10-18">the harness, straps, and hip belt can all be zipped away at the back.</cite> <cite index="25-19">Stowable straps effectively prevent snags and essentially transform the pack into a carry-on-size duffel,</cite> which matters when you're hauling it through an overhead bin or handing it to a bus driver.

<cite index="10-26">
  The sternum strap is on a rail, so you can make micro-adjustments, and it
  stays in place once you've got it dialed in.
</cite>
Shorter torsos should consider trying the S/M fit or the Fairview women's
version —
<cite index="10-24,10-25">
  those with shorter torsos may find the Osprey Fairview 40 works better, as
  it's designed with a smaller frame in mind.
</cite>

**Organization and Access**

<cite index="9-18,9-19">
  Unzipping the Farpoint 40 reveals a huge clamshell-style main compartment
  lined in bright neon green, which favors travelers who pack by stuffing rather
  than organizing — it has very little built-in structure by design.
</cite>
<cite index="9-20,9-21">
  Internal compression straps help cinch down clothing piles, and a mesh divider
  keeps clothing contained while making packed items easy to identify. A large
  mesh zip pocket on the flap gives you a place to stash extra gear like
  chargers or socks.
</cite>

<cite index="18-9">
  A quick-access zippered laptop/tablet sleeve protects devices up to 16", and
  an external toiletry pocket helps you breeze through security lines.
</cite>
The toiletry pocket is a small but legitimately useful travel-specific detail —
being able to pull it out for TSA without digging through your main compartment
saves real time.

<cite index="3-8">
  Despite the Farpoint's ability to carry plenty of heavy gear, it lacks many
  external pockets that are handy for accessing smaller travel essentials while
  on the go.
</cite>
<cite index="3-18">
  There's also a notable lack of a side water bottle pocket,
</cite>
which is a real omission on a travel pack. The front shove-it pocket can hold a
bottle in a pinch, but it's a workaround, not a solution.

**Durability and Materials**

<cite index="21-28,21-29">
  The current model is crafted from 450D recycled twist dobby polyester with a
  PFAS-free DWR coating — free of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances,
</cite>
which is worth noting if you care about environmental impact.
<cite index="1-24">
  The primary pack material holds up well — no issues with puncturing, fraying,
  or allowing water to soak gear.
</cite>
<cite index="22-36,22-37">
  The zippers are high-quality YKK, and the main compartment zipper is beefy but
  smooth.
</cite>
<cite index="22-38">
  All main zippers have interlocking loops so they can be locked with a luggage
  lock.
</cite>

**Carry-On Compliance**

<cite index="5-17,5-18,5-19">
  The Farpoint 40 fits into the overhead compartment and under the seat in front
  of you, and has held up without gate-check issues across a range of aircraft
  sizes — from small regional turboprops to full Airbuses.
</cite>
That said, always stow your harness before boarding: a dangling hip belt is the
fastest way to attract gate agent attention.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Hiking-grade suspension makes heavy loads far more comfortable than most travel packs
- Stowaway harness and hip belt is a genuinely smart design for multi-modal travel
- Durable 450D recycled polyester with PFAS-free DWR and bluesign certification
- Clamshell opening and neon-lined interior make packing and finding gear easy
- Lockable zippers add peace of mind in hostel and transit environments
- Adjustable torso fit accommodates a wide range of body sizes
- $185 MSRP is competitive for this feature set

**Cons**

- 1,587 g empty is heavy — the suspension system costs you real pack weight
- Internal organization is spartan; packing cubes are essentially mandatory
- No dedicated side water bottle pocket
- Outdoorsy aesthetic isn't for everyone — looks like a hiking pack, not an urban carry
- Hip belt is not removable, which can feel bulky if you rarely use it
- <cite index="5-20">
    Mesh pockets become difficult to use if the pack is fully loaded
  </cite>

## Who Should Buy This

<cite index="4-12,4-13">
  If you're a backpacker or independent traveler, this pack will suit your needs
  — unlike purely urban-style packs, the Farpoint 40 is comfortable to carry for
  extended periods, which helps when navigating places on foot or using public
  transport.
</cite>
<cite index="4-14,4-15,4-16,4-17">
  It's especially well-suited for certain trips — practically perfect for
  backpacking Southeast Asia, for example, where it's just the right size for a
  tropical kit and easy to throw onto a tuk-tuk or haul aboard a long-tail
  ferry.
</cite>
It's less suited to someone who wants a sleek, low-profile urban pack or who's
disciplined enough to travel with 12 lbs and doesn't need the suspension system
earning its keep.

## Verdict

The Farpoint 40 has stayed popular for years because it solves a real problem: how do you carry 20–30 lbs through airports, city streets, and the occasional dirt road without destroying your back? <cite index="4-31,4-32">Despite basic internal organization, it remains the king among budget-friendly, high-quality adventure packs — very few travel backpacks can touch its overall level of carrying comfort, and none at this price.</cite> If you're a comfort-first one-bag traveler and you're willing to pay the weight tax for a genuine suspension system, the Farpoint 40 earns its place. Ultralight purists will find better options, but for everyone else, this is a hard pack to argue with at $185.

## Overview

The Pinnacle Soloist is GSI Outdoors' one-person kitchen in a box: a 1.1L non-stick pot, a strainer lid, an insulated 14 fl. oz. mug/bowl, a telescoping foon and a welded stuff sack that moonlights as a wash basin. It is built for solo backpackers who want a complete, tidy cook-and-eat system that swallows a stove and a 220 g canister. The trade-off is weight: at 309 g, it is a convenience kit first and an ultralight kit second. GSI has since replaced it with the Pinnacle Soloist II, so remaining stock and used sets are the main way to find the original.

## Performance

**Packing.** The set's best trick is how it collapses. The pot's folding handle locks out for cooking and locks over the lid for transport, so the whole kit can ride without the stuff sack for anyone shaving grams. Inside the pot, there is room for a compact canister stove and a 220 g canister. The finished bundle is about the size of a grapefruit, in GSI's own description. One owner on Paddling.com pairs it with a 3.9 oz piezo canister stove that sits inside the bowl, and calls that "all I need other than fuel." On Trailspace, a long-term owner reports the pot fits neatly over an MSR PocketRocket.

**Cooking.** The pot is hard-anodized aluminum with a Teflon Radiance non-stick coating. GSI claims the coating reaches temperature 25 percent faster than other coatings. In practice, a 1.1L pot is a boil-and-soak vessel: dehydrated dinners, ramen, oatmeal, a pasta portion for one. A Trailspace reviewer notes it is sized generously enough for two people's hot drinks and light meals. The non-stick surface cleans up easily, and the welded sack lets campers wash up without a second container. GSI advises keeping the handle out of direct flame, which is worth remembering with wide-burner stoves.

**Durability.** Owner reports are consistently good. A Trailspace reviewer who used the set for roughly five years described only the tiniest dings and no degradation of the pot's surface, and another owner who had a problem got a replacement from GSI at no cost. The nylon lid and silicone ring are designed to resist crushing in a pack, and the lid has a sealing ring that helps hold heat. Owners also like the integrated strainer, which doubles as a bug-barrier on a bowl set down in camp.

**Eating and drinking.** The mug/bowl hybrid has a low center of gravity and an insulating sleeve, so coffee stays warm and the bowl is harder to tip than a tall mug. The sip-through lid cuts down on spills. The foon (spoon and fork in one) telescopes to reach the bottom of a freeze-dried pouch. It is a single-person ergonomic setup, and one owner on Paddling.com called the sporks durable and collapsible.

**Weight.** At 309 g for the complete kit, the Soloist asks for more than a bare titanium pot and a long-handled spoon. What it returns is a mug, a strainer, a wash basin and a bag that organizes everything. Hikers who would otherwise carry a separate cup, utensil and stove sack will recoup some of that difference. Stripped-down thru-hikers who cold-soak or boil water in a single mug will not.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Complete system: pot, lid, insulated mug/bowl, foon and sink in 309 g
- Stove and 220 g canister nest inside, so the set packs to a compact 5.0 x 5.4 x 5.5 in
- Locking folding handle lets the set travel without the stuff sack
- Strainer lid and insulated mug/bowl are well thought out
- Owners report years of use with little wear on the pot coating

**Cons**

- Heavier than a minimalist titanium pot-and-spoon setup
- 1.1L is tight for anything beyond a one-person meal
- Non-stick coating needs care compared with bare titanium or uncoated aluminum
- Handle should not be exposed to direct flame
- Original model is discontinued, replaced by the Soloist II

## Who Should Buy This

Solo backpackers who cook hot meals every night, mix in coffee or tea, and like a kit that stays organized in the pack are the target. Weekend hikers and section hikers moving up from a mismatched pot and camp cup will see the biggest upgrade. Anyone cooking for two, frying food, or counting every gram should look elsewhere: a titanium pot with a separate spoon is lighter, and a larger pot suits couples better.

## Verdict

The Pinnacle Soloist is a smart, durable one-person kitchen that nests a stove and canister without fuss and holds up for years. It gives up some weight for convenience, which is a fair deal for most solo backpackers who actually cook. Buy it for neatness and function, not for the lowest base weight.

## Overview

The X-Pot is Sea to Summit's answer to the "why does my pot take up a third of my pack" problem — a cook pot with collapsible silicone walls and a hard-anodized aluminum base that folds flat to about an inch tall when you're done cooking. It's aimed at backpackers and campers who care more about packed volume than shaving the last gram off pot weight, and it comes in three sizes (1.4L, 2.8L, 4.0L) to scale from solo trips to small groups.

## Performance

The core pitch of the X-Pot is packed volume, and it delivers. <cite index="14-30">It compresses to a disk 8 x 1 inch (20.3 x 2.54 cm)</cite>, which is a real space-saver compared to a rigid pot of the same capacity — you can stuff a bag of coffee or your stove canister inside it before it even goes in your pack. The stainless ring in the rim does what it's supposed to: <cite index="15-1,15-5">the hard anodized aluminum base offers a fast boil time and the stainless steel ring embedded in the rim lends a rigidity to the silicone walls for easy lifting and stirring</cite>. In practice that means the pot doesn't feel floppy or unstable even when full — a legitimate concern with a soft-sided vessel.

Boil times are solid thanks to the anodized aluminum base, but heat distribution is the trade-off you're signing up for. Outdoor Gear Lab's testing on the related X Set found that <cite index="12-2,12-3">cooking performance is good, but you have to tend carefully to the food, or it will burn, since due to the thin metal, water boils quickly, but heat is not distributed evenly</cite>. That tracks with what you'd expect from a small aluminum disc doing all the work — great for boiling water for dehydrated meals or coffee, less great for simmering rice or anything that needs even heat without babysitting.

Stability on a stove is another real-world quirk worth flagging. Because the sidewalls are silicone rather than rigid metal, there's no handle to steady the pot while it's balanced on a small canister stove. As one reviewer testing the pot on a Pocket Rocket-style burner put it, <cite index="12-21,12-22">the large pot balances on the stove, and you need to ensure the ground was flat before placing it</cite>. On uneven terrain that's a legitimate annoyance — you'll want a stable, flat spot or a stove with a wider pot support.

The lid's built-in strainer is a nice touch for draining pasta water without fishing for a separate strainer, and <cite index="17-13">the translucent pot lid with strainer shortens boil time since there's no need to lift the lid to check the boil</cite>. Just don't use the silicone handles as your grip during actual cooking — <cite index="15-17,15-18">the silicone handles are designed to secure the lid to the pot only during transport, and attaching them to the lid during cooking will cause damage to the lid</cite>.

One thing to know before you buy: advertised weights have been inconsistent across sizes and sources. One Trailspace reviewer of the 2.8L found <cite index="11-10">the advertised weight is 10.2 oz but it was actually 11.8 oz on my scale</cite>, so don't be surprised if your pot runs a bit heavier than the spec sheet says. It's not a dealbreaker, but if you're counting grams for a thru-hike shakedown, weigh it yourself rather than trusting the listed number.

Durability seems to hold up reasonably well with normal care — reviewers testing the silicone walls over a week of use figured <cite index="16-2">as long as they are taken care of, they should hold up for a while</cite> — but this isn't a pot you want to drag over rocks or expose to open flame, since the silicone sidewalls aren't rated for direct heat.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros:**

- Collapses to roughly an inch tall — genuinely useful for pack organization and bear canister/bear bag stuffing
- Fast boil times thanks to the anodized aluminum base
- Stainless ring in the rim keeps the pot rigid enough to lift and stir with confidence
- Built-in strainer lid is genuinely handy for pasta and rehydrated meals
- Nests well with matching X-Bowls/X-Mugs if you're building out the full X-series set

**Cons:**

- Uneven heat distribution means food scorches easily if you're not stirring
- No permanent handle for stove stability — needs flat, level ground
- Not fire-safe; stove-only cooking
- Real-world weight has run heavier than advertised in at least one independent test
- Silicone sidewalls limit cooking techniques (no searing, no sautéing — grab the X-Pan for that)

## Who Should Buy This

The X-Pot makes the most sense for backpackers and car campers who prioritize packed size over raw weight savings — think small-group trips where volume in the pack matters more than shaving the last few grams, or gear closets where storage space is tight. If you're a solo ultralight hiker chasing the lightest possible boil-water setup, a titanium pot will beat it on weight and even heating, though it won't collapse. If you regularly cook actual meals that need simmering rather than just boiling water, plan on active stirring or pair it with the companion X-Pan.

## Verdict

The X-Pot delivers on its core promise — a full-size cooking pot that folds down to a fraction of its working height — without being a gimmick. It boils water quickly and handles rehydrated meals fine, but the trade-offs (uneven heat, no rigid handle, stove-only use) are real and worth knowing before you buy. I'd call it a 7/10: a smart pick for space-conscious backpackers and small groups, but not the pot I'd reach for if precise heat control or fire cooking matters to you.

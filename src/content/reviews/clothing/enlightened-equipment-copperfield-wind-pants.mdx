## Overview

The Copperfield Wind Pants are a shell for your legs and not much else: a tube of 10D nylon with a shock-cord waist and a few snaps at the ankle. They are built for backpackers and trail runners who hike in shorts and want something to throw on at a windy pass or a cold dinner stop. The trade-off is plain. They weigh about as much as a pair of AAA batteries and have the durability to match.

## Performance

**Weight and packability.** Enlightened Equipment lists the lightest configuration at 1.31 oz (37.1 g), and the pants are marketed as light enough to disappear into a pocket. Owners confirm the claim. A Trailspace reviewer who took a pair through the Sierras and onto a section of the North Country Trail brings them on every trip because they weigh under two ounces and compress to almost nothing. A pair of long-distance hikers who chose the 10D fabric for both of their pairs describes the same logic: even if the pants come out only a handful of times, the weight cost is negligible.

**Wind and warmth.** The Copperfield pants work as a windbreak first and an insulator a distant second. Reviewers describe the effect as a tiny bit of added warmth. That is enough to take the chill off bare legs after a day in shorts, or to hold heat in over a pair of thermal leggings on a cold night. CleverHiker's testing of the Copperfield wind shirt, which uses the same family of fabrics, found good wind resistance for the weight, with only slight penetration under a blow dryer. The same testers noted it falls behind heavier options in sustained high wind and cold. The pants should be expected to follow the same pattern: a fix for breezes, not for winter.

**Bugs.** One Trailspace reviewer reports that mosquitoes can't bite through the fabric. For hikers who spend evenings at Sierra lakes, that is a real second job for 40 grams of nylon.

**Durability.** This is where the weight comes from. The 10D fabric is thin, and the Trailspace reviewer's one listed downside is that the pants aren't suited to bushwhacking. Still, wear reports are better than the denier suggests. One owner who logged at least a hundred wears on a men's medium pair saw only a little visible strain at the crotch seam. Enlightened Equipment itself notes that some of the lighter 10D and 7D colors can be quite transparent, so color choice matters for modesty.

**Feel and fit.** The 10D fabric has a smooth, almost silky hand and doesn't cling much until it gets very wet. In wind, though, it molds to the leg, which is worth knowing before wearing it with nothing underneath. Sizes run about a half size large, and the cut is meant to fit directly over Enlightened Equipment's Torrid insulated pants. The ankle snaps tighten the cuff to keep drafts and brush out, and the cord-lock waist is the only other adjustment. There are no pockets, no zippers and, by common agreement, no style: one reviewer likened the charcoal pair to a garbage bag with legs.

**Weather limits.** These are wind pants, not rain pants. The DWR finish handles light moisture, but reviewers who carry them say they won't keep a hiker dry for long.

## Choosing a Version

The four listed sizes differ by only a few grams: 37 g in small, 40 g in medium, 42 g in large and 44 g in extra large. Weight should not drive the choice. Pick by waist and leg length, and keep in mind that the pants run about a half size large. Buyers between sizes can generally size down for a closer fit, or stay put for room to layer over insulated pants.

Fabric is the more consequential decision. The 10D nylon is the standard build and the one most thru-hikers choose. Lighter 7D saves weight, with one reviewer measuring a 7D pair at 1.35 oz against 2 oz for a 20D pair, but it is more translucent and more fragile. Hikers who expect brush should look at 20D. One owner had a pair made with 20D on the front of the leg for moisture-laden vegetation and 7D on the back for breathability.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- 37–44 g, small enough to live in a hip-belt pocket
- Cuts wind and adds a modest amount of warmth over shorts or leggings
- Keeps mosquitoes from biting through the legs, according to owners
- Ankle snaps and cord-lock waist allow useful adjustment
- Made in Minnesota, with fabric weight and fit customizable at order

**Cons**

- Thin 10D nylon is vulnerable to snags and brush
- No pockets
- Lighter colors can be see-through, and the fabric clings in wind
- Not rain pants, and not enough for sustained cold or high wind
- Priced around $75–80 for a garment that weighs 40 grams

## Who Should Buy This

Ultralight hikers and runners who wear shorts most of the day and want a camp and break layer for chilly evenings, high passes and buggy dusks. They also suit fastpackers who need a leg windbreak that vanishes in a pack. Hikers who already carry rain pants or full-length trousers will find them close to redundant. Anyone who works through thick brush, or wants pockets and a rugged fabric, should look at heavier 20D wind pants or standard hiking pants.

## Verdict

The Copperfield Wind Pants do one narrow job well and weigh almost nothing doing it. For shorts-wearing minimalists, they are a sensible just-in-case layer that costs about 1.4 ounces. Those who need durability or true storm protection should spend their weight elsewhere.

## Overview

Apple's USB-C Charge Cable is a plain, 1-meter (3.3 ft) USB-C to USB-C lead for charging and syncing iPads, MacBooks, iPhones, AirPods and other USB-C gear. It is the cable that ships in Apple boxes, sold separately. For hikers who carry Apple devices, it is a known quantity that works with every Apple charger. The trade-off is that it is a generalist: it is longer, heavier and pricier than what most ultralight kits need, and it moves data at USB 2 rates.

## Performance

**Charging.** The cable carries up to 60W, which covers everything a backpacker is likely to plug in. That includes an iPhone on its fast-charge profile, an iPad, AirPods, a Vision Pro battery pack and the smaller MacBook Airs. Apple's own guidance is to pair it with a compatible USB-C power adapter. Run it off a power bank with USB-C Power Delivery and it behaves the same way. The 60W ceiling matters only for laptops. A MacBook Pro that wants more than that will charge, but slowly under load.

**Data.** Apple specifies USB 2 transfer rates, so the cable is fine for syncing a phone or pulling a few photos. It is the wrong choice for moving camera footage or backing up a drive. Anyone who expects 10 Gb/s from a USB-C cable should buy a different one. This cable will not deliver it.

**Durability.** The woven jacket is the main upgrade over Apple's older glossy white cables, which were known for fraying at the connector. Owners of Apple's longer USB-C cables describe the cord as noticeably thicker and more flexible than the one that came with an iPad. That thickness is good news for a cable that lives crumpled in a pack lid pocket. Reviews of Apple's cables are not uniformly glowing, though, and some buyers report failures within months. At roughly $20, a dead cable on day nine of a section hike is an expensive lesson.

**Weight and bulk.** At about 27 g, the cable adds a small but real load. The jacket is stiff enough to coil neatly, but a full meter of it is more than most people need. On the trail, the phone sits in a hip belt pocket or lashed to a shoulder strap, and the power bank sits in the pack. That setup calls for about 30 cm of cable. The extra 70 cm is dead weight, and it is a tangle risk in a quilt at night.

**Compatibility.** There are no gimmicks and no surprises. It works with Apple chargers, third-party PD bricks and Android devices, and it is the safest bet for Apple-first gear lists where the charger brick, the cable and the device should all agree on what they are doing.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Reliable 60W charging for phones, tablets, earbuds and thin-and-light laptops
- Woven jacket resists the fraying that plagued Apple's older cables
- Works with Apple chargers and any USB-C PD source
- Sold through Apple and major retailers, so a replacement is easy to find
- Short enough to stay out of the way, long enough to reach a bedside power bank

**Cons**

- USB 2 data speeds only
- About 27 g is heavy for a cable that could be a third of the length
- One meter is more than a power-bank-in-pocket setup needs
- Costs about $20, several times the price of shorter third-party cables
- 60W cap limits fast charging on larger laptops

## Who Should Buy This

This cable suits hikers who carry an iPhone, iPad or Apple earbuds and want a trusted lead for a power bank. It also fits anyone who charges from a wall outlet in town and wants one cable that can do both jobs. It is a sensible spare for a resupply box or a car kit.

Gram-counters should look elsewhere. A 15 to 30 cm USB-C cable weighs a fraction as much and does the same job between a bank and a phone. Anyone who needs fast file transfers or 100W laptop charging should buy a cable rated for USB 3.x or 240W instead.

## Verdict

Apple's USB-C Charge Cable is a solid, uncomplicated charger. It is not a thoughtful piece of backpacking gear. Buy it if the gear list is Apple-heavy and brand trust is worth the premium. Otherwise, a shorter cable will save weight and money.

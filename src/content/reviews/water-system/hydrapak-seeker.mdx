## Overview

The HydraPak Seeker is a collapsible TPU water bag for the unglamorous jobs: dirty bag for a gravity filter, camp water supply, dry-stretch reserve. It comes in 2 L, 3 L and 4 L, all with a 42 mm mouth, a coated pour handle and two lash points. The trade-off is that it is a storage tool rather than a drinking vessel, and new bags can carry a rubbery aftertaste.

## Performance

**Durability** is the Seeker's reputation. Reviewers who have carried it through multiple seasons of alpine climbing in the Sierra and Cascades, multi-pitch routes at Red Rock and desert hikes in Joshua Tree report no seam failures or tears. The RF-welded TPU construction gets the credit. Testers who have run it alongside the Platypus Platy say the Seeker outlasted a bag that tends to fail with age. When one owner found a pinhole near the collar, HydraPak replaced the bag without argument and shipped within days.

**Gravity filtration** is where the Seeker earns its pack space. The 42 mm mouth accepts HydraPak's own filter head and the Katadyn BeFree directly, and the two lash points let the bag hang from a bear line or a branch. Owners describe a simple routine: fill at the source, thread the filter on, hang, and let it drip while camp chores get done. A bigger bag batch-filters enough for dinner, dishes and morning bottles in one go.

Sawyer Squeeze and Platypus QuickDraw users hit a snag. Their 28 mm threads don't screw onto the Seeker's cap. HydraPak's Plug-N-Play cap with a 28 mm filter adapter bridges the gap, but it is an extra purchase. Anyone built around a Sawyer should consider whether a CNOC VectoX is the better fit.

**Filling** is easy. The wide mouth scoops from shallow pools without the contortions a narrow-necked bag demands. The tethered cap doubles as a loop to hold the bag upright at the water's edge, and the coated handle makes one-handed carrying and pouring straightforward while keeping cold hands out of the stream.

**Drinking** is the weak spot. A floppy bag and a wide opening add up to spills. Testers describe needing two hands and a slow tip, and more than one admits to wearing the results. Paired with a Plug-N-Play cap and hose, the bag can work as a reservoir, but it is happiest never touching a mouth.

**Taste** is the other recurring complaint. New bags carry a rubbery flavor that fades over the first few uses, though some owners say it never fully disappears. Baking-soda soaks and thorough air drying help. The Platy, by comparison, arrives virtually taste-free. The fabric handle connecting the cap to the bag can also develop a musty smell if stored damp.

**Packability** is a strong suit. Empty, the bag collapses to roughly the size of a fist and stows in the included storage bag. Because the TPU tolerates freezing and 60°C fills, it can serve as an ice pack or a makeshift hot-water bottle on a cold night.

## Choosing a Version

All three sizes share the same cap, handle, lash points and filter compatibility. What changes is capacity, shape and weight per liter.

**2 L (76 g).** At 320 × 130 mm, the slim profile slips into a side pocket or beside a frame sheet. Two liters covers bottle top-offs, a night's cooking, or a small dirty bag for a solo hiker with a BeFree. It works out to 38 g per liter. A hiker who filters at the source and drinks from bottles rarely needs more.

**3 L (92 g).** The middle size is the most weight-efficient of the three at roughly 31 g per liter, and it is only 16 g heavier than the 2 L. At 370 × 140 mm it is longer and still slender, enough for bottle refills, cooking and washing up for one or a pair of hikers. For most solo and two-person trips with a 42 mm filter, this is the size that fits the job without carrying surplus bag.

**4 L (147 g).** This is the camp bag. It costs 55 g more than the 3 L and 71 g more than the 2 L, at about 37 g per liter. In use it measures 310 × 220 mm, a squatter, wider shape than the other two. That volume suits batch-filtering for a group, carrying water across a long dry section, or cutting the number of trips to the creek. Groups, desert routes and hikers who cook elaborate dinners should buy this one. Solo ultralighters on water-rich routes will find it more than they need.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Multi-season durability, with no seam failures reported by long-term users
- 42 mm opening fills fast from shallow water
- Two lash points make gravity-filter setups hands-free
- Tethered cap prevents loss and doubles as a carry loop
- Coated handle helps with pouring and one-handed carrying
- Three sizes; the 3 L is the lightest per liter
- Beyond Lifetime Guarantee with responsive replacements
- BPA and PVC free; handles freezing and hot water up to 60°C

**Cons**

- Floppy TPU and a wide mouth make direct drinking spill-prone
- Rubbery taste can linger beyond the first washes
- Sawyer Squeeze and Platypus QuickDraw need an adapter setup
- No hose or bite valve in the box
- Handle tether can smell musty if stored damp

## Who Should Buy This

Backpackers running a BeFree or another 42 mm gravity setup should buy the Seeker, as should anyone who wants a dirty bag that survives abuse. The 3 L suits most solo and pair trips, the 2 L suits hikers counting every gram of pack volume, and the 4 L belongs at group camps and on desert routes. Hikers who mostly want a bladder to sip from on the move, or who have committed to a Sawyer Squeeze, should look at the Platypus Platy or CNOC VectoX.

## Verdict

The Seeker is a tough, well-designed bag that does its job in camp and disappears in the pack. The persistent plastic taste, the awkward drinking and the filter thread limits keep it from being universal. For a BeFree-based system it is one of the better bags available, and the 3 L is the pick for most hikers.

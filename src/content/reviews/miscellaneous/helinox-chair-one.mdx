## Overview

The Chair One is the camp chair that taught a generation of backpackers to bring furniture. It borrows tent-pole technology: a shock-corded DAC aluminum frame snaps into a mesh-and-polyester sling and folds down to roughly the size of a large water bottle. It is a stable, durable seat that carries 320 pounds. The trade-off is that it weighs nearly a pound more than Helinox's own Chair Zero, and its sling seat tires out some sitters after half an hour.

## Performance

**Packability.** Outdoor Gear Lab's testers rated the Chair One the most packable camping chair they had tested. Folded, it barely exceeds the dimensions of a 32-ounce water bottle. The zippered case is a practical touch. It has a daisy chain for lashing to a pack or a raft frame, plus a handle on each side. That is easier to live with than a cinch sack when the chair gets stuffed in a damp pack.

**Setup.** One internal shock cord pulls the poles together. Slide the frame into the seat's corner pockets, and the chair is built in under a minute. Owners comparing it with cheaper folding chairs usually mention this simplicity first. There are no loose parts and no tools.

**Comfort.** This is where opinions split. The Chair One is more comfortable than the Chair Zero by most direct comparisons. A head-to-head test measured a seat height of about 9.6 inches, about 2 inches higher than the Zero. The test also found a more open seat angle, a seat that holds its shape rather than sagging, and a much larger share of breathable mesh. Those differences mean straighter legs and less hammock-like slumping.

Even so, the chair is a sling. Outdoor Gear Lab's testers found that bigger sitters struggled to fit into the 20-inch-wide seat. They also would not recommend sitting in it for more than about 30 minutes at a stretch, because the seat encourages slouching. A reclined, low seat suits an evening of stove-side dinner and a podcast. It is a poor choice for a long card game. Anyone with stiff knees or a bad back will notice the low seat height when standing up.

**Durability and stability.** The anodized aluminum frame and 600D polyester seat are tougher than the chair's weight suggests. The nylon resin hubs and reinforced corners are built for repeated assembly. Helinox backs the chair with a five-year warranty. Outdoor Gear Lab did flag a frame that feels less sturdy than that of heavier camp chairs, a fair criticism of any tent-pole design. The rubber feet grip well on slick surfaces, but they sink in soft sand and mud, which is why Helinox sells separate ball-foot and groundsheet accessories.

**Weight versus the alternatives.** At 893 g, the Chair One is a real luxury item for a thru-hiker. Helinox's own pitch is plain: the Chair One suits everyday camping, while the 509 g Chair Zero is the backpacker's choice. The Zero gives up 56 lb of capacity, sits lower, and costs more. Helinox has also introduced the Chair One (re), a taller-seat successor with a 300D recycled fabric. At 1,020 g it is heavier than the original. The original 600D Chair One remains the lighter of the two.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Packs to about 14 x 4.5 x 4.5 in, with a zippered case and daisy chain
- 320 lb capacity, well above the Chair Zero's 265 lb
- Seat sits about 2 inches higher than the Zero and holds its shape
- Fast one-cord setup, with a five-year warranty
- Recycled 600D fabric and mesh panels breathe better than the Zero's seat

**Cons**

- 893 g is a pound heavier than the Chair Zero, and a lot for a long-distance carry
- The sling seat encourages slouching; testers limited sessions to around 30 minutes
- A narrow 20-inch seat is tight for larger sitters
- The feet sink in sand and soft ground without add-on accessories
- The frame feels less rigid than a conventional folding chair

## Who Should Buy This

The Chair One suits weekend backpackers, bikepackers, paddlers, and car campers who care about pack space more than a few ounces. It also suits festival-goers, sideline parents, and anyone who wants a seat that disappears into a duffel. People who need a sturdier seat should favor it over the Zero, particularly sitters over 200 pounds.

Thru-hikers counting every ounce should look at the Chair Zero or skip the chair entirely. Those who plan long evenings in camp, or who need a firm seat to stand up from, are better served by a heavier, taller car-camping chair.

## Verdict

The Chair One remains the sensible middle path: it carries more than the Zero, sits more comfortably, and costs a pound less on the shelf than the lightest competitors cost in dollars. It is not the lightest, and it is not the most supportive. Buy it for weekend trips and shared gear. Leave it home when the goal is a 25-mile day.

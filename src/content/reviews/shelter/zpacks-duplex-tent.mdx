## Overview

The Duplex Lite is Zpacks' narrowed, floor-shaved version of its long-running Duplex: a single-wall, two-door, two-vestibule trekking-pole tent made from PFAS-free Dyneema Composite Fabric. Zpacks calls it the lightest fully enclosed two-person tent in the world, and at 423 g (14.9 oz) the claim is hard to argue with. The catch is a 40-inch floor that suits one hiker and their gear far better than it suits two.

## Performance

**Weight and packed size.** The 423 g figure covers the tent with guylines and sewn-in line locks. Zpacks includes repair tape and two spare zipper sliders, and the stuff sack adds about 8 g. iRunFar's testers weighed their sample at 450 g (15.9 oz) with the stuff sack, which is still under a pound for a shelter with two doors and two vestibules. Rolled up, it measures about 4.5 by 11 inches. DCF never compresses like silnylon, but testers found it fits in the rear mesh pocket of many fastpacking packs.

**Where the grams went.** Compared with the standard Duplex, the Lite uses a thinner floor, 1.3 mm guylines in place of 2 mm, a floor 5 cm narrower, and a slightly lower bathtub wall. It also skips interior pockets. Zpacks puts the floor saving at about 25 percent. GearJunkie measured the result at roughly 3 oz lighter than the original.

**Livability.** The 40-inch floor fits one person with gear, or two people on standard 20-inch pads side by side. Owners are blunt that this is a solo tent first. One long-distance user who moved from the 45-inch Duplex to the Lite found the 5-inch difference mattered more than expected: the pack no longer lies flat beside the sleeper and ends up tilted on its side or resting against the legs. GearJunkie's tester flagged taller hikers in particular, with anyone over 6 feet likely to feel the length and width. Hikers under that height described the interior as roomy for the weight, and one owner called it a palace for a single person.

**Weather.** The Duplex layout has a long record on the AT, PCT and CDT. Hikers report the standard model riding out hard wind, heavy rain, hail and snow. iRunFar's testers called the Lite stormworthy in heavy rain. The four overlapping storm doors can be opened or closed independently. They are held with toggles and hooks, and iRunFar noted that this system is less secure than a zipper. It lets in some wind and makes noise when the fabric flaps. Ventilation is good for a single-wall shelter, with mesh panels and overlapping doors that allow airflow without extra vents. Condensation is still part of life with single-wall DCF. One AT thru-hiker said it is hard to keep the walls clear of the body on the sloped, uneven sites common on that trail.

**Setup.** Pitching takes a few repetitions to get right. Stake the corners, raise the two poles, then tighten the guylines. The bright yellow lines help in the dark. One owner found the thin guylines too slim to hold reliably in the supplied line locks.

**Durability.** Zpacks says the Lite does not need a footprint. SectionHiker advises adding an ultralight one for abrasive ground, since the lighter floor trades some durability for weight. Field reports are more relaxed. A thru-hiker who covered the PCT and CDT found the thinner floor fine, and GearJunkie judged the tent durable enough for a full thru-hike. The usual DCF caveat applies: slow pinhole leaks can develop over time and are fiddly to locate and patch.

## Choosing a Version

The two listings differ by 85 g, about 3 oz. The 423 g figure is Zpacks' headline weight for the standard build with the 0.55 oz/sq yd canopy. Zpacks notes that canopy material choice changes the total weight. Both versions share the same 40 x 90 in floor, 48 in peak height and two-door, two-vestibule layout.

The 423 g version suits hikers who want the lowest base weight and are comfortable with a narrow floor. The 508 g version costs the weight advantage that justifies the Lite's compromises. At that mass it matches the 507 g Classic Duplex, which gives a 45-inch floor. Hikers who would carry 508 g should weigh the wider Classic first, especially if they share the tent or are tall.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Among the lightest fully enclosed two-door, two-vestibule shelters available
- Packs to about 2.9 L
- Proven Duplex geometry with strong wind and rain records
- Taped seams, so no sealing before the first trip
- Each door can be opened or closed independently
- Small corner-to-corner footprint fits tight sites

**Cons**

- 40-inch floor is cramped for two adults, and pack space suffers even for solo hikers
- Thinner floor invites a footprint on abrasive ground
- Toggle-and-hook doors leak some wind and rattle
- 1.3 mm guylines grip poorly in the stock line locks
- Single-wall DCF produces condensation on damp nights
- Not compatible with the Flex Kit, so trekking poles are required
- High price

## Who Should Buy This

The Duplex Lite is for solo thru-hikers and fastpackers who already carry trekking poles, want two vestibules and full bug protection, and will pay a premium to cut grams. Pairs who are close friends and sleep on standard-width pads can make it work. Taller hikers, couples who want real elbow room, and anyone camping often on rough, gritty ground should look at the Classic Duplex.

## Verdict

At 423 g, the Duplex Lite gives hikers a storm-tested, fully enclosed shelter that weighs less than many bivy-and-tarp setups. Treat it as a roomy one-person tent and add a footprint where the ground is rough. It earns a firm recommendation for solo ultralighters and a pass for couples.

## Overview

The R1 Air Full-Zip Hoody is Patagonia's answer to a familiar problem: fleece that keeps you warm at the trailhead and turns into a sauna on the first climb. Its hollow-core yarn is knitted into a zigzag grid, trapping heat in the raised ridges and venting it through the gaps between them. The trade-off is a fabric that has no wind resistance of its own and a price of $199 that buys comfort and breathability, not bargain-bin durability.

## Performance

**Warmth and breathability.** The R1 Air's reputation rests on one trick: it runs warm without cooking. Treeline Review's testers called it one of the most breathable fleeces available while still insulating better than most, and found that over a base layer it stayed comfortable in temperatures near 60°F. Paired with a shell, the same testers found it warm enough for sub-freezing activity. Veteran backpacker Chris Townsend, who wore it through autumn and winter day walks and camping trips in the Cairngorms, found it very warm for its weight and more breathable than other fleeces he had tried.

It is not a furnace, though. Better Trail rated its warmth at 3.5 out of 5, noting it is not the warmest or lightest fleece in its test group, and Outdoor Gear Lab's testers found it could run hot on high-heart-rate efforts such as steep climbs. Women's-version testers added that wind cuts easily through the thin sections of the grid. On an exposed ridge, that means a shell goes on over it. Townsend notes the same thing: it isn't windproof, but it was built as a midlayer and worked under waterproof, windproof and insulated jackets alike.

**Comfort and mobility.** This is where reviewers gush. Outdoor Gear Lab scored it near the top of its fleece field for comfort, and Better Trail's testers put it among the most comfortable they tried, with notable stretch given that the fabric has no elastane. Flat seams keep the shoulders and underarms free of abrasion, which matters under a pack.

**Hood and fit.** The balaclava-style hood is snug enough to sit under a helmet or a shell hood and covers the neck and lower face. It has no drawcord and no adjustment. Wearers who want to cinch it down in a breeze won't get the option, and neither will they get any at the hem or cuffs, which rely on stretch binding. The cut is listed as slim, and Townsend found it close over a thin base layer. Better Trail's testers felt the newest version is looser and roomier near the hem than earlier ones.

**Pockets.** An earlier complaint, a chest pocket too small for a phone, has been addressed in the current version, according to Outdoor Gear Lab. Hip pockets still sit low enough to collide with a pack hipbelt, so the chest pocket does the real work on trail.

**Durability.** The weak spot. Outdoor Gear Lab lists quick pilling among its cons, and a critical REI buyer reported heavy pilling after only a couple of months of light wear. Better Trail adds that the fuzzy grid loses softness over time. Satisfaction is otherwise high: REI's men's listing averages 4.8 out of 5 across 38 reviews, with owners describing it as an everyday layer for hiking, snowboarding and working at home.

## Pros & Cons

**Pros**

- Breathability that outclasses most fleeces, with real warmth for a 354 g layer
- Stretchy, soft and free of irritating seams
- Close hood works under helmets and shell hoods
- Phone-sized chest pocket in the current version
- Recycled polyester, Fair Trade Certified sewn
- Looks good enough for town as well as trail

**Cons**

- Not wind resistant; needs a shell in exposed conditions
- Prone to pilling, and the surface softness fades
- No adjustment at hood or hem
- Can overheat during sustained hard climbing
- $199 is steep for a fleece

## Who Should Buy This

Anyone who hikes, ski tours or climbs in cold, dry-to-damp conditions and wants one midlayer that handles stop-and-go effort: the start-of-day chill, the sweaty switchback, the windy lunch break. It suits three-season backpackers and shoulder-season thru-hikers who already carry a shell and want a layer that doubles as camp and town wear. Those who need weather protection from the fabric itself should look at a wind-resistant hybrid fleece or an active insulation jacket instead. So should weight-obsessed hikers, who can find lighter options, and anyone hard on gear who would resent a layer that pills.

## Verdict

The R1 Air Hoody is the standard-setter for breathable, warm fleece, and its ratio of warmth to weight to comfort is hard to beat at 354 g. Its faults are cosmetic wear and a lack of wind protection, neither of which undermines its job as a midlayer under a shell. Buy it for active cold-weather use; wait for a sale if $199 stings.
